Can AI companies train on your work? The ANI v. OpenAI ruling

The Delhi High Court refused an interim injunction against ChatGPT, finding training prima facie fair dealing. What it decided and what is still open.

Generative AI tools are built by “training” models on vast amounts of text, images and other material, much of it protected by copyright. In ANI Media Pvt. Ltd. v. OpenAI OpCo LLC, the news agency ANI argued that OpenAI copied its reports to train ChatGPT and reproduced them in answers.

  • The interim ruling. On 24 July 2026 Justice Amit Bansal of the Delhi High Court refused ANI’s plea for an interim injunction. At this preliminary stage the Court held that using ANI’s content for training fell within the fair-dealing exception for private use, including research, in Section 52(1)(a) of the Copyright Act, 1957.
  • Jurisdiction. The Court held that it could hear the suit even though OpenAI said its servers and training were outside India.
  • Why no injunction. The Court weighed the harm an injunction would cause to AI development in India and to millions of users, and noted that ANI had not shown lost revenue or an existing licensing market for its content.
  • Not the final word. This is a prima facie view; the suit continues to trial. Separately, a committee set up by the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade published a working paper in December 2025 that canvasses a licensing model under which AI developers would pay for training data.
  • What creators can do now. Copyright still protects your expression, and output that reproduces a substantial part of your work may still infringe. Website owners can also block AI crawlers through technical settings.

For now, an Indian court has treated AI training as closer to research than to copying, but the law and policy are still moving.
