Nature and formation of contract/E-contract

A contract is an agreement enforceable by law: offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, free consent and lawful object (s. 10). Acceptance must be communicated; for instantaneous communication the contract is made where acceptance is received. E-contracts are valid (IT Act s. 10A). Privity means only parties can sue, but India allows consideration from a third party and recognises exceptions such as trusts, family arrangements, acknowledgment and agency.

Key provisions

  • Indian Contract Act, 1872: ss. 2, 3–10, 25
  • IT Act, 2000, ss. 10A, 11–13
  • BSA, 2023, ss. 61–63

Leading cases

  • Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. (1893): General offer accepted by performance.
  • Lalman Shukla v. Gauri Dutt (1913): Acceptance needs knowledge of offer.
  • Bhagwandas Goverdhandas Kedia v. Girdharilal Parshottamdas (1966): Telephonic contract made where acceptance heard.
  • Balfour v. Balfour (1919): No intention to create legal relations in domestic arrangements.
  • Trimex International FZE v. Vedanta Aluminium (2010): Contract concluded by email.
  • Chinnaya v. Ramayya (1882): Consideration may move from a third party.
  • Tweddle v. Atkinson (1861): Privity rule.
  • Khwaja Muhammad Khan v. Husaini Begum (1910, PC): Beneficiary of a marriage settlement may sue.

Factors vitiating free consent

Consent is free unless caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake (ss. 14–22). For undue influence the plaintiff must show that the other party could dominate their will and used it to obtain an unfair advantage. It is presumed in relations of trust or real authority, and with pardanashin women and unconscionable bargains, after which the burden shifts (s. 16(3)).

Key provisions

  • ss. 14–23, 64, 65
  • Evidence: BSA s. 123 (fiduciary relations)

Leading cases

  • Chikkam Ammiraju v. Chikkam Seshamma (1917): Threat of suicide is coercion.
  • Raghunath Prasad v. Sarju Prasad (1923, PC): Three-stage test for undue influence.
  • Subhas Chandra Das Mushib v. Ganga Prasad Das Mushib (1967): What plaintiff must prove; presumption.
  • Ladli Prasad Jaiswal v. Karnal Distillery (1963): Undue influence pleaded with particulars.
  • Derry v. Peek (1889): Fraud requires dishonesty.
  • Cundy v. Lindsay (1878): Mistake as to identity.
  • Raffles v. Wichelhaus (1864): Mutual mistake: no consensus.

Void, voidable, illegal and unenforceable agreements

Void agreements include those with minors, with unlawful object or consideration, without consideration (save s. 25 exceptions), in restraint of marriage, trade or legal proceedings, uncertain and wagering agreements. Voidable contracts can be avoided by the party whose consent was not free. Illegal agreements are void and taint collateral transactions.

Key provisions

  • ss. 11, 23–30, 65
  • Specific Relief Act, 1963, s. 33

Leading cases

  • Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose (1903, PC): Minor's agreement void ab initio.
  • Gherulal Parakh v. Mahadeodas Maiya (1959): Public policy narrowly applied; wagering not illegal.
  • Nordenfelt v. Maxim Nordenfelt (1894): Reasonable restraints (English law).
  • Niranjan Shankar Golikari v. Century Spinning (1967): Restraint during employment valid.
  • Superintendence Co. of India v. Krishan Murgai (1981): Post-employment restraint void.
  • Percept D'Mark v. Zaheer Khan (2006): Right-of-first-refusal clause void under s. 27.

Performance and discharge of contracts

Parties must perform or offer to perform (ss. 37–38). Time is of the essence only where intended (s. 55). Discharge occurs by performance, agreement (novation, rescission, alteration, remission: ss. 62–63), impossibility or frustration (s. 56), breach and operation of law.

Key provisions

  • ss. 37–67

Leading cases

  • Satyabrata Ghose v. Mugneeram Bangur (1954): Frustration under s. 56 is impossibility in a practical sense.
  • Taylor v. Caldwell (1863): Destruction of subject matter.
  • Krell v. Henry (1903): Frustration of purpose.
  • Naihati Jute Mills v. Khyaliram Jagannath (1968): Mere commercial hardship is not frustration.
  • Energy Watchdog v. CERC (2017): Price rise not force majeure.
  • Hind Construction v. State of Maharashtra (1979): Time not ordinarily of essence in building contracts.

Quasi-contracts

Quasi-contracts (ss. 68–72) impose obligations resembling contract to prevent unjust enrichment: necessaries supplied to incapable persons, payment by an interested person, non-gratuitous acts, finder of goods and money paid by mistake or coercion.

Key provisions

  • ss. 68–72

Leading cases

  • Moses v. Macferlan (1760): Unjust enrichment.
  • State of West Bengal v. B.K. Mondal & Sons (1962): Non-gratuitous act enjoyed by State (s. 70).
  • Sales Tax Officer v. Kanhaiya Lal Mukundlal Saraf (1959): Tax paid under mistake of law recoverable (s. 72).
  • Mahabir Kishore v. State of M.P. (1989): Restitution principles.

Consequences of breach of contract

Breach gives the injured party compensation for loss naturally arising or within the parties' contemplation (s. 73), subject to mitigation. Where a sum is named, the court awards reasonable compensation not exceeding it (s. 74). Specific performance became the general rule after the 2018 amendment of the Specific Relief Act.

Key provisions

  • ss. 73–75
  • Specific Relief Act, 1963 (as amended 2018), ss. 10, 14, 20

Leading cases

  • Hadley v. Baxendale (1854): Remoteness rules.
  • Fateh Chand v. Balkishan Dass (1963): s. 74: reasonable compensation; no penalty/LD distinction.
  • ONGC v. Saw Pipes (2003): Liquidated damages genuine pre-estimate.
  • Kailash Nath Associates v. DDA (2015): Proof of loss under s. 74.
  • Murlidhar Chiranjilal v. Harishchandra Dwarkadas (1962): Duty to mitigate.

Contract of indemnity, guarantee and insurance

Indemnity is a promise to save another from loss (s. 124); the indemnity holder can enforce it even before paying (equity). Guarantee is a tripartite promise to discharge a third party's liability on default (s. 126); the surety's liability is co-extensive with the debtor's (s. 128) and is discharged by variance, release or impairment of security. Insurance contracts are uberrimae fidei.

Key provisions

  • ss. 124–147
  • Insurance Act, 1938, s. 45

Leading cases

  • Gajanan Moreshwar Parelkar v. Moreshwar Madan Mantri (1942): Indemnity holder may sue before loss.
  • Bank of Bihar v. Damodar Prasad (1969): Creditor need not exhaust remedies against the debtor.
  • State Bank of India v. Indexport Registered (1992): Surety's liability co-extensive.
  • Satwant Kaur Sandhu v. New India Assurance (2009): Suppression of material facts voids policy.

Contract of agency

An agent is employed to act for, or represent, a principal in dealings with third persons (s. 182). What marks an agent is representative character and derivative authority: the power to bind the principal. Agency can arise by express or implied authority, ratification (s. 196), estoppel/holding out (s. 237) or necessity; it is terminated under s. 201, unless coupled with interest (s. 202).

Key provisions

  • ss. 182–238

Leading cases

  • Lakshminarayan Ram Gopal v. Govt. of Hyderabad (1954): Agent distinguished from servant.
  • Freeman & Lockyer v. Buckhurst Park Properties (1964): Apparent authority.
  • Watteau v. Fenwick (1893): Usual authority of undisclosed agent.
  • Keighley, Maxsted & Co. v. Durant (1901): Ratification only by a disclosed principal.
  • Great Northern Railway v. Swaffield (1874): Agency of necessity.

Sale of goods and hire purchase

Under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 a sale transfers property at once; an agreement to sell does so later (s. 4). Implied conditions as to title, description, fitness and merchantability (ss. 14–17) qualify caveat emptor; exclusion clauses are read strictly and are now policed by consumer law. Hire-purchase is a bailment with an option to buy: the hirer has not 'agreed to buy'. The Hire-Purchase Act, 1972 never came into force and was repealed in 2005.

Key provisions

  • Sale of Goods Act, 1930: ss. 4, 12–17, 18–26, 27, 45–54, 62
  • Consumer Protection Act, 2019, s. 2(46), 49(2)

Leading cases

  • Priest v. Last (1903): Fitness for purpose.
  • Grant v. Australian Knitting Mills (1936): Merchantable quality; latent defects.
  • Baldry v. Marshall (1925): Implied condition of fitness.
  • Rowland v. Divall (1923): Breach of title condition.
  • Helby v. Matthews (1895): Hirer with option has not 'agreed to buy'.
  • Lee v. Butler (1893): Instalment purchaser bound to buy.
  • K.L. Johar & Co. v. Deputy Commercial Tax Officer (1965): Hire-purchase vs sale for tax.
  • Charanjit Singh Chadha v. Sudhir Mehra (2001): Financier remains owner; hirer is bailee.

Formation and dissolution of partnership

Partnership is the relation between persons who agree to share profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all (s. 4). Sharing profits is only prima facie evidence; the real test is mutual agency (s. 6). Lenders, employees, widows of partners and sellers of goodwill may share profits without being partners. Registration affects the right to sue (s. 69).

Key provisions

  • Indian Partnership Act, 1932: ss. 4, 6, 11–13, 18–19, 25, 30, 39–44, 69
  • LLP Act, 2008

Leading cases

  • Cox v. Hickman (1860): Mutual agency is the true test.
  • K.D. Kamath & Co. v. CIT (1971): Mutual agency and partnership.
  • Haldiram Bhujiawala v. Anand Kumar Deepak Kumar (2000): s. 69 bars suits on contracts with third parties, not statutory rights.

Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881

The NI Act covers promissory notes, bills and cheques. A holder in due course takes free of defects. Dishonour of a cheque for insufficiency of funds is an offence (s. 138) with a statutory presumption of a legally enforceable debt (s. 139), interim compensation (s. 143A) and deposit in appeal (s. 148).

Key provisions

  • NI Act, 1881: ss. 4–6, 9, 118, 138–148

Leading cases

  • Rangappa v. Sri Mohan (2010): Presumption under s. 139 includes existence of debt.
  • Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod v. State of Maharashtra (2014): Jurisdiction (overtaken by 2015 amendment).
  • Meters and Instruments v. Kanchan Mehta (2017): Compounding encouraged.
  • Bir Singh v. Mukesh Kumar (2019): Blank signed cheque attracts presumption.
  • In re Expeditious Trial of Cases under s. 138 (2021): Directions to reduce pendency.

Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996

The Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, based on the UNCITRAL Model Law, keeps court intervention minimal (s. 5). Key features are the arbitration agreement (s. 7), reference (s. 8), appointment (s. 11), kompetenz-kompetenz (s. 16), narrow grounds to set aside (s. 34) and enforcement of foreign awards (Part II). Disputes involving rights in rem, crimes, insolvency, guardianship and other public matters are not arbitrable.

Key provisions

  • ss. 2, 5, 7–9, 11, 12, 16, 29A, 34, 36, 37, 44–60
  • Mediation Act, 2023
  • Commercial Courts Act, 2015, s. 12A

Leading cases

  • Bharat Aluminium Co. v. Kaiser Aluminium (2012): Part I does not apply to foreign-seated arbitrations.
  • Booz Allen & Hamilton v. SBI Home Finance (2011): Rights in rem not arbitrable.
  • Vidya Drolia v. Durga Trading Corp. (2021): Fourfold test of non-arbitrability.
  • Ssangyong Engineering v. NHAI (2019): Narrow public-policy review after 2015 amendment.
  • Central Organisation for Railway Electrification v. ECI-SPIC-SMO-MCML (2024): Unilateral appointment of arbitrators violates equality.
  • Gayatri Balasamy v. ISG Novasoft Technologies (2025): Courts have a limited power to modify awards under s. 34 (4:1).

Standard form contracts

Standard form contracts are drafted by one party and offered on a take-it-or-leave-it basis. Courts protect the weaker party through notice rules, strict interpretation (contra proferentem), refusal to enforce unconscionable terms as opposed to public policy (s. 23), and now the Consumer Protection Act's power to declare 'unfair contracts' void.

Key provisions

  • Indian Contract Act s. 23
  • CPA 2019 ss. 2(46), 49(2), 59(2)

Leading cases

  • L'Estrange v. Graucob (1934): Signature binds.
  • Thornton v. Shoe Lane Parking (1971): Notice must be before contract.
  • Central Inland Water Transport Corp. v. Brojo Nath Ganguly (1986): Unconscionable term void under s. 23.
  • LIC v. Consumer Education & Research Centre (1995): Public bodies' standard terms must be fair.
  • Pioneer Urban Land v. Govindan Raghavan (2019): One-sided builder terms are an unfair trade practice.
