Performance, discharge and frustration

Study notes

Performance (ss. 37–67)Discharge

Performance (ss. 37–67)

  • Obligation to perform (s. 37), unless dispensed with or excused; binds representatives after death unless the contract is personal.
  • Offer to perform (tender) (s. 38): an unconditional tender at a proper time and place, with an opportunity to inspect, excuses the promisor from liability for non-performance.
  • Who performs (ss. 40–41): the promisor personally if the contract is personal; otherwise an agent. If the promisee accepts performance from a third person, he cannot later enforce against the promisor.
  • Joint promisors (ss. 42–45): jointly and severally liable; contribution among them.
  • Time and place (ss. 46–50); time as the essence (s. 55): if time is of the essence, failure makes the contract voidable; in contracts for immovable property, time is ordinarily not of the essence (Chand Rani v. Kamal Rani, 1993).
  • Reciprocal promises (ss. 51–54): order of performance.

Discharge

1. By performance

Actual or attempted (tender).

2. By agreement (ss. 62–63)

  • Novation: substituting a new contract (s. 62).
  • Rescission and alteration (s. 62).
  • Remission: the promisee may dispense with or remit performance, extend time or accept other satisfaction (s. 63).
  • Waiver and merger.

3. By impossibility (s. 56): frustration

  • An agreement to do an impossible act is void (s. 56, para 1); a contract that becomes impossible or unlawful after it is made becomes void (para 2); a promisor who knew of the impossibility must compensate (para 3).
  • Satyabrata Ghose v. Mugneeram Bangur & Co. (1954): "impossible" is not literal; it includes impracticability and frustration of the purpose, where an unforeseen event destroys the foundation of the contract. Requisition of land for military purposes did not frustrate a contract to develop plots, because the delay was not of a kind that destroyed the basis of the bargain.
  • Taylor v. Caldwell (1863): destruction of the music hall; Krell v. Henry (1903): coronation procession cancelled (frustration of purpose).
  • Not frustration: increased cost or hardship (Alopi Parshad & Sons v. Union of India, 1960; Energy Watchdog v. CERC, 2017: a rise in imported coal prices did not frustrate power purchase agreements).
  • Force majeure clauses are governed by s. 32 (contingent contracts) where the parties provided for the event; s. 56 applies where they did not (Energy Watchdog).
  • COVID-19: Halliburton Offshore Services v. Vedanta (Delhi HC, 2020): the pandemic cannot excuse non-performance that began before it.

4. By breach

  • Actual breach at the time of performance.
  • Anticipatory breach (s. 39): refusal before the due date; the promisee may terminate at once or wait (Hochster v. De La Tour, 1853).

5. By operation of law

Death (personal contracts), insolvency, merger, unauthorised material alteration.

6. By lapse of time

Limitation Act, 1963 bars the remedy, not the right.

Revision points

  • Tender (s. 38); time as essence (s. 55).
  • Novation, rescission, remission (ss. 62–63).
  • Frustration: s. 56; Satyabrata Ghose (1954); not mere hardship (Alopi Parshad, Energy Watchdog 2017); s. 32 for force majeure.
  • Anticipatory breach (s. 39).
Syllabus unit 4 · Asked 7 times · 2015–2025

Answer writing: Performance and discharge of contracts

Decode the demand. Questions test frustration (s. 56) and modes of discharge. Separate frustration from force majeure.

Open with

Contracts are made to be performed, but the law discharges them when performance becomes impossible or the parties agree otherwise.

Answer frame

  1. Performance and tender
  2. Discharge by agreement (ss. 62–63)
  3. Frustration (s. 56): tests and limits
  4. Breach
  5. Cases

Concept in brief

Parties must perform or offer to perform (ss. 37–38). Time is of the essence only where intended (s. 55). Discharge occurs by performance, agreement (novation, rescission, alteration, remission: ss. 62–63), impossibility or frustration (s. 56), breach and operation of law.

Key provisions

  • ss. 37–67

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Satyabrata Ghose v. Mugneeram Bangur (1954): Frustration under s. 56 is impossibility in a practical sense.
  • Taylor v. Caldwell (1863): Destruction of subject matter.
  • Krell v. Henry (1903): Frustration of purpose.
  • Naihati Jute Mills v. Khyaliram Jagannath (1968): Mere commercial hardship is not frustration.
  • Energy Watchdog v. CERC (2017): Price rise not force majeure.
  • Hind Construction v. State of Maharashtra (1979): Time not ordinarily of essence in building contracts.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • COVID-19 force majeure litigation
  • Energy Watchdog (2017) on price rises

Critical lens

Courts read frustration narrowly to keep contracts stable.

Close with

Commercial certainty demands that frustration be rare.

Previous-year questions (7)
  1. 2025"The parties to a contract must either perform or offer to perform their respective promises unless the performance is dispensed with or excused under the provisions of the Contract Act or of any other law." Explain the statement in reference to relevant provisions of the Indian Contract Act, 1872.
  2. 2022"Time is an essence of the contract." What are the remedies available to the aggrieved party in case of non-fulfilment of the obligation within the stipulated time?
  3. 2022State the circumstances of supervening impossibility and frustration of contract in the light of the decided cases.
  4. 2021What are the various modes in which a contract may be discharged? Explain in the light of decided cases.
  5. 2019"Discharge of a contract includes breach of contract, but breach of a contract does not necessarily include discharge of contract." Examine the statement with suitable illustrations.
  6. 2018"It is well-settled that if and when there is frustration, the dissolution of the contract occurs automatically.... It does not depend on the choice or election of either party. It depends on the effect of what has actually happened on the possibility of performing the contract." Discuss the effects of the frustration of the contract.
  7. 2015A, a singer, contracts with B, the manager of a restaurant, to sing at his restaurant for two nights every week during the next two months; and B engages to pay to her ₹ 5,000/- rupees for each night's performance. On the sixth night, A wilfully absents herself from the restaurant and B, in consequence, rescinds the contract. Decide.
