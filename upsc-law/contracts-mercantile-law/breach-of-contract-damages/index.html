Breach of contract, damages and specific relief (s. 73–75)

Study notes

Remedies for breachCompensation for loss (s. 73)Liquidated damages and penalty (s. 74)Rescission (s. 75)Specific performance (Specific Relief Act, 1963, as amended in 2018)Injunctions

Remedies for breach

  1. Damages (s. 73)
  2. Liquidated damages / penalty (s. 74)
  3. Rescission (with compensation, s. 75)
  4. Specific performance and injunction (Specific Relief Act, 1963)
  5. Quantum meruit

Compensation for loss (s. 73)

  • The injured party may recover compensation for loss which naturally arose in the usual course of things from the breach, or which the parties knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result.
  • Not for remote and indirect loss.
  • Hadley v. Baxendale (1854): the two limbs (ordinary loss, and special loss within the parties' contemplation). Victoria Laundry v. Newman Industries (1949): ordinary profits recoverable, exceptional profits from special contracts not.
  • Mitigation (Explanation to s. 73): the means available to remedy the inconvenience must be considered (British Westinghouse v. Underground Electric Railways, 1912).
  • Kinds of damages: ordinary, special, nominal; exemplary damages generally not awarded in contract (except, in some English cases, dishonour of cheques by banks or breach of promise to marry). Damages for mental distress only where the contract's object is pleasure or peace of mind (Jarvis v. Swans Tours, 1973); consumer forums award compensation for harassment (Ghaziabad Development Authority v. Balbir Singh, 2004).

Liquidated damages and penalty (s. 74)

  • When a contract names a sum (or a penalty) payable on breach, the injured party may recover reasonable compensation not exceeding the sum named, whether or not actual loss is proved.
  • Indian law does not apply the English distinction between penalty and liquidated damages strictly; the stated sum is the ceiling.
  • Fateh Chand v. Balkishan Das (1963): only reasonable compensation up to the stipulated amount; forfeiture of earnest money is governed by s. 74 if it is a penalty.
  • Maula Bux v. Union of India (1969): forfeiture of security deposits must be reasonable.
  • ONGC v. Saw Pipes (2003): where the stipulated sum is a genuine pre-estimate, proof of actual loss is not needed.
  • Kailash Nath Associates v. DDA (2015): where damage or loss is not caused by the breach, no compensation can be awarded; proof of loss is dispensed with only where loss is difficult or impossible to prove.

Rescission (s. 75)

A party who rightfully rescinds is entitled to compensation for damage from non-performance.

Specific performance (Specific Relief Act, 1963, as amended in 2018)

  • Before 2018: discretionary, granted only where damages were inadequate.
  • After 2018 (s. 10): specific performance shall be enforced subject to ss. 11(2), 14 and 16; it is no longer discretionary in most cases.
  • Substituted performance (s. 20): the injured party may get the contract performed by a third party and recover the cost.
  • Contracts not specifically enforceable (s. 14): where substituted performance is available, contracts involving continuous duty the court cannot supervise, personal contracts, and determinable contracts.
  • Infrastructure projects (s. 20A, s. 41(ha)): no injunction that would impede or delay a project.
  • Katta Sujatha Reddy v. Siddamsetty Infra Projects (2022): the 2018 amendment is prospective.

Injunctions

Negative covenants may be enforced by injunction even where specific performance is not available (s. 42; Lumley v. Wagner, 1852).

Revision points

  • s. 73: Hadley v. Baxendale; mitigation.
  • s. 74: reasonable compensation up to the cap: Fateh Chand, Saw Pipes, Kailash Nath.
  • SRA 2018: specific performance the rule (s. 10), substituted performance (s. 20), infrastructure bar (s. 41(ha)).
Syllabus unit 6 · Asked 4 times · 2014–2024

Answer writing: Consequences of breach of contract

Decode the demand. Questions test ss. 73–74 and remoteness. Use Hadley's two limbs and Fateh Chand.

Open with

Damages put the injured party where it would have been had the contract been performed, but only for foreseeable losses.

Answer frame

  1. s. 73 and remoteness
  2. Mitigation
  3. s. 74: liquidated damages and penalties
  4. Specific performance after 2018
  5. Cases

Concept in brief

Breach gives the injured party compensation for loss naturally arising or within the parties' contemplation (s. 73), subject to mitigation. Where a sum is named, the court awards reasonable compensation not exceeding it (s. 74). Specific performance became the general rule after the 2018 amendment of the Specific Relief Act.

Key provisions

  • ss. 73–75
  • Specific Relief Act, 1963 (as amended 2018), ss. 10, 14, 20

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Hadley v. Baxendale (1854): Remoteness rules.
  • Fateh Chand v. Balkishan Dass (1963): s. 74: reasonable compensation; no penalty/LD distinction.
  • ONGC v. Saw Pipes (2003): Liquidated damages genuine pre-estimate.
  • Kailash Nath Associates v. DDA (2015): Proof of loss under s. 74.
  • Murlidhar Chiranjilal v. Harishchandra Dwarkadas (1962): Duty to mitigate.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Economics: efficient breach (Posner)
  • Specific Relief (Amendment) Act, 2018 and ease of doing business

Critical lens

Indian law merges penalty and liquidated damages under s. 74, unlike English law.

Close with

Remedies should make performance the norm and breach costly.

Previous-year questions (4)
  1. 2024Discuss the rules which are taken into account by the courts while awarding damages for the breach of contract. Refer to the relevant statutory provisions and case law.
  2. 2017"Section 74 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 has cut down the most troublesome knot of Common Law doctrine of awarding damages." Discuss the statement.
  3. 2016"The object of awarding damages for a breach of contract is to put the injured party in the same position, so far as money can do it, as if he had not been injured." In light of the above statement, explain the various kinds of damages that the court can award. Also, explain the rules relating to the assessment of damages.
  4. 2014"If a contract is broken, the law will endeavour so far as money can do it, to place the injured party in the same position as if the contract had been performed." Explain the above statement and discuss the principle the court follows for the assessment of damages.
