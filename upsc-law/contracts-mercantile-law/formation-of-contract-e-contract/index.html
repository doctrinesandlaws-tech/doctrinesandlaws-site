What makes a contract (Indian Contract Act, 1872)

An agreement enforceable by law is a contract (s. 2(h)). All agreements are contracts if made by the free consent of parties competent to contract, for a lawful consideration and a lawful object, and not expressly declared void (s. 10).

Offer (proposal) and acceptance

  • Offer (s. 2(a)): signifying willingness to do or abstain, with a view to obtaining assent. - General offer to the world (Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co., 1893); performance of the conditions is acceptance. - Offer must be known to be accepted: Lalman Shukla v. Gauri Dutt (1913): a servant who found the missing boy without knowing of the reward could not claim it. - Invitation to treat is not an offer: price quotations (Harvey v. Facey, 1893), goods on display (Pharmaceutical Society v. Boots, 1953), auctions, tenders.
  • Acceptance (s. 2(b)) must be absolute and unqualified (s. 7). A counter-offer rejects the original (Hyde v. Wrench, 1840). Silence is not acceptance (Felthouse v. Bindley, 1862).
  • Communication (ss. 3–5): an offer is complete when it comes to the offeree's knowledge; acceptance is complete against the proposer when put in course of transmission, and against the acceptor when it comes to the proposer's knowledge (the postal rule). Revocation of an offer is possible before acceptance is complete against the proposer.
  • Instantaneous communication: Bhagwandas Goverdhandas Kedia v. Girdharilal Parshottamdas (1966): for telephone contracts, the contract is made where the acceptance is heard; the postal rule does not apply. (Following Entores v. Miles Far East, 1955.)

Consideration

  • Definition (s. 2(d)): at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained, does or abstains, or promises to do or abstain.
  • Durga Prasad v. Baldeo (1880): an act at the desire of a third party (the Collector) is not consideration.
  • Chinnaya v. Ramayya (1882): consideration may move from a third person; contrast privity of contract (a stranger to the contract cannot sue, subject to exceptions such as trusts and family arrangements).
  • Exceptions to "no consideration, no contract" (s. 25): a written, registered agreement made out of natural love and affection between near relatives; a promise to compensate for something done voluntarily; a written promise to pay a time-barred debt. Gifts are also valid (s. 25, Expl. 1).
  • Kedarnath Bhattacharji v. Gorie Mahomed (1886): charitable subscriptions enforceable when the promisee acts on the promise.

Capacity (ss. 11–12)

  • Competent: a person of the age of majority, of sound mind, and not disqualified by law.
  • Minors: Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose (1903): a minor's agreement is void ab initio; no restitution of the mortgage money. A minor is liable for necessaries out of his estate (s. 68). Courts may order a minor who obtained property by fraud to restore it (Specific Relief Act, 1963, s. 33).
  • Persons of unsound mind (s. 12); disqualified persons (alien enemies, insolvents, convicts).

E-contracts

  • Information Technology Act, 2000: - s. 10A: contracts formed by electronic means are valid. - ss. 11–13: attribution, acknowledgement, and time and place of dispatch and receipt of electronic records. - s. 3A and s. 5: electronic signatures. - The First Schedule excludes negotiable instruments (other than cheques), powers of attorney, trusts, wills and contracts for sale of immovable property.
  • Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (ss. 61–63): electronic records as evidence.
  • Forms: click-wrap (clicking "I agree"; generally enforceable), browse-wrap (terms by hyperlink; enforceable only with reasonable notice), shrink-wrap.
  • Trimex International FZE v. Vedanta Aluminium (2010): a contract concluded through email exchange is binding even without a formal signed document.

Revision points

  • s. 10 essentials; Carlill, Lalman Shukla, Harvey v. Facey.
  • ss. 3–5 communication; Bhagwandas Kedia (1966) for instant communication.
  • s. 2(d) consideration: Durga Prasad, Chinnaya; s. 25 exceptions.
  • Mohori Bibee (1903); IT Act s. 10A; Trimex (2010).
Syllabus unit 1 · Asked 12 times · 2013–2026

Answer writing: Nature and formation of contract/E-contract

Decode the demand. Questions test offer and acceptance (including e-contracts) and privity with its exceptions. Use the Contract Act's illustrations.

Open with

A contract is an agreement enforceable by law; every essential in s. 10 is a filter that turns promises into obligations.

Answer frame

  1. Essentials (s. 10)
  2. Offer, acceptance, communication
  3. Consideration (s. 2(d)) and from a third party
  4. Privity and its exceptions
  5. E-contracts (IT Act s. 10A)

Concept in brief

A contract is an agreement enforceable by law: offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, free consent and lawful object (s. 10). Acceptance must be communicated; for instantaneous communication the contract is made where acceptance is received. E-contracts are valid (IT Act s. 10A). Privity means only parties can sue, but India allows consideration from a third party and recognises exceptions such as trusts, family arrangements, acknowledgment and agency.

Key provisions

  • Indian Contract Act, 1872: ss. 2, 3–10, 25
  • IT Act, 2000, ss. 10A, 11–13
  • BSA, 2023, ss. 61–63

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. (1893): General offer accepted by performance.
  • Lalman Shukla v. Gauri Dutt (1913): Acceptance needs knowledge of offer.
  • Bhagwandas Goverdhandas Kedia v. Girdharilal Parshottamdas (1966): Telephonic contract made where acceptance heard.
  • Balfour v. Balfour (1919): No intention to create legal relations in domestic arrangements.
  • Trimex International FZE v. Vedanta Aluminium (2010): Contract concluded by email.
  • Chinnaya v. Ramayya (1882): Consideration may move from a third party.
  • Tweddle v. Atkinson (1861): Privity rule.
  • Khwaja Muhammad Khan v. Husaini Begum (1910, PC): Beneficiary of a marriage settlement may sue.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Comparative: UK Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act, 1999
  • Click-wrap and browse-wrap agreements

Critical lens

Privity can defeat beneficiaries' legitimate expectations; Indian exceptions soften it.

Close with

Contract law evolves to fit digital commerce without losing its core of consent.

Previous-year questions (12)

Privity of Contract

  1. 2026Explain the applicability of the rule of ‘privity of contract’ in India. Also discuss the circumstances in which the rule does not prevent a person from enforcing a contract without his being a party to it. [P-II 6(b), 15 marks]
    How to approach: Explain + exceptions. Indian rule: privity of contract but not of consideration (Chinnaya). Exceptions: trusts, family arrangements, acknowledgement or estoppel, covenants running with land, agency, assignment. Comparative: UK 1999 Act.
  2. 2020"In the course of time, the courts have introduced a number of exceptions in which the rule of privity of contract does not prevent a person from enforcing a contract which has been made for his benefit but without his being a party to it." Explain the statement with the help of leading case law.
  3. 2013“Privity of contract is no longer a rule but only an exception.” Explain in the context of modern transactions.

General Principles (Offer, Acceptance, Consideration)

  1. 2025"Any departure from the terms of the offer or the addition of any qualification while accepting the offer vitiates the acceptance unless it is agreed to by the offeror." Elucidate the statement in the light of the provisions of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 and established principles.
  2. 2023"The law of contract is not the whole law of agreements, nor is it the whole law of obligations. It is the law of those agreements which create obligations, and those obligations which have their source in agreement" - Salmond. Critically examine this statement.
  3. 2022"All the contracts are agreements, but all the agreements are not contracts." Elucidate the statement.
  4. 2019"The law of contracts is not the whole law of agreements, nor is it the whole law of obligations, but it also deals with the rights and obligations of both." Elucidate.
  5. 2018If certain goods are displayed either in a show window or inside the shop and such goods bear price tags, discuss whether such display amounts to an offer to sell. Explain the distinction between offer and invitation to offer with the help of decided cases.
  6. 2017"Revocation of proposal is death of the proposal." Explain the statement and mention the manners of revocation.
  7. 2017Under the Indian Contract Act, 1872, when is a contract deemed to be entered into by the parties? Discuss.
  8. 2016"An offer is to an acceptance what a lighted match-stick is to a train of gunpowder. It produces something which cannot be recalled or undone" - Anson. Explain.
