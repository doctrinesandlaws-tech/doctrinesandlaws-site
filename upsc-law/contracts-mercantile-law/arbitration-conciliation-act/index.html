Framework

The Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 is based on the UNCITRAL Model Law (1985) and the UNCITRAL Conciliation Rules. Major amendments: 2015, 2019 and 2021.

  • Part I: arbitration seated in India (domestic and international commercial arbitration).
  • Part II: enforcement of foreign awards (New York Convention, Geneva Convention).
  • Part III: conciliation.
  • After Bharat Aluminium Co. v. Kaiser Aluminium (BALCO, 2012), Part I does not apply to foreign-seated arbitrations (except ss. 9, 27, 37 as added in 2015 unless excluded).

The arbitration agreement

  • s. 7: in writing, including exchange of letters, emails or electronic communications; can be a clause or a separate agreement.
  • s. 8: a judicial authority must refer parties to arbitration if a valid agreement exists (prima facie test after 2015).
  • In Re: Interplay between Arbitration Agreements under the A&C Act and the Indian Stamp Act (2023, 7 judges): an unstamped arbitration agreement is not void; stamping objections go to the tribunal.
  • Cox and Kings v. SAP India (2023, 5 judges): the group of companies doctrine can bind non-signatories based on mutual intention.

The tribunal

  • Appointment (s. 11): by the parties; failing that, by the Supreme Court or High Court (or designated arbitral institutions, after 2019).
  • Independence and impartiality (s. 12, Fifth and Seventh Schedules): persons with listed relationships are ineligible unless waived in writing after disputes arise. - TRF Ltd. v. Energo Engineering (2017) and Perkins Eastman v. HSCC (2019): a person ineligible to be an arbitrator cannot appoint one; unilateral appointment by an interested party is invalid. - Central Organisation for Railway Electrification v. ECI-SPIC-SMO-MCML (JV) (2024, 5 judges): unilateral appointment clauses in public-private contracts violate equality of parties; PSUs cannot compel the other side to choose from a panel they control.
  • Competence-competence (s. 16): the tribunal rules on its own jurisdiction.
  • Interim measures: by courts (s. 9) and by the tribunal (s. 17, enforceable like court orders).
  • Time limit (s. 29A): award within 12 months of completion of pleadings, extendable by 6 months by consent and further by court; international commercial arbitrations are exempt.
  • Fast track (s. 29B).

Challenging and enforcing awards

  • s. 34: grounds include incapacity, invalid agreement, lack of notice, matters beyond scope, improper composition, non-arbitrability, and conflict with the public policy of India. - The 2015 Explanation narrowed public policy to fraud or corruption, contravention of the fundamental policy of Indian law, and conflict with basic notions of morality or justice; no review on merits. - Patent illegality applies only to domestic awards (s. 34(2A)). - Ssangyong Engineering v. NHAI (2019): an award that rewrites the contract or ignores vital evidence may be set aside. - Limitation: three months plus 30 days (s. 34(3)). - Gayatri Balasamy v. ISG Novasoft Technologies (2025, 5 judges, 4:1): courts have a limited power to modify awards under s. 34 (severing invalid portions, correcting clerical errors, adjusting post-award interest), but cannot review merits.
  • Automatic stay on enforcement was removed in 2015 (s. 36): stay must be sought.
  • Appeals (s. 37) are limited.

Foreign awards (Part II)

  • s. 44: New York Convention awards from notified countries.
  • s. 48: refusal grounds mirror Art. V of the Convention; public policy narrowed (2015).
  • Vijay Karia v. Prysmian Cavi (2020): enforcement can be refused only in exceptional cases.
  • Seat vs venue: BGS SGS Soma JV v. NHPC (2019): designating a "venue" without contrary indicia means the seat.

Conciliation (Part III, ss. 61–81)

  • A voluntary, non-binding process; the conciliator assists the parties (s. 67).
  • A settlement agreement signed by the parties (s. 73) has the same status and effect as an arbitral award (s. 74).
  • Confidentiality (s. 75); no arbitration or litigation during conciliation (s. 77).
  • The Mediation Act, 2023 provides a statutory framework for mediation, including pre-litigation mediation and enforceable settlement agreements, and is set to replace much of the conciliation framework; its provisions are being brought into force in phases.

Revision points

  • Parts I–III; Model Law base; BALCO (2012) territoriality.
  • s. 7, 8, 11, 12 (Perkins Eastman, CORE 2024), 16, 17, 29A.
  • s. 34 public policy (2015 Explanation), patent illegality (domestic only), Ssangyong (2019), Gayatri Balasamy (2025) modification.
  • Part II s. 48; conciliation s. 74; Mediation Act 2023.
Syllabus unit 12 · Asked 10 times · 2014–2025

Answer writing: Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996

Decode the demand. Questions test arbitrability, court intervention and the role of ADR in public matters.

Open with

The Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 makes party autonomy the rule and court intervention the exception.

Answer frame

  1. Model Law basis and minimal intervention (s. 5)
  2. Arbitration agreement and arbitrability
  3. Appointment and kompetenz-kompetenz
  4. Challenge and enforcement
  5. Amendments and reform

Concept in brief

The Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, based on the UNCITRAL Model Law, keeps court intervention minimal (s. 5). Key features are the arbitration agreement (s. 7), reference (s. 8), appointment (s. 11), kompetenz-kompetenz (s. 16), narrow grounds to set aside (s. 34) and enforcement of foreign awards (Part II). Disputes involving rights in rem, crimes, insolvency, guardianship and other public matters are not arbitrable.

Key provisions

  • ss. 2, 5, 7–9, 11, 12, 16, 29A, 34, 36, 37, 44–60
  • Mediation Act, 2023
  • Commercial Courts Act, 2015, s. 12A

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Bharat Aluminium Co. v. Kaiser Aluminium (2012): Part I does not apply to foreign-seated arbitrations.
  • Booz Allen & Hamilton v. SBI Home Finance (2011): Rights in rem not arbitrable.
  • Vidya Drolia v. Durga Trading Corp. (2021): Fourfold test of non-arbitrability.
  • Ssangyong Engineering v. NHAI (2019): Narrow public-policy review after 2015 amendment.
  • Central Organisation for Railway Electrification v. ECI-SPIC-SMO-MCML (2024): Unilateral appointment of arbitrators violates equality.
  • Gayatri Balasamy v. ISG Novasoft Technologies (2025): Courts have a limited power to modify awards under s. 34 (4:1).

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Law Commission 246th Report (2014) → 2015 amendment
  • Justice B.N. Srikrishna Committee (2017) → 2019 amendment
  • India International Arbitration Centre Act, 2019

Critical lens

Court intervention and delay still deter international parties from choosing India as a seat.

Close with

India's arbitration ambition depends on courts trusting tribunals.
