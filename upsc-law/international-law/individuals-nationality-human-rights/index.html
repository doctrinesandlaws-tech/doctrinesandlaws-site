UPSC Law Optional · Paper I · Unit 5

Individuals: nationality, statelessness, human rights

International Law for the UPSC Civil Services (Main) Law optional: how to decode the question, an answer frame, key provisions, leading cases and 16 previous-year questions (2013–2026).

All 15 units of International Law · Jump to the questions

Syllabus unit 5 · Asked 16 times · 2013–2026

Syllabus: Individuals

Decode the demand. Questions test nationality (modes, nationality vs domicile, statelessness) and enforcement of human rights. Give mechanisms, not just rights.

Open with

The individual has moved from an object to a subject of international law, through nationality, human rights and individual criminal responsibility.

Answer frame

  1. Nationality: modes, genuine link (Nottebohm)
  2. Nationality vs domicile
  3. Statelessness conventions
  4. Human rights: instruments and enforcement mechanisms
  5. Indian position

Concept in brief

Individuals are now subjects of international law in significant ways: nationality links them to a State, human rights treaties give them rights and sometimes a direct petition, and international criminal law makes them responsible. Nationality differs from domicile, which is a private-law link of permanent residence. Enforcement works through treaty bodies, the UN Human Rights Council and its Universal Periodic Review, and regional courts.

Key provisions

  • UDHR 1948
  • ICCPR and ICESCR 1966, First Optional Protocol to ICCPR
  • 1954 and 1961 statelessness conventions
  • Citizenship Act, 1955
  • Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Nottebohm (Liechtenstein v. Guatemala) (1955): Genuine link needed for diplomatic protection.
  • Barcelona Traction (1970): Obligations erga omnes.
  • LaGrand (Germany v. USA) (2001): Individual rights under the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations; provisional measures binding.
  • Jadhav (India v. Pakistan) (2019): Denial of consular access breached Art. 36 VCCR.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • UN treaty bodies, Universal Periodic Review, special rapporteurs
  • Regional courts (ECtHR, Inter-American Court)
  • Assam NRC and statelessness concerns

Critical lens

Enforcement still depends on State consent and political will.

Close with

Human rights are universal in claim but local in enforcement; national institutions are the first line.

Previous-year questions (16)

(a) Nationality

  1. 2026Define the term ‘nationality’. State the various modes of acquisition of nationality by a person. Differentiate between ‘nationality’ and ‘domicile’. [P-I 7(c), 15 marks]
    How to approach: Define nationality; modes (birth: jus soli/jus sanguinis, naturalisation, registration, resumption, subjugation); nationality vs domicile table; Nottebohm. Indian Citizenship Act, 1955 as an illustration.
  2. 2025Examine the definition, meaning of 'Nationality' and modes of acquisition of nationality. Also, make a distinction between Nationality and Citizenship. [7a]
  3. 2024Examine the importance of nationality and discuss the modes of acquisition of nationality. [5c]
  4. 2022Discuss various modes of acquisition and loss of Nationality. [6c]
  5. 2021Define 'Double Nationality' and 'Statelessness'. Evaluate the efforts taken to eliminate or reduce them. [5d]
  6. 2017Define 'Nationality'. What are the modes of acquisition and loss of nationality? What is the position of nationality of married women? [7b]
  7. 2015"A man's nationality is a continuing legal relationship between the sovereign state on the one hand and the citizen on the other." Explain the above statement. Also mention the difference between 'nationality', 'double nationality' and 'statelessness'. [6b]
  8. 2013“States are not under a duty to recognise a nationality acquired by a person who has no genuine link or connection with the naturalising state.” In light of the above statement, explain the concept of ‘nationality’ and its acquisition with the help of suitable instances. [5e]

(c) Human Rights, and procedures available for their enforcement

  1. 2026What do you mean by Human Rights? Discuss the procedure for the enforcement of Human Rights of a person as laid down under the International Law. [P-I 8(c), 15 marks]
    How to approach: Define human rights, then enforcement procedures: Charter-based (HRC, UPR, special procedures), treaty-based (committees, individual complaints), regional courts, ICC for atrocity crimes. India's national institutions.
  2. 2023"Triumph of Positivism has reduced an individual to be an object of international law rather than a subject of international law." Comment on the status of the individual under international law in the light of the above statement. [5a]
  3. 2020Discuss the status of individuals in International Law, especially with reference to human rights treaties. [5e]
  4. 2018"International law is primarily concerned with the Rights, Duties and Interests of States." Critically examine the statement with reference to the place of Individuals and Non-State entities in International law. [8a]
  5. 2018It is generally viewed that "Rights and Duties are correlative". However, the International Human Rights Movement has developed more as rights-oriented than duties-oriented. Why has this happened? Explain with the help of various International Human Rights instruments. Can you think of a 'Human Duty Movement' instead of a 'Human Rights Movement'? [5c]
  6. 2016Discuss the status of individuals in International Law, especially with respect to Human Rights Treaties. [5b]
  7. 2015"In the ultimate analysis, individuals alone are the subjects of International law." Comment. Also discuss the Transformation theory in the context of the relation between International law and Municipal law. [8c]

(b) Statelessness

  1. 2024What is statelessness? A stateless person is often subjected to a number of human rights violations. What are the impediments that people face due to statelessness? Elaborate the human rights issues that are connected to statelessness. [7c]
