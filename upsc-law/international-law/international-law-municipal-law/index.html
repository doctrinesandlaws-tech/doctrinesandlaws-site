Study notes

TheoriesMethods of receptionPractice of other StatesThe international planeThe Indian positionAssessment

Theories

  • Monism: international and domestic law form one legal order. In Kelsen's version, international law is supreme.
  • Dualism (Triepel, Anzilotti): two separate systems; international law applies domestically only when transformed by national law.
  • Coordination or harmonisation (Fitzmaurice, Rousseau): the systems operate in different fields; conflicts are resolved by the State's international responsibility rather than by one system invalidating the other.

Methods of reception

  • Incorporation (adoption): custom becomes part of national law automatically unless inconsistent with statute.
  • Transformation: a treaty must be enacted by the legislature to have domestic effect.

Practice of other States

  • United Kingdom: customary international law is part of the common law (Trendtex Trading v. Central Bank of Nigeria, 1977); treaties need an Act of Parliament.
  • United States: treaties are the "supreme law of the land" (Art. VI), but only self-executing treaties apply directly in courts (Medellín v. Texas, 2008).

The international plane

A State cannot rely on its internal law to justify failing to perform a treaty (Art. 27, VCLT). Internal law is merely a fact for international tribunals (Certain German Interests in Polish Upper Silesia, PCIJ, 1926).

The Indian position

  • Art. 51(c): the State shall endeavour to foster respect for international law and treaty obligations (a Directive Principle).
  • Art. 253: Parliament may make laws to implement treaties, even on State List subjects.
  • Art. 73: the Union's executive power includes entering into treaties; there is no requirement of parliamentary approval (Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel v. Union of India, 1969). But cession of territory needs a constitutional amendment (In re Berubari Union, 1960).
  • Customs are incorporated: Gramophone Co. of India v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey (1984): rules of customary international law are part of Indian law unless they conflict with an Act of Parliament.
  • Treaties need legislation, but courts use them where there is no conflict: - Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin (1980): Art. 11 ICCPR (no imprisonment for inability to pay a debt) read into the Civil Procedure Code to require proof of wilful default. - Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997): CEDAW used to frame guidelines on sexual harassment where domestic law was silent. - PUCL v. Union of India (1997): Art. 17 ICCPR read into Art. 21 for phone tapping safeguards. - Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996): the precautionary and polluter-pays principles are part of customary international law and of Indian law. - National Legal Services Authority v. Union of India (2014): the Yogyakarta Principles guided recognition of transgender persons. - K.S. Puttaswamy (2017): international human rights instruments informed the right to privacy.

Assessment

India is broadly dualist for treaties and monist for custom. Courts act as "transformers" by interpreting rights in harmony with international obligations, which critics call backdoor incorporation and supporters see as faithful to Art. 51(c).

Revision points

  • Monism / dualism / coordination.
  • UK: Trendtex; US: Medellín; VCLT Art. 27.
  • India: Arts. 51(c), 73, 253; Maganbhai; Gramophone (custom); Jolly George, Vishaka, PUCL, Vellore (treaties and custom used).
Syllabus unit 2 · Asked 12 times · 2013–2026

Answer writing: Relationship between International Law and Municipal Law

Decode the demand. Questions ask for theories (monism, dualism, coordination) and Indian practice. Keep the theory brief and give the Indian position in depth.

Open with

How a State receives international law is decided by its own constitution; the theories only explain the choices.

Answer frame

  1. Monism, dualism, coordination theories
  2. UK and US practice
  3. Indian constitutional scheme (Arts. 51(c), 73, 253)
  4. Judicial approach in India (Gramophone, Vishaka, NALSA)
  5. Assessment

Concept in brief

Monists see one legal order with international law supreme; dualists see two systems, requiring transformation. Practice is mixed: the UK incorporates custom, while treaties need legislation. India is dualist for treaties (Art. 253), but courts read international law into domestic law where it is consistent and fills gaps.

Key provisions

  • Constitution, Arts. 51(c), 73, 246, 253
  • VCLT 1969, Art. 27

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Trendtex Trading v. Central Bank of Nigeria (1977): Doctrine of incorporation for custom.
  • Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel v. Union of India (1969): Treaty implementation and Art. 253.
  • Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin (1980): Covenant rights apply only through municipal law, but guide interpretation.
  • Gramophone Co. of India v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey (1984): Comity and incorporation where not in conflict with statute.
  • Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997): CEDAW used to fill a legislative gap.
  • National Legal Services Authority v. Union of India (2014): International norms (Yogyakarta Principles) read into Arts. 14, 19, 21.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Kelsen (monism) vs Triepel and Anzilotti (dualism)
  • Comparative: Art. 25 German Basic Law; US Supremacy Clause
  • Data: India's treaty practice without parliamentary approval

Critical lens

Judicial incorporation fills gaps but can bypass Parliament's treaty role.

Close with

India is dualist in form and harmonising in practice.

    How to approach: Critically examine theories: monism (Kelsen), dualism (Triepel, Anzilotti), transformation, incorporation, coordination. Then Indian practice (Arts. 51(c), 253; Gramophone; Vishaka). Critique: no theory explains practice fully.
