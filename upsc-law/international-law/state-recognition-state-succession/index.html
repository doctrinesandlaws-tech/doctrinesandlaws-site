Study notes

StatehoodRecognition of StatesRecognition of governmentsState succession

Statehood

Montevideo Convention on the Rights and Duties of States (1933), Art. 1: a State needs a permanent population, a defined territory, an effective government and capacity to enter into relations with other States. Self-determination and the manner of creation (not by unlawful force) now also matter.

Recognition of States

  • Constitutive theory (Oppenheim, Lauterpacht): a State exists in law only once recognised by others. Lauterpacht argued there is a duty to recognise an entity that meets the criteria.
  • Declaratory theory: recognition only acknowledges an existing fact (Montevideo Convention, Art. 3: "the political existence of the State is independent of recognition"). This is the prevailing view, though recognition has strong practical effects.
  • Forms: express (a formal statement) or implied (establishing diplomatic relations, concluding a bilateral treaty); de jure (full) or de facto (provisional, where stability is uncertain).
  • Premature recognition of a secessionist entity while the parent State is still fighting can amount to intervention.
  • Collective recognition through admission to the UN.
  • Stimson doctrine (1932): non-recognition of situations created by unlawful force; now reflected in the duty of non-recognition of serious breaches of peremptory norms (ILC Articles on State Responsibility, Art. 41).
  • Accordance with International Law of the Unilateral Declaration of Independence in respect of Kosovo (ICJ advisory opinion, 2010): the declaration itself did not violate international law; the Court did not decide whether Kosovo is a State.

Recognition of governments

  • Relevant when governments change unconstitutionally.
  • Tobar doctrine (1907): do not recognise governments that come to power unconstitutionally.
  • Estrada doctrine (Mexico, 1930): recognise States, not governments; simply continue or withdraw diplomatic relations.
  • Tinoco Arbitration (1923): effective control matters more than recognition.
  • Effects in domestic courts: Luther v. Sagor (1921): recognition is retroactive to the date the government took control.

State succession

Succession is the replacement of one State by another in responsibility for a territory.

  • Treaties: Vienna Convention on Succession of States in respect of Treaties (1978): - Newly independent States (former colonies) start with a clean slate (Art. 16). - On separation or dissolution, treaties generally continue for each successor (Art. 34). - On transfer of territory, the "moving treaty frontiers" rule applies (Art. 15). - Boundary treaties and territorial regimes are not affected (Arts. 11–12).
  • Property, archives and debts: Vienna Convention of 1983 (not in force); in practice, equitable apportionment by agreement.
  • Uti possidetis juris: colonial boundaries become international boundaries (Burkina Faso v. Mali, ICJ, 1986).
  • Membership of organisations: India continued the UN membership of British India in 1947 while Pakistan was admitted as a new member (Indian Independence (International Arrangements) Order, 1947). Russia continued the USSR's seat (1991).
  • Private rights and contracts: acquired rights are generally respected, though nationalisation with compensation is permitted.

Revision points

  • Montevideo criteria; declaratory theory prevails; Kosovo AO (2010).
  • De jure vs de facto; Stimson and Estrada doctrines.
  • VCSST 1978: clean slate for newly independent States; boundary treaties survive; uti possidetis.
Syllabus unit 3 · Asked 15 times · 2013–2026

Answer writing: State Recognition and State Succession

Decode the demand. Expect theories of recognition and the difference between recognition and succession. Use modern examples.

Open with

Recognition is a political act with legal consequences; succession concerns what happens to rights and obligations when sovereignty changes.

Answer frame

  1. Recognition: theories and modes (de jure, de facto, express, implied)
  2. Doctrines (Estrada, Stimson, Tobar)
  3. Succession: treaties, property, debts
  4. Recognition vs succession compared
  5. Contemporary cases

Concept in brief

Recognition is the acknowledgement by one State of another State or government. The constitutive theory says recognition creates legal personality; the declaratory theory (Montevideo Convention, Art. 3) says it only confirms existing statehood. Succession concerns which rights and duties pass when sovereignty changes; Vienna Conventions of 1978 and 1983 apply the clean-slate rule to newly independent States, with exceptions for boundaries.

Key provisions

  • Montevideo Convention, 1933, Arts. 1, 3
  • Vienna Convention on Succession of States in respect of Treaties, 1978
  • Vienna Convention (State Property, Archives and Debts), 1983
  • Indian Independence (International Arrangements) Order, 1947

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Tinoco Arbitration (UK v. Costa Rica) (1923): Effective control, not recognition, decides who governs.
  • Luther v. Sagor (1921): Recognition is retroactive in effect.
  • Burkina Faso/Mali Frontier Dispute (1986): Uti possidetis juris for colonial boundaries.
  • Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros (Hungary/Slovakia) (1997): Territorial treaties survive succession.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Kosovo Advisory Opinion (2010)
  • The Taliban government (2021) and non-recognition
  • India's own succession in 1947

Critical lens

Recognition remains discretionary and politicised; succession rules are only partly codified.

Close with

The declaratory view dominates doctrine, but the constitutive effect shows in practice.
