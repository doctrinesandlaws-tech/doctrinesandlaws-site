Defining terrorism

There is no comprehensive treaty definition. India proposed a Comprehensive Convention on International Terrorism in 1996; negotiations stall over the "freedom fighter" question and the treatment of State armed forces. The Security Council resolution 1566 (2004) gives a working description: criminal acts intended to cause death or serious injury to provoke terror or compel a government or organisation.

The sectoral conventions

  • Aviation: Tokyo Convention (1963) on offences on board aircraft; Hague Convention (1970) for the suppression of unlawful seizure of aircraft (hijacking); Montreal Convention (1971) on acts against the safety of civil aviation; Beijing Convention and Protocol (2010).
  • Others: Internationally Protected Persons (1973), Hostages (1979), Maritime Navigation (SUA, 1988), Terrorist Bombings (1997), Financing of Terrorism (1999), Nuclear Terrorism (2005).
  • Common features: define offences, require criminalisation, establish jurisdiction on several bases, and impose aut dedere aut judicare (extradite or prosecute).

Security Council measures

  • Resolution 1267 (1999) sanctions regime (Al-Qaida/ISIL lists: asset freeze, travel ban, arms embargo).
  • Resolution 1373 (2001), a binding Chapter VII resolution: criminalise financing, freeze assets, deny safe haven, share information; created the Counter-Terrorism Committee.
  • Resolution 2178 (2014) on foreign terrorist fighters.
  • The Financial Action Task Force sets standards against terrorist financing.

State-sponsored terrorism and responsibility

  • States must not organise, assist or tolerate terrorist acts against other States (Friendly Relations Declaration, 1970).
  • Attribution under the law of State responsibility: the "effective control" test (Nicaragua, 1986; Bosnian Genocide, ICJ, 2007) versus the "overall control" test (Tadić, ICTY, 1999) for classifying conflicts.
  • Lockerbie: Libya surrendered suspects (1999) for trial and paid compensation; Security Council sanctions were lifted in 2003.
  • India: the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 (amended 2004, 2008, 2019); the Anti-Hijacking Act, 2016 (replaced the 1982 Act; death penalty where hijacking causes death); the Suppression of Unlawful Acts against Safety of Maritime Navigation and Fixed Platforms on Continental Shelf Act, 2002.

The International Criminal Court

  • Rome Statute (1998; in force 2002); seat at The Hague.
  • Crimes: genocide (Art. 6), crimes against humanity (Art. 7), war crimes (Art. 8), aggression (Art. 8bis).
  • Complementarity (Art. 17): the ICC acts only when national courts are unwilling or unable genuinely to prosecute.
  • Preconditions (Art. 12): crime on the territory of, or by a national of, a State party (or a State accepting jurisdiction).
  • Triggers (Art. 13): referral by a State party, referral by the Security Council (Darfur, 2005; Libya, 2011), or the Prosecutor's own motion with pre-trial authorisation.
  • Art. 16: the Council may defer an investigation for 12 months.
  • No immunity for officials (Art. 27); arrest depends on State cooperation, which has been a recurring weakness, including when warrants target serving leaders.
  • India is not a party: concerns include the Security Council's role in referral and deferral, the Prosecutor's own-motion powers, and the exclusion of terrorism and nuclear weapons from the list of crimes.

Revision points

  • No general definition; CCIT (India, 1996).
  • Hague 1970 (hijacking), Montreal 1971; Financing 1999; aut dedere aut judicare.
  • UNSC 1267, 1373; FATF.
  • ICC: Arts. 5–8bis, 12, 13, 16, 17, 27; India's reasons for staying out.
Syllabus unit 13 · Asked 10 times · 2015–2025

Answer writing: International Terrorism, State-sponsored terrorism, Hijacking, International Criminal Court

Decode the demand. Questions test the definitional gap in terrorism, hijacking conventions and the ICC. Show both the legal framework and India's role.

Open with

Terrorism lacks a universal definition, so international law fights it through sectoral treaties and Security Council action.

Answer frame

  1. Definitional problem and India's CCIT
  2. Sectoral conventions (hijacking, financing)
  3. UNSC Res. 1373 and the 1267 sanctions list
  4. ICC: jurisdiction, complementarity
  5. India's stance on the Rome Statute

Concept in brief

There is no agreed general definition of terrorism; India's draft Comprehensive Convention (1996) remains pending. Sectoral treaties cover hijacking (Tokyo 1963, Hague 1970, Montreal 1971, Beijing 2010), financing (1999) and bombings. The ICC (Rome Statute, 1998) tries genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and aggression on a complementarity basis; India is not a party.

Key provisions

  • Hague Convention 1970
  • UNSC Res. 1373 (2001)
  • Rome Statute Arts. 5–8 bis, 13, 17, 27
  • Anti-Hijacking Act, 2016
  • UAPA 1967

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Lockerbie (Libya v. UK/USA) (1992): Chapter VII resolutions prevail over the Montreal Convention.
  • Prosecutor v. Thomas Lubanga (ICC 2012): First ICC conviction: child soldiers.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • FATF as a soft-law enforcement tool
  • ICC arrest warrants against sitting leaders (2023, 2024)
  • Anti-Hijacking Act, 2016; UAPA

Critical lens

Political disagreement over 'freedom fighters' blocks a general definition.

Close with

Effective counter-terrorism needs a common definition and accountability without double standards.
