Territorial jurisdiction, extradition and asylum

Bases of jurisdictionLimits: immunitiesExtraditionAsylum

Bases of jurisdiction

Jurisdiction is a State's power to make, apply and enforce law. S.S. Lotus (PCIJ, 1927): a State may not enforce its law on another's territory, but may legislate over conduct abroad unless a rule prohibits it.

  1. Territorial principle (primary): subjective (the act began in the territory) and objective (its effects were completed there, as in Lotus).
  2. Nationality (active personality): over nationals wherever they are. India: s. 1(4)–(5) of the BNS and s. 208 of the BNSS (earlier IPC s. 4 and CrPC s. 188).
  3. Passive personality: over crimes against nationals abroad (accepted for terrorism).
  4. Protective principle: acts abroad threatening State security (counterfeiting, espionage).
  5. Universal jurisdiction: piracy, war crimes, genocide, crimes against humanity, torture (Eichmann, Israel, 1961).

Limits: immunities

  • State immunity: from absolute to restrictive immunity (no immunity for commercial acts). Jurisdictional Immunities of the State (Germany v. Italy) (ICJ, 2012): immunity applies even for serious violations by armed forces in wartime.
  • Diplomatic immunity: Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, 1961 (inviolability of the premises and of the diplomat; full criminal immunity).
  • Heads of State and foreign ministers: Arrest Warrant (DRC v. Belgium) (ICJ, 2002): a serving foreign minister enjoys full immunity from foreign criminal jurisdiction, but immunity is not impunity (prosecution at home, after office, or before international courts).

Extradition

The surrender of an accused or convicted person by one State to another. There is no general duty without a treaty, though comity may be enough.

  • Principles: - Double criminality: the act must be a crime in both States. - Speciality: the person may be tried only for the offence for which extradition was granted (Daya Singh Lahoria v. Union of India, 2001). - Political offence exception: not available for terrorism (under multilateral conventions and India's treaties). - Non-extradition of nationals by some civil-law States. - Aut dedere aut judicare: extradite or prosecute (Belgium v. Senegal, ICJ, 2012, on the Torture Convention). - Human rights bars: risk of torture or death penalty (Soering v. United Kingdom, ECtHR, 1989).
  • India: the Extradition Act, 1962 (Chapter II for non-treaty States, Chapter III for treaty States; s. 31 restrictions including political offences, and the 1993 amendment excluding listed offences from the political-offence defence). Extraditions with assurances, for example Abu Salem (Portugal, 2005; the Supreme Court later required India to honour the assurance on sentence).
  • Savarkar case (France v. Britain, PCA, 1911): irregularity in arrest on French soil did not require his return.

Asylum

  • Territorial asylum: given within the State's own territory; an exercise of sovereignty. UDHR Art. 14: right to seek and enjoy asylum, but no right to be granted it. UN Declaration on Territorial Asylum, 1967.
  • Diplomatic asylum: in embassies. Not recognised as a general rule of international law. Asylum case (Colombia v. Peru) (ICJ, 1950): Colombia failed to prove a regional custom allowing it to qualify the offence unilaterally; Haya de la Torre (1951).
  • Non-refoulement: no return to persecution (Art. 33, Refugee Convention; Art. 3, Convention against Torture).
  • India: not a party to the Refugee Convention; refugees are governed by the Foreigners Act and executive policy. Ktaer Abbas Habib Al Qutaifi v. Union of India (Gujarat HC, 1999) read non-refoulement into Art. 21; Mohammad Salimullah v. Union of India (2021) declined to stay the deportation of Rohingya refugees, holding that the right not to be deported is ancillary to the right to reside under Art. 19(1)(e), available only to citizens.

Revision points

  • Five bases; Lotus (1927); universal jurisdiction (Eichmann).
  • Immunities: Germany v. Italy (2012), Arrest Warrant (2002).
  • Extradition: double criminality, speciality (Daya Singh Lahoria), political offence, aut dedere aut judicare.
  • Asylum: Colombia v. Peru (1950); non-refoulement; Salimullah (2021).
Syllabus unit 6 · Asked 15 times · 2013–2026

Answer writing: Territorial jurisdiction of States, Extradition, and Asylum

Decode the demand. Questions ask about jurisdiction principles, extradition and asylum, often 'is asylum a right?'. Distinguish the right to seek from the right to receive.

Open with

Jurisdiction marks the reach of a State's law; extradition and asylum mark the limits of that reach across borders.

Answer frame

  1. Bases of jurisdiction
  2. Extradition principles and Indian law
  3. Asylum: territorial vs diplomatic
  4. Is asylum a right? (UDHR Art. 14, non-refoulement)
  5. Indian practice

Concept in brief

A State has jurisdiction on territorial, nationality, protective, passive-personality and universality grounds. Extradition rests on treaties and reciprocity, with double criminality, speciality and the political-offence exception. Asylum is territorial (granted within the State) or diplomatic (in embassies, accepted only regionally). The UDHR gives a right to seek asylum, not a right to be granted it; non-refoulement protects refugees.

Key provisions

  • Extradition Act, 1962
  • UDHR Art. 14
  • Refugee Convention 1951, Art. 33 (India not a party)

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • S.S. Lotus (PCIJ 1927): Objective territorial jurisdiction.
  • Asylum (Colombia v. Peru) (1950): Diplomatic asylum is not general custom.
  • Savarkar (France v. Great Britain) (PCA 1911): No duty to return a fugitive irregularly recaptured.
  • Abu Salem v. State of Maharashtra (2010): Rule of speciality binds Indian prosecutors.
  • NHRC v. State of Arunachal Pradesh (1996): Chakma refugees protected under Art. 21.
  • Mohammad Salimullah v. Union of India (2021): Non-refoulement claims of Rohingya; no interim stay on deportation.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • India hosts Tibetan, Sri Lankan Tamil and other refugees without a refugee statute
  • Comparative: EU Common European Asylum System
  • Recent extradition debates over fugitive economic offenders

Critical lens

Without a national refugee law, protection in India depends on executive discretion.

Close with

Asylum remains a State's right to grant, softened by non-refoulement's duty not to return.
