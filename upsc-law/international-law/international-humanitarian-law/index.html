What international humanitarian law (IHL) does

IHL (the law of armed conflict, jus in bello) limits the effects of armed conflict: it protects people who are not or no longer fighting and restricts means and methods of warfare. It applies equally to all parties regardless of who started the war (it is separate from jus ad bellum).

Sources

  • Hague law (means and methods): Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907, especially the 1907 Regulations.
  • Geneva law (protection of victims): the four Geneva Conventions of 1949: - I: wounded and sick in the field - II: wounded, sick and shipwrecked at sea - III: prisoners of war - IV: civilians
  • Additional Protocols of 1977: AP I (international conflicts, including wars of national liberation), AP II (non-international conflicts); AP III (2005) adds the Red Crystal emblem. India has not ratified AP I or AP II.
  • Common Article 3: minimum protections in non-international armed conflicts.
  • Customary IHL: the ICRC Customary IHL Study (2005); Tadić (ICTY Appeals, 1995) confirmed that many rules apply in non-international conflicts.
  • Martens Clause: in cases not covered by treaties, civilians and combatants remain under the protection of the principles of humanity and the dictates of public conscience.

Fundamental principles

  1. Distinction: between civilians and combatants, and between civilian objects and military objectives; indiscriminate attacks are prohibited (AP I, Arts. 48, 51).
  2. Proportionality: an attack is prohibited if expected incidental civilian harm is excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated (AP I, Art. 51(5)(b)).
  3. Precaution: constant care to spare civilians; verify targets; give effective advance warning where possible (AP I, Art. 57).
  4. Military necessity: only measures needed to achieve a legitimate military purpose, and not otherwise prohibited.
  5. Humanity / unnecessary suffering: weapons causing superfluous injury are prohibited (Nuclear Weapons advisory opinion, 1996, called distinction and unnecessary suffering "cardinal principles").

Weapons conventions

Biological Weapons Convention (1972), Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (1980, with protocols on blinding lasers, landmines and others), Chemical Weapons Convention (1993), Ottawa Mine Ban Treaty (1997; India not a party), Convention on Cluster Munitions (2008; India not a party).

Enforcement

  • Grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions require States to search for and prosecute or extradite offenders (universal jurisdiction).
  • International tribunals: ICTY (1993), ICTR (1994), the ICC (war crimes under Art. 8 of the Rome Statute).
  • The ICRC visits detainees, acts as a neutral intermediary and promotes IHL.
  • India: the Geneva Conventions Act, 1960.

Contemporary developments

  • Autonomous weapons: the CCW Group of Governmental Experts discusses limits on lethal autonomous weapon systems; the General Assembly has adopted resolutions urging action.
  • Cyber warfare: IHL applies to cyber operations in armed conflict (Tallinn Manual 2.0, 2017).
  • Urban warfare: the Political Declaration on Explosive Weapons in Populated Areas (2022).
  • Non-State armed groups and "mixed" conflicts; protection of humanitarian workers and journalists.
  • Starvation as a method of warfare: prohibited; criminalised in non-international conflicts by a 2019 amendment to the Rome Statute.

Revision points

  • Hague law vs Geneva law; GCs 1949 + APs 1977; Common Art. 3.
  • Five principles: distinction, proportionality, precaution, necessity, humanity.
  • Martens Clause; Tadić (1995) on customary rules in NIACs.
  • New issues: autonomous weapons, cyber, explosive weapons in cities.
Syllabus unit 11 · Asked 9 times · 2014–2023

Answer writing: Fundamental principles of international humanitarian law

Decode the demand. Questions ask for core principles and recent developments. Name the principles and apply them to a current conflict neutrally.

Open with

IHL does not ask whether a war is lawful but how it is fought; its principles bind all parties equally.

Answer frame

  1. Sources: Hague and Geneva law
  2. Core principles
  3. International vs non-international conflicts (common Art. 3)
  4. Enforcement: ICRC, ICC, State courts
  5. Contemporary challenges: drones, cyber, urban warfare

Concept in brief

International humanitarian law limits the means and methods of war and protects people out of the fight. Its core principles are distinction, proportionality, precaution, military necessity and humanity, and the Martens Clause. The Geneva Conventions of 1949 and their 1977 Protocols, with common Article 3 for non-international conflicts, are the main instruments. India implements them through the Geneva Conventions Act, 1960.

Key provisions

  • Hague Conventions 1899/1907
  • Geneva Conventions I–IV 1949, AP I and II 1977
  • Geneva Conventions Act, 1960

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Prosecutor v. Tadić (ICTY 1995): Armed conflict definition; war crimes in non-international conflicts.
  • Legality of Nuclear Weapons (1996): Cardinal principles of IHL.
  • Rev. Mons. Sebastiao Francisco Xavier dos Remedios Monteiro v. State of Goa (1970): Geneva Conventions not directly enforceable without implementation.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • ICRC customary IHL study (2005)
  • Autonomous weapons debates at the CCW
  • India: Geneva Conventions Act, 1960

Critical lens

Compliance is weak where accountability is absent.

Close with

Humanity in war survives only through enforcement and education.
