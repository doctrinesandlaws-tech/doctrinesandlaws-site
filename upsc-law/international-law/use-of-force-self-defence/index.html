The prohibition

Art. 2(4), UN Charter: members shall refrain from the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any State, or in any manner inconsistent with the UN's purposes. The prohibition is customary and widely regarded as jus cogens.

Two Charter exceptions: self-defence (Art. 51) and Security Council authorisation under Chapter VII (Art. 42).

Aggression

  • GA Resolution 3314 (1974) defines aggression and lists acts: invasion, bombardment, blockade, sending armed bands (Art. 3(g)).
  • Rome Statute, Art. 8bis (Kampala amendments, 2010; ICC jurisdiction activated in 2018): the crime of aggression by leaders, for manifest violations of the Charter.

Self-defence (Art. 51)

  • Armed attack is required. Military and Paramilitary Activities in and against Nicaragua (Nicaragua v. US) (ICJ, 1986): only the "most grave forms" of force amount to armed attack; supplying weapons is not armed attack; collective self-defence needs a request from the victim State.
  • Necessity and proportionality (customary): the Caroline formula (1837) required a necessity that is "instant, overwhelming, leaving no choice of means, and no moment for deliberation"; Oil Platforms (Iran v. US) (2003).
  • Reporting to the Security Council; the right lasts until the Council takes measures.
  • Anticipatory self-defence: against an imminent attack is widely accepted; pre-emptive self-defence against non-imminent threats (the 2002 US "Bush doctrine") is not.
  • Non-State actors: Legal Consequences of the Construction of a Wall (ICJ, 2004) and Armed Activities (DRC v. Uganda) (2005) suggested self-defence applies to attacks attributable to a State. After 9/11, Security Council resolutions 1368 and 1373 (2001) recognised self-defence in the context of terrorist attacks; the "unwilling or unable" test is invoked by some States but contested.

Intervention

  • Non-intervention (Art. 2(7); Nicaragua): coercion in matters a State may decide freely (choice of political system, foreign policy) is prohibited.
  • Intervention by invitation of the lawful government is generally permitted, but not to suppress self-determination or in a full civil war.
  • Humanitarian intervention without Council authorisation (NATO in Kosovo, 1999) remains legally contested; the Independent International Commission on Kosovo called it "illegal but legitimate".
  • Responsibility to Protect (R2P), 2005 World Summit Outcome (paras. 138–139): States must protect their populations from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity; the international community may act collectively through the Security Council. First invoked for military action in Libya (resolution 1973, 2011).
  • Protection of nationals abroad (Entebbe, 1976) is invoked but contested.

Collective security

Chapter VII: Art. 39 determination of a threat to peace, Art. 41 sanctions, Art. 42 military measures. Authorisations: Korea (1950, during the Soviet boycott), Iraq–Kuwait (resolution 678, 1990), Libya (1973, 2011).

Contemporary issues

  • Russia–Ukraine (2022): the General Assembly (resolution ES-11/1) demanded Russia's withdrawal; the ICJ ordered provisional measures in Allegations of Genocide (2022).
  • Cyber operations that cause physical damage can amount to use of force (Tallinn Manual 2.0).
  • Drones and targeted killing strain the necessity and imminence requirements.

Revision points

  • Art. 2(4) + two exceptions (Art. 51, Chapter VII).
  • Nicaragua (1986): gravity, armed attack, collective self-defence request.
  • Caroline (necessity, proportionality); anticipatory vs pre-emptive.
  • Kosovo (1999) and R2P (2005; Libya 2011).
Syllabus unit 10 · Asked 10 times · 2013–2026

Answer writing: Lawful recourse to force

Decode the demand. Questions ask about aggression, self-defence or intervention, often combined. Use Art. 2(4) and Art. 51 as anchors and test any example against necessity and proportionality.

Open with

The UN Charter turned the use of force from a sovereign right into a prohibited act, with narrow exceptions.

Answer frame

  1. Prohibition (Art. 2(4)) and aggression
  2. Self-defence: armed attack, necessity, proportionality, reporting
  3. Collective security (Ch. VII)
  4. Intervention: forms and legality, R2P
  5. Contemporary practice

Concept in brief

Art. 2(4) prohibits the threat or use of force. Exceptions are Chapter VII action and self-defence against an armed attack (Art. 51), which must be necessary and proportionate and reported to the Security Council. Intervention (dictatorial interference) is generally unlawful; humanitarian intervention and Responsibility to Protect remain contested outside Council authorisation.

Key provisions

  • UN Charter Arts. 2(4), 2(7), 39–51
  • GA Res. 3314 (1974) Definition of Aggression
  • 2005 World Summit Outcome, paras. 138–139 (R2P)

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Caroline incident (1837): Necessity 'instant, overwhelming' and proportionality.
  • Corfu Channel (1949): Right of intervention rejected.
  • Nicaragua v. USA (1986): Armed attack threshold; non-intervention customary.
  • Oil Platforms (Iran v. USA) (2003): Self-defence must be necessary and proportionate.
  • Armed Activities (DRC v. Uganda) (2005): No self-defence without attribution of armed attack.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Russia–Ukraine: ICJ provisional measures of March 2022
  • Anticipatory and pre-emptive self-defence debates (Bush doctrine)
  • Self-defence against non-State actors and terrorist infrastructure in State practice, including India's stated rationale in 2025

Critical lens

State practice is stretching self-defence; the law's credibility depends on consistent application.

Close with

Self-defence must stay a shield against attack, not a sword for policy.
