The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT), 1969

In force 1980. India is not a party, but Indian courts and the government treat most of its rules as customary international law. A treaty is an international agreement concluded between States in written form and governed by international law (Art. 2(1)(a)).

Formation

  1. Capacity and full powers (Art. 7): heads of State and government and foreign ministers do not need full powers.
  2. Adoption and authentication of the text (Arts. 9–10).
  3. Consent to be bound (Arts. 11–17): signature, exchange of instruments, ratification, acceptance, approval or accession.
  4. Before entry into force, a signatory must not defeat the treaty's object and purpose (Art. 18).
  5. Entry into force (Art. 24) and registration with the UN Secretariat (Art. 102, UN Charter).

Reservations (Arts. 19–23)

  • A reservation is a unilateral statement that excludes or modifies the legal effect of certain provisions.
  • Not allowed if the treaty prohibits it or if it is incompatible with the object and purpose (Art. 19).
  • Reservations to the Genocide Convention (ICJ advisory opinion, 1951): introduced the compatibility test and flexible acceptance and objection.
  • Acceptance and objection (Art. 20); effect (Art. 21): an objecting State may still treat the treaty as in force between them minus the reserved provision.
  • Interpretative declarations differ from reservations.
  • Human rights bodies sometimes treat invalid reservations as severable (UN Human Rights Committee, General Comment 24).

Application and interpretation

  • Pacta sunt servanda (Art. 26); internal law no excuse (Art. 27).
  • Non-retroactivity (Art. 28) and territorial scope (Art. 29).
  • Interpretation (Arts. 31–33): ordinary meaning in context and in light of object and purpose, with subsequent agreement and practice; travaux préparatoires as a supplementary means.
  • Third States: a treaty creates neither obligations nor rights for a third State without its consent (Art. 34), save for custom.

Invalidity (Arts. 46–53)

  • Violation of internal law on competence, if manifest and of fundamental importance (Art. 46).
  • Error (Art. 48), fraud (Art. 49), corruption of a representative (Art. 50).
  • Coercion of a representative (Art. 51) or of the State by the threat or use of force (Art. 52).
  • Conflict with a peremptory norm (jus cogens) existing at the time (Art. 53) or emerging later (Art. 64).

Termination and suspension (Arts. 54–64)

  • By the treaty's own terms or by consent of all parties (Art. 54).
  • Denunciation where the treaty is silent only if intended or implied by its nature (Art. 56).
  • Material breach (Art. 60): entitles the other party to terminate or suspend; not for humanitarian provisions protecting persons (Art. 60(5)).
  • Supervening impossibility of performance (Art. 61).
  • Fundamental change of circumstances (rebus sic stantibus, Art. 62): narrowly applied. Fisheries Jurisdiction (UK v. Iceland) (1973) and Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros (Hungary v. Slovakia) (ICJ, 1997) rejected the plea; it never applies to boundary treaties.
  • Severance of diplomatic relations does not terminate treaties (Art. 63).
  • Procedure (Arts. 65–68): notification and peaceful settlement.

Contemporary example

In April 2025 India announced that it would hold the Indus Waters Treaty (1960) "in abeyance". The treaty has no clause for unilateral suspension, so the move raises the questions VCLT Arts. 60–62 address (breach, impossibility, fundamental change) and the role of the treaty's own dispute machinery (neutral expert and Court of Arbitration). Present both sides in an answer.

Revision points

  • Formation: full powers (Art. 7), Art. 18 obligation, consent modes.
  • Reservations: Genocide AO (1951) compatibility test; Arts. 19–21.
  • Invalidity: Arts. 46–53; jus cogens (53, 64).
  • Termination: Arts. 60–62; Gabčíkovo (1997).
Syllabus unit 7 · Asked 13 times · 2013–2025

Answer writing: Treaties

Decode the demand. Expect formation and reservations, pacta sunt servanda and rebus sic stantibus. Use VCLT articles as headings.

Open with

Treaties are the main source of modern international law, and the VCLT 1969 is the 'treaty on treaties'.

Answer frame

  1. Formation and consent
  2. Reservations (Genocide AO, Arts. 19–23)
  3. Interpretation (Arts. 31–33)
  4. Invalidity and jus cogens
  5. Termination and suspension

Concept in brief

The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, 1969 governs formation (consent, signature, ratification), reservations, interpretation in good faith, invalidity (including jus cogens) and termination. Pacta sunt servanda is the core rule; rebus sic stantibus allows exit only on a fundamental, unforeseen change.

Key provisions

  • VCLT 1969: Arts. 2, 11–18, 19–23, 26, 27, 31–33, 46–53, 60–62, 64

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Reservations to the Genocide Convention (1951): Compatibility test for reservations.
  • Fisheries Jurisdiction (UK v. Iceland) (1973): Fundamental change of circumstances narrowly applied.
  • Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros (1997): Necessity and changed circumstances do not end a treaty.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • India's April 2025 decision to hold the Indus Waters Treaty in abeyance, a live debate on suspension and changed circumstances
  • Political science: treaty compliance and reciprocity

Critical lens

Rebus sic stantibus is narrow by design; unilateral suspension is legally contested.

Close with

Pacta sunt servanda keeps the system stable; flexibility must come through negotiated change.
