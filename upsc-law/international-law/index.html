Nature and Definition of International Law

Decode the demand. Two set questions recur: is international law 'law', and define it with its sources. Present Austin's objection, then the rebuttals.

Open with

Whether international law is 'law' depends on the definition of law: by Austin's command theory it fails; by Hart's or the practice of States it succeeds.

Answer frame

  1. Definitions (Oppenheim, Starke)
  2. The Austinian objection
  3. Rebuttals: Hart, Kelsen, consent and practice
  4. Sources (Art. 38 ICJ Statute)
  5. Enforcement realities

Concept in brief

International law is the body of rules that States (and, increasingly, international organisations and individuals) treat as binding in their relations. Austin denied it was 'law' for lack of a sovereign; the modern answer points to consistent State practice, treaty compliance, dispute settlement and enforcement through collective and reciprocal measures. Sources are listed in Art. 38(1) of the ICJ Statute.

Key provisions

  • ICJ Statute, Art. 38(1)
  • UN Charter, Art. 2
  • Constitution, Art. 51

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • S.S. Lotus (France v. Turkey) (PCIJ 1927): States are free unless a prohibitive rule exists.
  • North Sea Continental Shelf (1969): Custom needs State practice and opinio juris.
  • Asylum (Colombia v. Peru) (1950): Regional custom must be proved by the party relying on it.
  • Military and Paramilitary Activities (Nicaragua v. USA) (1986): Customary law exists alongside treaty law.
  • The Paquete Habana (1900, US SC): International law is part of our law.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Jurisprudence: Hart's primary and secondary rules; Kelsen's grundnorm
  • International relations: realism vs liberal institutionalism
  • Henkin's observation that most States obey most rules most of the time

Critical lens

Weak enforcement against great powers (vetoes, non-appearance) remains the strongest objection.

Close with

International law is a horizontal, consent-based legal order; its weakness is enforcement, not its legal character.

Relationship between International Law and Municipal Law

Decode the demand. Questions ask for theories (monism, dualism, coordination) and Indian practice. Keep the theory brief and give the Indian position in depth.

Open with

How a State receives international law is decided by its own constitution; the theories only explain the choices.

Answer frame

  1. Monism, dualism, coordination theories
  2. UK and US practice
  3. Indian constitutional scheme (Arts. 51(c), 73, 253)
  4. Judicial approach in India (Gramophone, Vishaka, NALSA)
  5. Assessment

Concept in brief

Monists see one legal order with international law supreme; dualists see two systems, requiring transformation. Practice is mixed: the UK incorporates custom, while treaties need legislation. India is dualist for treaties (Art. 253), but courts read international law into domestic law where it is consistent and fills gaps.

Key provisions

  • Constitution, Arts. 51(c), 73, 246, 253
  • VCLT 1969, Art. 27

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Trendtex Trading v. Central Bank of Nigeria (1977): Doctrine of incorporation for custom.
  • Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel v. Union of India (1969): Treaty implementation and Art. 253.
  • Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin (1980): Covenant rights apply only through municipal law, but guide interpretation.
  • Gramophone Co. of India v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey (1984): Comity and incorporation where not in conflict with statute.
  • Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997): CEDAW used to fill a legislative gap.
  • National Legal Services Authority v. Union of India (2014): International norms (Yogyakarta Principles) read into Arts. 14, 19, 21.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Kelsen (monism) vs Triepel and Anzilotti (dualism)
  • Comparative: Art. 25 German Basic Law; US Supremacy Clause
  • Data: India's treaty practice without parliamentary approval

Critical lens

Judicial incorporation fills gaps but can bypass Parliament's treaty role.

Close with

India is dualist in form and harmonising in practice.

State Recognition and State Succession

Decode the demand. Expect theories of recognition and the difference between recognition and succession. Use modern examples.

Open with

Recognition is a political act with legal consequences; succession concerns what happens to rights and obligations when sovereignty changes.

Answer frame

  1. Recognition: theories and modes (de jure, de facto, express, implied)
  2. Doctrines (Estrada, Stimson, Tobar)
  3. Succession: treaties, property, debts
  4. Recognition vs succession compared
  5. Contemporary cases

Concept in brief

Recognition is the acknowledgement by one State of another State or government. The constitutive theory says recognition creates legal personality; the declaratory theory (Montevideo Convention, Art. 3) says it only confirms existing statehood. Succession concerns which rights and duties pass when sovereignty changes; Vienna Conventions of 1978 and 1983 apply the clean-slate rule to newly independent States, with exceptions for boundaries.

Key provisions

  • Montevideo Convention, 1933, Arts. 1, 3
  • Vienna Convention on Succession of States in respect of Treaties, 1978
  • Vienna Convention (State Property, Archives and Debts), 1983
  • Indian Independence (International Arrangements) Order, 1947

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Tinoco Arbitration (UK v. Costa Rica) (1923): Effective control, not recognition, decides who governs.
  • Luther v. Sagor (1921): Recognition is retroactive in effect.
  • Burkina Faso/Mali Frontier Dispute (1986): Uti possidetis juris for colonial boundaries.
  • Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros (Hungary/Slovakia) (1997): Territorial treaties survive succession.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Kosovo Advisory Opinion (2010)
  • The Taliban government (2021) and non-recognition
  • India's own succession in 1947

Critical lens

Recognition remains discretionary and politicised; succession rules are only partly codified.

Close with

The declaratory view dominates doctrine, but the constitutive effect shows in practice.

Law of the sea

Decode the demand. Very frequently asked. Questions test zones and passage regimes, often through a case or a current choke-point. Draw a zone diagram.

Open with

UNCLOS 1982, the 'constitution for the oceans', balances coastal-State control with freedom of navigation through graded maritime zones.

Answer frame

  1. Baselines and internal waters
  2. Territorial sea and innocent passage (Arts. 17–19)
  3. Straits: transit passage (Arts. 37–44)
  4. Contiguous zone, EEZ, continental shelf
  5. Disputes and current issues

Concept in brief

UNCLOS 1982 codifies the zones: territorial sea up to 12 nm with innocent passage, contiguous zone up to 24 nm, an EEZ up to 200 nm and the continental shelf. Straits used for international navigation carry transit passage, which cannot be suspended. The high seas are free, and the deep seabed is the common heritage of mankind. India implements the regime through the Maritime Zones Act, 1976.

Key provisions

  • UNCLOS 1982: Arts. 3, 17–19, 33, 37–44, 55–57, 76, 86–87, 136
  • Territorial Waters, Continental Shelf, EEZ and Other Maritime Zones Act, 1976

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Corfu Channel (UK v. Albania) (1949): Warships have innocent passage through international straits; coastal State must warn of known dangers.
  • Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries (1951): Straight baselines on a deeply indented coast.
  • North Sea Continental Shelf (1969): Shelf is natural prolongation; equitable delimitation.
  • Bay of Bengal Maritime Boundary (Bangladesh v. India) (PCA 2014): Equitable delimitation between neighbours.
  • South China Sea Arbitration (Philippines v. China) (2016): Historic rights claims incompatible with UNCLOS zones.
  • Enrica Lexie (Italy v. India) (PCA 2020): Marines entitled to immunity; India entitled to compensation.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Grotius's Mare Liberum vs Selden's Mare Clausum
  • Strait of Hormuz carries about a fifth of the world's seaborne oil
  • Iran (signed, not ratified) and the US (non-party) outside UNCLOS
  • India's 1976 Maritime Zones Act and Indian Ocean security

Draw this

Baseline → 12 nm territorial sea → 24 nm contiguous zone → 200 nm EEZ → continental shelf (up to 350 nm) → high seas

Critical lens

Non-ratifying States and militarisation of choke-points test UNCLOS's universality.

Close with

Freedom of navigation through straits is a shared interest that only a rule-based order can protect.

Individuals

Decode the demand. Questions test nationality (modes, nationality vs domicile, statelessness) and enforcement of human rights. Give mechanisms, not just rights.

Open with

The individual has moved from an object to a subject of international law, through nationality, human rights and individual criminal responsibility.

Answer frame

  1. Nationality: modes, genuine link (Nottebohm)
  2. Nationality vs domicile
  3. Statelessness conventions
  4. Human rights: instruments and enforcement mechanisms
  5. Indian position

Concept in brief

Individuals are now subjects of international law in significant ways: nationality links them to a State, human rights treaties give them rights and sometimes a direct petition, and international criminal law makes them responsible. Nationality differs from domicile, which is a private-law link of permanent residence. Enforcement works through treaty bodies, the UN Human Rights Council and its Universal Periodic Review, and regional courts.

Key provisions

  • UDHR 1948
  • ICCPR and ICESCR 1966, First Optional Protocol to ICCPR
  • 1954 and 1961 statelessness conventions
  • Citizenship Act, 1955
  • Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Nottebohm (Liechtenstein v. Guatemala) (1955): Genuine link needed for diplomatic protection.
  • Barcelona Traction (1970): Obligations erga omnes.
  • LaGrand (Germany v. USA) (2001): Individual rights under the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations; provisional measures binding.
  • Jadhav (India v. Pakistan) (2019): Denial of consular access breached Art. 36 VCCR.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • UN treaty bodies, Universal Periodic Review, special rapporteurs
  • Regional courts (ECtHR, Inter-American Court)
  • Assam NRC and statelessness concerns

Critical lens

Enforcement still depends on State consent and political will.

Close with

Human rights are universal in claim but local in enforcement; national institutions are the first line.

Territorial jurisdiction of States, Extradition, and Asylum

Decode the demand. Questions ask about jurisdiction principles, extradition and asylum, often 'is asylum a right?'. Distinguish the right to seek from the right to receive.

Open with

Jurisdiction marks the reach of a State's law; extradition and asylum mark the limits of that reach across borders.

Answer frame

  1. Bases of jurisdiction
  2. Extradition principles and Indian law
  3. Asylum: territorial vs diplomatic
  4. Is asylum a right? (UDHR Art. 14, non-refoulement)
  5. Indian practice

Concept in brief

A State has jurisdiction on territorial, nationality, protective, passive-personality and universality grounds. Extradition rests on treaties and reciprocity, with double criminality, speciality and the political-offence exception. Asylum is territorial (granted within the State) or diplomatic (in embassies, accepted only regionally). The UDHR gives a right to seek asylum, not a right to be granted it; non-refoulement protects refugees.

Key provisions

  • Extradition Act, 1962
  • UDHR Art. 14
  • Refugee Convention 1951, Art. 33 (India not a party)

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • S.S. Lotus (PCIJ 1927): Objective territorial jurisdiction.
  • Asylum (Colombia v. Peru) (1950): Diplomatic asylum is not general custom.
  • Savarkar (France v. Great Britain) (PCA 1911): No duty to return a fugitive irregularly recaptured.
  • Abu Salem v. State of Maharashtra (2010): Rule of speciality binds Indian prosecutors.
  • NHRC v. State of Arunachal Pradesh (1996): Chakma refugees protected under Art. 21.
  • Mohammad Salimullah v. Union of India (2021): Non-refoulement claims of Rohingya; no interim stay on deportation.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • India hosts Tibetan, Sri Lankan Tamil and other refugees without a refugee statute
  • Comparative: EU Common European Asylum System
  • Recent extradition debates over fugitive economic offenders

Critical lens

Without a national refugee law, protection in India depends on executive discretion.

Close with

Asylum remains a State's right to grant, softened by non-refoulement's duty not to return.

Syllabus unit 15 · Asked 9 times · 2015–2026

Protection and Improvement of the Human Environment: International Efforts

Decode the demand. Questions trace milestones (Stockholm → Rio → Kyoto → Paris) and principles. A timeline earns marks.

Open with

International environmental law grew from Stockholm's warning (1972) into a dense web of principles and treaties, now reinforced by the ICJ's 2025 climate opinion.

Answer frame

  1. Milestones before Kyoto
  2. Kyoto Protocol: targets and mechanisms
  3. After Kyoto: Copenhagen, Paris and the COPs
  4. Principles: precaution, CBDR-RC, polluter pays
  5. Judicial turn: ITLOS 2024, ICJ 2025

Concept in brief

Stockholm (1972) launched international environmental law; Rio (1992) gave sustainable development, precaution (Principle 15) and polluter pays (Principle 16). The UNFCCC (1992), Kyoto Protocol (1997, binding targets for Annex I countries with market mechanisms) and Paris Agreement (2015, NDCs) form the climate regime. The ICJ's advisory opinion of July 2025 confirmed binding State obligations on climate change.

Key provisions

  • Stockholm Declaration 1972
  • Rio Declaration 1992
  • UNFCCC 1992, Kyoto 1997, Paris 2015
  • Montreal Protocol 1987 (Kigali 2016)
  • CBD 1992

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Trail Smelter (US v. Canada) (1941): No State may allow its territory to cause serious transboundary harm.
  • Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros (1997): Sustainable development recognised.
  • Pulp Mills (Argentina v. Uruguay) (2010): EIA is a customary obligation.
  • Obligations of States in respect of Climate Change (ICJ AO, 23 July 2025): Unanimous: States owe binding duties to prevent significant climate harm.
  • Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996): Precaution and polluter pays are part of Indian law.
  • M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India (2024): Right against the adverse effects of climate change under Arts. 14 and 21.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • India's net-zero 2070 target and Mission LiFE
  • Climate finance gaps
  • Vellore and Ranjitsinh as the domestic counterpart

Draw this

Stockholm 1972 → Brundtland 1987 → Rio 1992 → Kyoto 1997 → Copenhagen 2009 → Paris 2015 → ICJ AO 2025

Critical lens

Voluntary pledges under Paris lack teeth; equity between North and South remains unresolved.

Close with

Climate law is moving from pledges to obligations; implementation and finance will decide its success.
