UNCLOS, 1982

The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (in force 1994; India ratified in 1995) is the "constitution of the oceans". India's domestic law is the Territorial Waters, Continental Shelf, Exclusive Economic Zone and other Maritime Zones Act, 1976.

Baselines

  • Normal baseline: the low-water line along the coast (Art. 5).
  • Straight baselines: for deeply indented or fringed coasts (Art. 7); upheld in Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries (ICJ, 1951).
  • Waters landward of the baseline are internal waters: full sovereignty, no right of innocent passage.

Maritime zones

ZoneExtent from baselineCoastal State's rights
Territorial seaUp to 12 nm (Art. 3)Sovereignty, subject to innocent passage
Contiguous zoneUp to 24 nm (Art. 33)Control to prevent and punish breaches of customs, fiscal, immigration and sanitary laws
Exclusive economic zoneUp to 200 nm (Arts. 55–57)Sovereign rights over living and non-living resources, energy; jurisdiction over artificial islands, research, environment
Continental shelfNatural prolongation to the outer edge of the margin, at least 200 nm; ceiling 350 nm or 100 nm beyond the 2,500 m isobath (Art. 76)Sovereign rights to explore and exploit seabed resources
High seasBeyond national jurisdictionFreedoms of navigation, overflight, fishing, cables, research (Art. 87)

Key rules

  • Innocent passage (Arts. 17–19): passage is innocent so long as it is not prejudicial to peace, good order or security; Art. 19(2) lists non-innocent activities. Corfu Channel (ICJ, 1949): warships have a right of innocent passage through international straits.
  • Transit passage through straits used for international navigation (Art. 38) cannot be suspended.
  • Archipelagic States (Part IV) may draw archipelagic baselines.
  • Continental shelf rights are inherent and do not depend on occupation or proclamation (North Sea Continental Shelf, 1969; Truman Proclamation, 1945).
  • Flag State jurisdiction on the high seas is exclusive (Art. 92); exceptions include piracy (Art. 101) with universal jurisdiction (Art. 105), the slave trade, unauthorised broadcasting and hot pursuit (Art. 111).
  • The Area (deep seabed beyond national jurisdiction) is the "common heritage of mankind" (Part XI), managed by the International Seabed Authority.

Dispute settlement (Part XV)

Compulsory procedures: ITLOS, the ICJ, or arbitration under Annex VII.

  • South China Sea Arbitration (Philippines v. China) (2016): historic rights claims inside the "nine-dash line" have no legal basis under UNCLOS.
  • Bay of Bengal Maritime Boundary Arbitration (Bangladesh v. India) (2014): the tribunal used equidistance, adjusted to avoid cutting Bangladesh off from the sea; Bangladesh received about 19,467 sq km of the roughly 25,602 sq km in dispute.
  • Enrica Lexie (Italy v. India) (PCA, 2020): India had concurrent jurisdiction over the incident, but the Italian marines enjoyed functional immunity; India was entitled to compensation for loss of life and damage.

New developments

  • BBNJ Agreement (High Seas Treaty, 2023) on marine biodiversity beyond national jurisdiction: marine protected areas, sharing of benefits from marine genetic resources, environmental impact assessments. It entered into force in January 2026.
  • ITLOS advisory opinion on climate change (2024): greenhouse gas emissions are "pollution of the marine environment" under UNCLOS; States must take all necessary measures to reduce them.
  • Sea-level rise and the stability of baselines are on the ILC's agenda.

Revision points

  • 12 / 24 / 200 nm; shelf up to 350 nm.
  • Innocent passage (Art. 19), transit passage (Art. 38), hot pursuit (Art. 111), piracy (Arts. 101, 105).
  • Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries (baselines); South China Sea (2016); Enrica Lexie (2020); BBNJ (in force 2026).
Syllabus unit 4 · Asked 20 times · 2013–2026

Answer writing: Law of the sea

Decode the demand. Very frequently asked. Questions test zones and passage regimes, often through a case or a current choke-point. Draw a zone diagram.

Open with

UNCLOS 1982, the 'constitution for the oceans', balances coastal-State control with freedom of navigation through graded maritime zones.

Answer frame

  1. Baselines and internal waters
  2. Territorial sea and innocent passage (Arts. 17–19)
  3. Straits: transit passage (Arts. 37–44)
  4. Contiguous zone, EEZ, continental shelf
  5. Disputes and current issues

Concept in brief

UNCLOS 1982 codifies the zones: territorial sea up to 12 nm with innocent passage, contiguous zone up to 24 nm, an EEZ up to 200 nm and the continental shelf. Straits used for international navigation carry transit passage, which cannot be suspended. The high seas are free, and the deep seabed is the common heritage of mankind. India implements the regime through the Maritime Zones Act, 1976.

Key provisions

  • UNCLOS 1982: Arts. 3, 17–19, 33, 37–44, 55–57, 76, 86–87, 136
  • Territorial Waters, Continental Shelf, EEZ and Other Maritime Zones Act, 1976

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Corfu Channel (UK v. Albania) (1949): Warships have innocent passage through international straits; coastal State must warn of known dangers.
  • Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries (1951): Straight baselines on a deeply indented coast.
  • North Sea Continental Shelf (1969): Shelf is natural prolongation; equitable delimitation.
  • Bay of Bengal Maritime Boundary (Bangladesh v. India) (PCA 2014): Equitable delimitation between neighbours.
  • South China Sea Arbitration (Philippines v. China) (2016): Historic rights claims incompatible with UNCLOS zones.
  • Enrica Lexie (Italy v. India) (PCA 2020): Marines entitled to immunity; India entitled to compensation.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Grotius's Mare Liberum vs Selden's Mare Clausum
  • Strait of Hormuz carries about a fifth of the world's seaborne oil
  • Iran (signed, not ratified) and the US (non-party) outside UNCLOS
  • India's 1976 Maritime Zones Act and Indian Ocean security

Draw this

Baseline → 12 nm territorial sea → 24 nm contiguous zone → 200 nm EEZ → continental shelf (up to 350 nm) → high seas

Critical lens

Non-ratifying States and militarisation of choke-points test UNCLOS's universality.

Close with

Freedom of navigation through straits is a shared interest that only a rule-based order can protect.
