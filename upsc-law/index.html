The answer method behind every unit

High-scoring Law optional answers share a pattern: they answer the exact question, build on sections and the ratio of cases, add a layer from outside the bare law, and end by looking forward. Each unit on this desk is written to that pattern.

  1. Decode the demand. Read the directive word first. Discuss wants both sides; critically examine or evaluate wants a verdict after testing the claim; elucidate or explain wants clarity with illustrations; comment wants a reasoned opinion. A quoted statement is a thesis to test, not to repeat. Count the parts of the question and answer every one.
  2. Budget words by marks. About 150 words for 10 marks, 200 for 15 and 250 for 20.
  3. Open precisely. One or two lines: a definition, the governing Article or section, or a sharp framing of the tension. No history lessons.
  4. Build the body under headings. Each heading makes one point and backs it with a provision and a case. Cite the ratio in a line; skip the facts unless the question is about them.
  5. Solve problems with IRAC. Issue, Rule (section plus case), Application to the facts, Conclusion. Commit to an answer.
  6. Add the interdisciplinary edge. Law Commission and committee reports, comparative law, jurisprudence, economics, data and current developments: one or two per answer, chosen for relevance.
  7. Show a critical lens. One honest limitation or counter-view shows judgement.
  8. Draw when it helps. A flowchart, timeline, table or zone diagram in a 15- or 20-mark answer saves words.
  9. Close forward. End with a way forward or a constitutional value, not a summary.

Sources and method

Questions are taken from the official UPSC question papers. Notes are written by Doctrines & Laws from primary sources: the Constitution of India, Central Acts on India Code, treaties and the judgments themselves. For depth, pair them with standard texts such as M.P. Jain (Indian Constitutional Law), Malcolm Shaw (International Law), Ratanlal & Dhirajlal (Law of Crimes and Law of Torts) and Pollock & Mulla (Indian Contract Act).
