What makes PIL different

Public interest litigation (PIL) is litigation for the enforcement of public or group interests, especially of those who cannot reach courts themselves. It departs from the adversarial model in three ways:

  1. Standing: any person acting bona fide may sue for others.
  2. Procedure: letters, newspaper reports and postcards can be treated as petitions; courts appoint commissions to gather facts.
  3. Remedies: continuing mandamus, guidelines, monitoring committees and compensation.

Phases of PIL in India

  1. Origins (late 1970s–1980s): rights of the poor - Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979): undertrials and speedy trial. - S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981): relaxed standing (Bhagwati J.). - People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982, Asiad workers): forced labour includes paying below minimum wage. - Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984): bonded labour; letter petitions. - Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985): pavement dwellers.
  2. Expansion (1990s): environment and governance - M.C. Mehta cases (oleum gas, Ganga, vehicular pollution, Taj Trapezium). - Vineet Narain v. Union of India (1998): CBI independence. - Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997): workplace sexual harassment guidelines. - T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v. Union of India (1995 onwards): forests under continuing mandamus.
  3. Consolidation and caution (2000s–present) - PUCL v. Union of India (2001 onwards): right to food; mid-day meals. - Prakash Singh v. Union of India (2006): police reforms. - Common Cause v. Union of India (2018): living wills. - Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (2024): the electoral bond scheme struck down.

Limits and safeguards

  • BALCO Employees' Union v. Union of India (2002): no PIL against economic policy.
  • State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010): guidelines to verify credentials and bona fides of petitioners; costs on frivolous PILs.
  • Tehseen Poonawalla v. Union of India (2018): PIL is not a tool for political or personal vendetta.
  • Supreme Court's PIL guidelines (1988, revised) list categories that are entertained (bonded labour, neglected children, atrocities, environment) and exclude landlord–tenant and service matters.

Critique

  • For: access to justice for the marginalised; accountability of the executive; filling legislative vacuums.
  • Against: judicial overreach into policy; courts lack expertise and implementation capacity; "publicity interest litigation"; burden on the docket; continuing mandamus can make courts administrators.
  • Balance: courts should use PIL to enforce rights, structure deliberation and require the executive to act, rather than to run institutions themselves.

Revision points

  • Three departures: standing, procedure, remedies.
  • Phases: rights of the poor → environment and governance → consolidation.
  • Limits: BALCO (2002), Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010).
  • Critique: overreach vs access.
Syllabus unit 1 · Asked 9 times · 2013–2025

Answer writing: Public Interest Litigation

Decode the demand. Questions test PIL's evolution, uses and misuse.

Open with

PIL turned the Supreme Court into a court for the poor; its legitimacy depends on staying public, not personal.

Answer frame

  1. Origin and features
  2. Achievements (bonded labour, environment, prisoners)
  3. Techniques: epistolary jurisdiction, continuing mandamus
  4. Misuse and guidelines
  5. Way forward

Concept in brief

PIL lets any public-spirited person approach the Supreme Court or High Courts on behalf of those who cannot. It relaxed standing and procedure (letters treated as petitions) and produced continuing mandamus. Courts now filter misuse through guidelines.

Key provisions

  • Arts. 32, 226, 39A
  • SC guidelines on letter petitions (1988, revised)

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981): Liberal standing.
  • Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984): Bonded labour; epistolary jurisdiction.
  • People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982): Asiad workers.
  • Vineet Narain v. Union of India (1998): Continuing mandamus over investigations.
  • State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010): Guidelines to prevent PIL misuse.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Upendra Baxi's 'social action litigation'
  • Comparative: South African socio-economic rights litigation

Critical lens

PIL risks judicial governance and publicity-driven petitions.

Close with

PIL should remain a remedy for the voiceless, not a shortcut for the powerful.

Previous-year questions (9)
  1. 2025"Public interest litigation is a tool to protect fundamental rights of persons or groups of persons who are unable to approach the court due to poverty or social and economic conditions." Critically analyse this statement.
  2. 2024"There is, in recent years, a feeling which is not without any foundation that 'public interest litigation' is now tending to become 'publicity interest litigation' or 'private interest litigation', and has a tendency to be counter-productive." Examine the statement critically.
  3. 2023"The Constitutional courts through their judicial activism have made a substantial contribution in protecting women against exploitation, using Public Interest Litigation as a tool for securing their Constitutional rights." Explain with leading case laws.
  4. 2020"The Supreme Court of India in public interest litigation cases relating to the COVID-19 pandemic adopted a judicial policy of non-interference into the policies of the Government." Elucidate with the help of decided cases.
  5. 2019"Public interest litigation is not the pill of all ills, it is the boon of the courts. However, it is also the duty of the court to prevent its misuse." Elucidate.
  6. 2018Public Interest Litigation (PIL) in India, of late, has been used not only to represent the unrepresented and weak but also to advance the interests of others. Comment on the recent trends relating to the application, use and misuse of PIL in India.
  7. 2017"Public interest litigation has played a very crucial role in the protection of the environment in India." Elucidate and illustrate with the help of decided cases.
  8. 2015"PIL writ petition cannot be filed in the Supreme Court under Article 32 of the Indian Constitution only if a question concerning the enforcement of 'Fundamental Rights' is involved." Comment.
  9. 2013In matters such as enforcement of social, economic, cultural or political rights or civil liberties or gender concerns, courts in India have been inclined to apply relaxed rules of standing or litigational competence rather than strict rules of locus. Discuss.
