From MRTP to competition

  • The Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, 1969 aimed at curbing concentration of economic power.
  • The Raghavan Committee (2000) recommended a modern competition law focused on market behaviour, not size.
  • The Competition Act, 2002 (substantive provisions in force from 2009) set up the Competition Commission of India (CCI). Appeals go to the NCLAT (since 2017) and then the Supreme Court.

Three pillars

1. Anti-competitive agreements (s. 3)

  • Agreements causing or likely to cause an appreciable adverse effect on competition (AAEC) in India are void.
  • Horizontal agreements among competitors (s. 3(3)): price fixing, limiting production, market sharing, bid rigging are presumed to cause AAEC (cartels).
  • Vertical agreements (s. 3(4)): tie-in, exclusive supply or distribution, refusal to deal, resale price maintenance, judged under the rule of reason (factors in s. 19(3)).
  • Penalties: up to 10% of average turnover, or for cartels up to three times profit or 10% of turnover for each year, whichever is higher.
  • Leniency (s. 46) and leniency plus (2023).
  • Cases: the cement cartel (2012, penalties of over ₹6,000 crore, largely upheld); Excel Crop Care v. CCI (2017): penalty on relevant turnover (turnover from the product in question).

2. Abuse of dominant position (s. 4)

  • Dominance itself is not unlawful; abuse is: unfair or discriminatory prices or conditions, limiting production or technical development, denial of market access, leveraging dominance into another market, and supplementary obligations.
  • Factors for dominance (s. 19(4)) and relevant market (s. 19(5)–(7)).
  • CCI v. Google (Android, 2022): penalty of about ₹1,337 crore for abusing dominance through mandatory pre-installation and other restrictions; the NCLAT largely upheld the findings in 2023 (setting aside some directions); further appeals are pending.
  • CCI v. Bharti Airtel (2019): in regulated sectors, the CCI should act after the sector regulator (TRAI) has decided the jurisdictional facts.

3. Combinations (ss. 5–6)

  • Mergers and acquisitions above asset or turnover thresholds need prior CCI approval.
  • The 2023 amendment added a deal value threshold (transactions above ₹2,000 crore with substantial business operations in India) to catch digital acquisitions, cut review timelines (150 days) and introduced settlements and commitments (ss. 48A–48B).

Digital markets

  • The Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance (2022) recommended ex-ante regulation of "systemically important digital intermediaries".
  • A draft Digital Competition Bill (2024) proposed ex-ante obligations for large digital enterprises; after consultation, the government signalled a re-examination, and market studies continue.

Competition and consumer welfare

Competition law protects the competitive process to benefit consumers (price, quality, choice, innovation). It overlaps with consumer protection, sectoral regulation (telecom, electricity, insurance) and IP (s. 3(5) saves reasonable conditions for protecting IP rights).

Revision points

  • MRTP 1969 → Raghavan Committee → Competition Act 2002 (CCI, NCLAT appeals).
  • s. 3 (horizontal presumption; vertical rule of reason), s. 4 abuse, ss. 5–6 combinations.
  • Excel Crop Care (2017) relevant turnover; Bharti Airtel (2019); Google Android (2022).
  • 2023 amendment: deal value threshold, settlements and commitments, leniency plus.
Syllabus unit 4 · Asked 7 times · 2013–2026

Answer writing: Competition Law: Concept, purpose/prospects

Decode the demand. Questions test the shift from MRTP to the Competition Act, and digital markets.

Open with

The Competition Act, 2002 shifted India from controlling big business to protecting competition and consumers.

Answer frame

  1. MRTP Act and its limits (Raghavan Committee)
  2. Anti-competitive agreements
  3. Abuse of dominance
  4. Combinations and the 2023 amendment
  5. Digital markets

Concept in brief

The Competition Act, 2002 replaced the MRTP Act, 1969, shifting the focus from controlling size and monopoly to preventing conduct that harms competition and consumers. It prohibits anti-competitive agreements (s. 3) and abuse of dominance (s. 4), and regulates combinations (ss. 5–6). The 2023 amendment added deal-value thresholds and settlements and commitments.

Key provisions

  • Competition Act, 2002 ss. 3, 4, 5, 6, 18, 19, 26, 27, 32
  • Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • CCI v. Steel Authority of India (2010): Nature of CCI proceedings.
  • Excel Crop Care v. CCI (2017): Penalty on relevant turnover.
  • CCI v. Bharti Airtel (2019): Sectoral regulator first on technical issues.
  • CCI v. Coordination Committee of Artists (2017): Trade associations and boycotts.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Committee on Digital Competition Law (2024) and the draft Digital Competition Bill
  • CCI's Google Android order (2022)

Critical lens

Ex-post enforcement is slow for fast-moving digital markets.

Close with

Consumer welfare remains the touchstone, but digital markets may need ex-ante rules.

Previous-year questions (7)
  1. 2026How does the Indian Competition Act, 2002, reflect a shift from regulating monopoly to protecting the interests of consumers? Discuss. [P-II 5(a), 10 marks]
    How to approach: Discuss. MRTP's size-based approach vs the Competition Act's effects-based approach (ss. 3, 4, 5–6); consumer welfare in the Preamble; 2023 amendments. Example of a consumer-protective CCI order.
  2. 2025"Both horizontal and vertical agreements are included in Section 3 of the Competition Act, 2002, even when horizontal agreements are considered more harmful in comparison to vertical agreements." Discuss.
  3. 2022What do you mean by 'abuse of dominance' and 'abusive conduct' prohibited under the Competition Act, 2002?
  4. 2020"The intellectual property right and competition law generally work in tandem but often become friends in disagreement." Elucidate the above statement by referring to the mandate of the TRIPS Agreement, 1995 and its compliance under the Competition Act, 2002.
  5. 2019"The basic purpose of competition policy and the law is to preserve and promote competition as a means of ensuring efficient allocation of resources in an economy." Elucidate the statement in the light of the new economic scenario in India.
  6. 2017How far is the Competition Act, 2002 an improvement over the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, 1969 (MRTP Act, 1969) with respect to 'abuse of dominant position'? Discuss and explain the relevant statutory provisions.
  7. 2013Competition Law in India has not achieved the results as was expected. Discuss the bottlenecks with which it suffers and suggest the remedies necessary to make it fruitful.
