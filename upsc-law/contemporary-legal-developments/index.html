Public Interest Litigation

Decode the demand. Questions test PIL's evolution, uses and misuse.

Open with

PIL turned the Supreme Court into a court for the poor; its legitimacy depends on staying public, not personal.

Answer frame

  1. Origin and features
  2. Achievements (bonded labour, environment, prisoners)
  3. Techniques: epistolary jurisdiction, continuing mandamus
  4. Misuse and guidelines
  5. Way forward

Concept in brief

PIL lets any public-spirited person approach the Supreme Court or High Courts on behalf of those who cannot. It relaxed standing and procedure (letters treated as petitions) and produced continuing mandamus. Courts now filter misuse through guidelines.

Key provisions

  • Arts. 32, 226, 39A
  • SC guidelines on letter petitions (1988, revised)

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981): Liberal standing.
  • Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984): Bonded labour; epistolary jurisdiction.
  • People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982): Asiad workers.
  • Vineet Narain v. Union of India (1998): Continuing mandamus over investigations.
  • State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010): Guidelines to prevent PIL misuse.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Upendra Baxi's 'social action litigation'
  • Comparative: South African socio-economic rights litigation

Critical lens

PIL risks judicial governance and publicity-driven petitions.

Close with

PIL should remain a remedy for the voiceless, not a shortcut for the powerful.

Intellectual property rights—Concept, types/prospects

Decode the demand. Questions test IPR types and the balance between rights and public interest, especially copyright and patents.

Open with

Intellectual property is a bargain: exclusive rights for creators in return for eventual public access.

Answer frame

  1. Justifications (labour, incentive, personality)
  2. Types and statutes
  3. Limits: fair dealing, compulsory licences, s. 3(d)
  4. Public interest cases
  5. TRIPS and Indian flexibilities

Concept in brief

Intellectual property rights give exclusive rights for a limited time to encourage creation, but are not absolute. Copyright protects expression, not ideas, and is limited by fair dealing (s. 52) and compulsory licences. Patents exclude evergreening (s. 3(d)) and allow compulsory licensing (s. 84). Trade marks and geographical indications protect origin and reputation.

Key provisions

  • Copyright Act, 1957 ss. 13, 14, 31, 31A, 52
  • Patents Act, 1970 ss. 3(d), 84, 92
  • Trade Marks Act, 1999
  • GI of Goods Act, 1999
  • TRIPS Agreement

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • R.G. Anand v. Delux Films (1978): Idea-expression dichotomy.
  • Eastern Book Co. v. D.B. Modak (2008): Modicum of creativity.
  • University of Oxford v. Rameshwari Photocopy Services (Del. HC 2016): Course packs for education within s. 52.
  • Novartis AG v. Union of India (2013): s. 3(d) and enhanced efficacy.
  • Bayer Corp. v. Union of India (Bom. HC 2014): First compulsory licence upheld.
  • Cadila Health Care v. Cadila Pharmaceuticals (2001): Deceptive similarity in drug names.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Doha Declaration on TRIPS and Public Health (2001)
  • WTO TRIPS waiver for COVID vaccines (2022)
  • AI-generated works and authorship

Critical lens

Over-protection harms access to knowledge and medicines; under-protection harms innovation.

Close with

India's model balances innovation with access through calibrated exceptions.

Information Technology Law, including Cyber Laws—Concept, purpose/prospects

Decode the demand. Questions test the IT Act's framework, contraventions vs offences, intermediaries and data protection.

Open with

The IT Act, 2000 began as an e-commerce law and grew into India's cyber-security and speech regulator.

Answer frame

  1. Objectives and scope
  2. Civil contraventions (s. 43) vs criminal offences (ss. 65–74)
  3. Intermediary liability (s. 79)
  4. Blocking and speech (s. 69A, Shreya Singhal)
  5. Data protection: DPDP Act, 2023

Concept in brief

The IT Act, 2000 gives legal recognition to electronic records and signatures and regulates intermediaries. It separates civil contraventions (s. 43, compensation through adjudicating officers under s. 46) from criminal offences (ss. 65–74, investigated by police and tried by courts). Intermediaries keep safe harbour (s. 79) if they act on court or government orders. Personal data is now governed by the DPDP Act, 2023.

Key provisions

  • IT Act ss. 2, 4–10A, 43, 43A, 46, 65–67B, 69A, 72, 79
  • DPDP Act, 2023

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015): s. 66A struck down; s. 79 read down.
  • Avnish Bajaj v. State (NCT of Delhi) (2008): Liability of an online platform's director.
  • Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer (2014): s. 65B certificate for electronic evidence.
  • Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal (2020): Certificate mandatory.
  • K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017): Informational privacy.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Justice B.N. Srikrishna Committee (2018)
  • Budapest Convention (India not a party)
  • Data: cybercrime complaints on the national portal

Critical lens

Executive-heavy regulation of speech online raises Art. 19 concerns.

Close with

A modern digital law needs rights, clear liability and an independent regulator.

Competition Law: Concept, purpose/prospects

Decode the demand. Questions test the shift from MRTP to the Competition Act, and digital markets.

Open with

The Competition Act, 2002 shifted India from controlling big business to protecting competition and consumers.

Answer frame

  1. MRTP Act and its limits (Raghavan Committee)
  2. Anti-competitive agreements
  3. Abuse of dominance
  4. Combinations and the 2023 amendment
  5. Digital markets

Concept in brief

The Competition Act, 2002 replaced the MRTP Act, 1969, shifting the focus from controlling size and monopoly to preventing conduct that harms competition and consumers. It prohibits anti-competitive agreements (s. 3) and abuse of dominance (s. 4), and regulates combinations (ss. 5–6). The 2023 amendment added deal-value thresholds and settlements and commitments.

Key provisions

  • Competition Act, 2002 ss. 3, 4, 5, 6, 18, 19, 26, 27, 32
  • Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • CCI v. Steel Authority of India (2010): Nature of CCI proceedings.
  • Excel Crop Care v. CCI (2017): Penalty on relevant turnover.
  • CCI v. Bharti Airtel (2019): Sectoral regulator first on technical issues.
  • CCI v. Coordination Committee of Artists (2017): Trade associations and boycotts.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Committee on Digital Competition Law (2024) and the draft Digital Competition Bill
  • CCI's Google Android order (2022)

Critical lens

Ex-post enforcement is slow for fast-moving digital markets.

Close with

Consumer welfare remains the touchstone, but digital markets may need ex-ante rules.

    How to approach: Discuss. MRTP's size-based approach vs the Competition Act's effects-based approach (ss. 3, 4, 5–6); consumer welfare in the Preamble; 2023 amendments. Example of a consumer-protective CCI order.
  2. 2025"Both horizontal and vertical agreements are included in Section 3 of the Competition Act, 2002, even when horizontal agreements are considered more harmful in comparison to vertical agreements." Discuss.
  3. 2022What do you mean by 'abuse of dominance' and 'abusive conduct' prohibited under the Competition Act, 2002?

  5. 2019"The basic purpose of competition policy and the law is to preserve and promote competition as a means of ensuring efficient allocation of resources in an economy." Elucidate the statement in the light of the new economic scenario in India.
  6. 2017How far is the Competition Act, 2002 an improvement over the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, 1969 (MRTP Act, 1969) with respect to 'abuse of dominant position'? Discuss and explain the relevant statutory provisions.

Alternate Dispute Resolution: Concept, types/prospects

Decode the demand. Questions test the types of ADR and their limits in public-law disputes.

Open with

ADR is not alternative to justice but alternative to adjudication; it suits disputes the parties can settle.

Answer frame

  1. Types: arbitration, mediation, conciliation, Lok Adalat
  2. Legal framework
  3. Advantages and data
  4. Limits: arbitrability, public law, constitutional rights
  5. Reform

Concept in brief

ADR covers arbitration, conciliation, mediation, negotiation and Lok Adalats. CPC s. 89 lets courts refer disputes; Lok Adalat awards are deemed decrees; pre-institution mediation is mandatory in commercial suits not seeking urgent relief. ADR suits private, contractual disputes, but disputes involving constitutional rights, crimes or public interest generally require adjudication by courts.

Key provisions

  • CPC s. 89, Order X rr. 1A–1C
  • Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, ss. 19–22
  • Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996
  • Mediation Act, 2023
  • Commercial Courts Act, 2015, s. 12A

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Salem Advocate Bar Assn. v. Union of India (2005): s. 89 framework.
  • Afcons Infrastructure v. Cherian Varkey Construction (2010): Which cases suit ADR.
  • Patil Automation v. Rakheja Engineers (2022): s. 12A mediation mandatory.
  • Vidya Drolia v. Durga Trading Corp. (2021): Limits of arbitrability.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Mediation Act, 2023
  • Pendency of about five crore cases (NJDG)
  • Singapore Convention on Mediation (India signed 2019)

Critical lens

Rights-based and public-interest disputes need a public, reasoned decision.

Close with

ADR complements courts; it cannot replace them where rights against the State are at stake.

Previous-year questions (5)

    How to approach: Critically discuss. ADR suits private and contractual disputes; limits: arbitrability (Booz Allen, Vidya Drolia), public policy, rights in rem, criminal and constitutional matters; benefits for pendency. Balanced close.

Major statutes concerning environmental law

Decode the demand. Questions test statutes, principles (precautionary, polluter pays, public trust) and institutions such as the NGT.

Open with

Indian environmental law combines statutes with judge-made principles rooted in Art. 21.

Answer frame

  1. Constitutional basis
  2. Key statutes
  3. Principles and their adoption in India
  4. Institutions: CPCB, NGT
  5. Gaps and reform

Concept in brief

India's environmental law rests on Arts. 21, 48A and 51A(g) and statutes on water, air, forests, wildlife, biodiversity and environment protection, with the NGT as a specialised forum. Courts have adopted sustainable development, the precautionary principle (replacing the older assimilative-capacity principle), polluter pays and public trust.

Key provisions

  • Water Act, 1974
  • Air Act, 1981
  • EPA, 1986
  • Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 (Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam, 2023)
  • Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972
  • Biological Diversity Act, 2002
  • NGT Act, 2010
  • Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996): Precaution and polluter pays.
  • A.P. Pollution Control Board v. M.V. Nayudu (1999): Shift from assimilative capacity to precaution; reverse burden.
  • M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath (1997): Public trust doctrine.
  • T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v. Union of India (1997): Dictionary meaning of 'forest'.
  • Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar (1991): Pollution-free water and air in Art. 21.
  • M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India (2024): Right against climate change effects.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Shift from assimilative capacity (Stockholm) to precaution (Rio Principle 15), traced in Nayudu
  • Air-quality data and NCAP
  • Forest (Conservation) Amendment, 2023 debate

Critical lens

Strong principles but weak enforcement and frequent dilution of clearances.

Close with

Sustainable development must mean development within ecological limits.

Right to Information Act

Decode the demand. Questions test RTI's objectives, exemptions and recent amendments.

Open with

RTI turned the citizen from a supplicant into a rights-holder against the State.

Answer frame

  1. Constitutional basis and objectives
  2. Key provisions and timelines
  3. Exemptions and public interest
  4. Amendments (2019, DPDP 2023)
  5. Assessment

Concept in brief

The RTI Act, 2005 operationalises the right to know under Art. 19(1)(a). Public authorities must disclose proactively (s. 4) and answer requests within 30 days, subject to exemptions (s. 8) and severability. The 2019 amendment let the Centre fix commissioners' tenure and pay; the DPDP Act, 2023 amended s. 8(1)(j) to exempt personal information outright.

Key provisions

  • RTI Act ss. 2(f), 2(h), 2(j), 4, 6–8, 11, 19, 20, 24

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • State of U.P. v. Raj Narain (1975): Right to know.
  • CBSE v. Aditya Bandopadhyay (2011): Evaluated answer sheets are information.
  • Girish Ramchandra Deshpande v. CIC (2012): Service records are personal information.
  • Thalappalam Service Co-op Bank v. State of Kerala (2013): Meaning of 'public authority'.
  • CPIO, Supreme Court v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal (2019): Office of the CJI is a public authority.
  • Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (2024): Electoral bond scheme struck down for violating the right to information.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • 2nd ARC 1st Report on the right to information as the 'master key' to good governance
  • MKSS movement in Rajasthan
  • Data: pending appeals before Information Commissions

Critical lens

Vacancies and the 2023 change to s. 8(1)(j) weaken transparency.

Close with

An independent and adequately staffed Information Commission is the backbone of RTI.

Trial by media

Decode the demand. Questions test the conflict between fair trial and press freedom and how to balance it.

Open with

A trial is held in court, not in studios; media freedom must not become a verdict before the verdict.

Answer frame

  1. Meaning and causes
  2. Constitutional conflict (Arts. 19(1)(a), 21)
  3. Legal tools: contempt, postponement orders
  4. Self-regulation and social media
  5. Way forward

Concept in brief

Trial by media is when coverage presumes guilt before the court decides, prejudicing fair trial (Art. 21) and the presumption of innocence. Press freedom (Art. 19(1)(a)) is balanced through contempt law, postponement orders and self-regulation, not prior censorship.

Key provisions

  • Contempt of Courts Act, 1971, ss. 2(c), 3, 4
  • Law Commission 200th Report (2006)
  • Cable TV Networks Act, 1995

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Sahara India Real Estate v. SEBI (2012): Postponement orders to protect fair trial.
  • R.K. Anand v. Registrar, Delhi High Court (2009): Sting operation and media role.
  • Manu Sharma v. State (NCT of Delhi) (2010): Media must not prejudice trials.
  • Nilesh Navalakha v. Union of India (Bom. HC 2021): Media trial during investigation amounts to interference.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Law Commission 200th Report (2006)
  • Comparative: UK Contempt of Court Act, 1981 strict liability rule
  • News Broadcasting and Digital Standards Authority guidelines

Critical lens

Gag orders risk censorship; inaction risks prejudice.

Close with
