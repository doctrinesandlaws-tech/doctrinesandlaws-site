General defences in tort

Overview

Even when the elements of a tort are present, the defendant may escape liability by proving a general defence.

1. Volenti non fit injuria (consent)

  • A person who consents to a risk cannot sue for harm from it.
  • Consent must be free; knowledge of the risk is not the same as consent (Smith v. Baker & Sons, 1891, workman injured by a stone from a crane).
  • Hall v. Brooklands Auto Racing Club (1933): spectators accept the ordinary risks of a car race.
  • Padmavati v. Dugganaika (1975): passengers who jumped from a jeep after a wheel came loose; the owner was not liable.
  • Limits: no consent to negligence beyond the ordinary risk; rescue cases (Haynes v. Harwood, 1935): a rescuer is not taken to consent; Unfair Contract Terms principles limit exclusion clauses for personal injury.

2. Plaintiff the wrongdoer

Ex turpi causa non oritur actio: no action arises from a base cause, but a wrongdoer is not an outlaw (Bird v. Holbrook, 1828: a trespasser injured by a spring-gun recovered).

3. Inevitable accident

An accident that could not have been avoided by reasonable care (Stanley v. Powell, 1891: a pellet ricocheted from a tree; Holmes v. Mather, 1875).

4. Act of God (vis major)

An extraordinary natural event that could not reasonably be anticipated (Nichols v. Marsland, 1876: an extraordinary rainfall burst artificial lakes). Ordinary heavy rain does not qualify, and human acts such as mob violence are not acts of God (Ramalinga Nadar v. Narayana Reddiar, Kerala HC, 1971).

5. Private defence

Reasonable force to protect person or property; the force must be proportionate (Collins v. Renison, 1754).

6. Mistake

Generally no defence (in trespass, conversion), except in malicious prosecution and false imprisonment where reasonable belief negatives malice or establishes lawful authority.

7. Necessity

An act to prevent greater harm (Leigh v. Gladstone, 1909: force-feeding of a hunger-striking prisoner; Cope v. Sharpe, 1912: entering land to stop a fire spreading).

8. Statutory authority

An act authorised by statute is a defence for its inevitable consequences, provided there is no negligence (Vaughan v. Taff Vale Railway, 1860: sparks from an engine; Hammersmith Railway v. Brand, 1869). Smith v. London and South Western Railway (1870): negligence defeats the defence.

9. Contributory negligence (partial defence)

Formerly a complete defence; now damages are apportioned according to fault (UK Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act, 1945). Indian courts apportion on equitable principles (Municipal Corporation of Greater Bombay v. Laxman Iyer, 2003). Last opportunity rule (Davies v. Mann, 1842) is largely superseded.

Revision points

  • Volenti: consent ≠ knowledge (Smith v. Baker); rescue exception (Haynes v. Harwood).
  • Act of God (Nichols v. Marsland) vs inevitable accident (Stanley v. Powell).
  • Necessity (Leigh v. Gladstone; Cope v. Sharpe); statutory authority (Vaughan v. Taff Vale).
  • Contributory negligence: apportionment.

Answer writing: General defences

Decode the demand. Questions test volenti non fit injuria and its limits, and inevitable accident and act of God. Use the rescue and employment exceptions.

Open with

Volenti non fit injuria: no wrong is done to one who consents, but consent must be free and informed, not mere knowledge.

Answer frame

  1. Meaning and ingredients
  2. Knowledge is not consent (Smith v Baker)
  3. Limits: rescue cases, unfair contract terms, statutory duties
  4. Other general defences
  5. Application

Concept in brief

General defences include consent (volenti non fit injuria), plaintiff the wrongdoer, inevitable accident, act of God, private defence, mistake, necessity and statutory authority. Volenti needs both knowledge and free acceptance of the risk; it does not cover rescuers, employees who merely know of danger, or unfair contract terms.

Key provisions

  • Consumer Protection Act, 2019 (unfair contract)

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Smith v. Baker (1891): Knowledge is not consent.
  • Hall v. Brooklands Auto-Racing Club (1933): Spectators accept ordinary risks.
  • Haynes v. Harwood (1935): Rescue cases outside volenti.
  • Padmavati v. Dugganaika (1975): Inevitable accident; volenti for passengers.
  • Nichols v. Marsland (1876): Act of God.
  • Stanley v. Powell (1891): Inevitable accident.
  • Vaughan v. Taff Vale Railway (1860): Statutory authority.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Sports and adventure tourism waivers
  • Consumer law limits on exclusion clauses (CPA 2019 s. 2(46))

Critical lens

Courts read consent narrowly to protect weaker parties.

Close with

Volenti is a shield for genuine risk-taking, not for shifting the cost of negligence.
