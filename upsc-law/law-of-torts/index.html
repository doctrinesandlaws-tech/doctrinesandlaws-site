Syllabus unit 1 · Asked 6 times · 2014–2025

Nature and definition

A tort is a civil wrong, other than a breach of contract or trust, remedied by unliquidated damages. Liability turns on the violation of a legal right, not on loss: injuria sine damno is actionable, damnum sine injuria is not. India applies English common law as modified by justice, equity and good conscience.

Key provisions

  • Art. 300 (State liability)
  • Consumer Protection Act, 2019

Leading cases

  • Ashby v. White (1703): Injuria sine damno actionable.
  • Gloucester Grammar School Case (1410): Damnum sine injuria not actionable.
  • Mogul Steamship v. McGregor (1892): Competition causing loss is lawful.
  • Bhim Singh v. State of J&K (1985): Constitutional tort compensation for illegal detention.

Liability based upon fault and strict liability; Absolute liability

Most torts require fault (intention or negligence). Strict liability (Rylands v. Fletcher) makes a person liable for escape of a dangerous thing from land used non-naturally, subject to defences. India's absolute liability (Oleum Gas) allows no exceptions for hazardous enterprises; it underpins the Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991.

Key provisions

  • Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991
  • National Green Tribunal Act, 2010, s. 17

Leading cases

  • Rylands v. Fletcher (1868): Strict liability rule.
  • Read v. J. Lyons (1947): Escape is essential.
  • M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987): Absolute liability for hazardous industries; no exceptions.
  • Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India (1996): Polluter pays; absolute liability applied.
  • Union Carbide Corp. v. Union of India (1989): Bhopal settlement.

Vicarious liability, including State Liability

Vicarious liability makes one person liable for another's tort because of their relationship, chiefly employer and employee acting in the course of employment. It rests on policy (deep pocket, risk distribution, deterrence) rather than the employer's fault. For the State, the old sovereign-function immunity (Kasturilal) has been eroded; public law compensation for violation of Art. 21 is not subject to it.

Key provisions

  • Art. 300
  • Government of India Act, 1858, s. 65 (origin)

Leading cases

  • Lloyd v. Grace, Smith & Co. (1912): Liability for fraud by clerk acting in apparent authority.
  • Century Insurance v. Northern Ireland Road Transport Board (1942): Negligent act in course of employment.
  • Lister v. Hesley Hall (2001): Close-connection test.
  • State of Rajasthan v. Vidyawati (1962): State liable like a private employer.
  • Kasturilal Ralia Ram Jain v. State of U.P. (1965): Sovereign immunity applied (later doubted).
  • N. Nagendra Rao v. State of A.P. (1994): Sovereign immunity limited.
  • Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar (1983): Compensation under Art. 32.
  • Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa (1993): Strict public law liability; immunity not a defence.

Syllabus unit 4 · Asked 5 times · 2014–2026

General defences

General defences include consent (volenti non fit injuria), plaintiff the wrongdoer, inevitable accident, act of God, private defence, mistake, necessity and statutory authority. Volenti needs both knowledge and free acceptance of the risk; it does not cover rescuers, employees who merely know of danger, or unfair contract terms.

Key provisions

  • Consumer Protection Act, 2019 (unfair contract)

Leading cases

  • Smith v. Baker (1891): Knowledge is not consent.
  • Hall v. Brooklands Auto-Racing Club (1933): Spectators accept ordinary risks.
  • Haynes v. Harwood (1935): Rescue cases outside volenti.
  • Padmavati v. Dugganaika (1975): Inevitable accident; volenti for passengers.
  • Nichols v. Marsland (1876): Act of God.
  • Stanley v. Powell (1891): Inevitable accident.
  • Vaughan v. Taff Vale Railway (1860): Statutory authority.

Joint tortfeasors

Joint tortfeasors are persons responsible for the same tort, through agency, vicarious liability or common action; each is liable for the whole damage. Independent tortfeasors causing the same damage are treated similarly. Contribution between them is governed by equity in India in the absence of a statute.

Key provisions

  • (UK) Law Reform (Married Women and Tortfeasors) Act, 1935; Civil Liability (Contribution) Act, 1978

Leading cases

  • Brooke v. Bool (1928): Joint enterprise.
  • The Koursk (1924): Independent vs joint tortfeasors.
  • Merryweather v. Nixan (1799): Old rule against contribution.
  • Khushro S. Gandhi v. N.A. Guzder (1970): Release of one joint tortfeasor.

Remedies

Judicial remedies are damages (nominal, contemptuous, compensatory, aggravated, exemplary), injunctions and specific restitution; extra-judicial remedies include self-defence and abatement. Remoteness limits recovery to foreseeable kinds of damage.

Key provisions

  • Specific Relief Act, 1963, ss. 36–42

Leading cases

  • Rookes v. Barnard (1964): Categories of exemplary damages.
  • Re Polemis (1921): Direct consequence test (abandoned).
  • Overseas Tankship v. Morts Dock (Wagon Mound No. 1) (1961): Foreseeability test.
  • Common Cause v. Union of India (1999): Exemplary damages against State officials.

Negligence

Negligence needs a duty of care, breach (falling below the standard of a reasonable person), and damage that is not too remote. Res ipsa loquitur lets the facts speak when the thing was under the defendant's control and accidents of that kind do not ordinarily happen without negligence; it raises an inference but, in India, does not shift the legal burden of proof completely. Professional negligence is judged by the Bolam standard, with gross negligence required for criminal liability.

Key provisions

  • IPC s. 304A → BNS s. 106
  • Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, ss. 140, 163A, 166

Leading cases

  • Donoghue v. Stevenson (1932): Neighbour principle.
  • Caparo Industries v. Dickman (1990): Three-stage duty test.
  • Bolam v. Friern Hospital (1957): Professional standard.
  • Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab (2005): Medical negligence: gross negligence for criminal liability.
  • Scott v. London & St. Katherine Docks (1865): Res ipsa loquitur.
  • Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Subhagwanti (1966): Clock tower collapse: res ipsa applied.
  • Syad Akbar v. State of Karnataka (1980): Res ipsa is a rule of evidence; burden does not shift entirely.
  • Pramodkumar Rasikbhai Jhaveri v. Karmasey Kunvargi Tak (2002): Contributory negligence apportioned.

Defamation

Defamation is publication of a false statement that lowers a person in the estimation of right-thinking members of society. Libel is permanent, slander transient. Defences include justification (truth), fair comment, absolute privilege (Parliament, courts) and qualified privilege.

Key provisions

  • IPC s. 499 → BNS s. 356 (criminal)
  • Art. 19(2)

Leading cases

  • Hulton v. Jones (1910): Intention irrelevant; reference to plaintiff.
  • Cassidy v. Daily Mirror (1929): Innuendo.
  • Tolley v. J.S. Fry & Sons (1931): Innuendo in advertisement.
  • Youssoupoff v. MGM Pictures (1934): Defamatory film is libel.
  • Ram Jethmalani v. Subramaniam Swamy (2006): Defamatory press statements.
  • R. Rajagopal v. State of T.N. (1994): Public officials and actual-malice-style protection.

Nuisance

Nuisance is unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land (private) or an act affecting the public at large (public). The same act can be both: a public nuisance becomes privately actionable where the plaintiff suffers special damage beyond the public. Public nuisance is also a crime and a ground for magisterial orders.

Key provisions

  • CPC s. 91
  • IPC s. 268 → BNS s. 270
  • CrPC s. 133 → BNSS s. 152

Leading cases

  • Attorney-General v. PYA Quarries (1957): Public nuisance affects a class of people.
  • Castle v. St. Augustine's Links (1922): Golf balls on a highway: public nuisance with special damage.
  • Radhey Shyam v. Gur Prasad Sharma (1978): Noise as private nuisance.
  • Dr. Ram Raj Singh v. Babulal (1982): Brick-grinding dust held nuisance.
  • Municipal Council, Ratlam v. Vardhichand (1980): Public nuisance duties of municipalities.

Conspiracy

The tort of conspiracy has two forms: a lawful-means conspiracy whose predominant purpose is to injure the claimant, and an unlawful-means conspiracy where the means are themselves unlawful. Damage must result.

Leading cases

  • Crofter Hand Woven Harris Tweed v. Veitch (1942): Predominant purpose test.
  • Quinn v. Leathem (1901): Combination to injure actionable.

False imprisonment

False imprisonment is total restraint of a person's liberty without lawful justification; awareness is not necessary and no force is needed. Partial obstruction is not enough. Illegal detention by police also gives a public-law remedy under Arts. 21 and 32.

Key provisions

  • Arts. 21, 22
  • IPC ss. 339–348 → BNS ss. 126–136

Leading cases

  • Bird v. Jones (1845): Total restraint required.
  • Meering v. Grahame-White Aviation (1919): Knowledge of restraint not needed.
  • Herd v. Weardale Steel (1915): Restraint under contract terms.
  • Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar (1983): Compensation for illegal detention.
  • Bhim Singh v. State of J&K (1985): Exemplary compensation for unlawful arrest.

Malicious prosecution

Malicious prosecution requires that the defendant prosecuted the plaintiff, without reasonable and probable cause, with malice, that the proceedings ended in the plaintiff's favour, and that damage resulted.

Leading cases

  • Mohamed Amin v. Jogendra Kumar Bannerjee (1947, PC): Elements of malicious prosecution.
  • West Bengal State Electricity Board v. Dilip Kumar Ray (2007): Ingredients restated.
  • Willers v. Joyce (2016, UK SC): Extended to malicious civil proceedings.

Consumer Protection Act

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 replaced the 1986 Act. 'Consumer' now expressly covers online and offline transactions through e-commerce, teleshopping, direct selling and multi-level marketing; a Central Consumer Protection Authority acts against unfair trade practices and misleading advertisements, and a product-liability chapter lets consumers sue manufacturers, sellers and service providers. Commissions can declare unfair contract terms void.

Key provisions

  • CPA 2019: ss. 2(7), 2(9), 2(11), 2(46), 2(47), 10, 18, 34–73, 82–87
  • Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020

Leading cases

  • Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha (1995): Paid medical services are 'service'.
  • Lucknow Development Authority v. M.K. Gupta (1994): Housing by public authorities covered; compensation for harassment.
  • Laxmi Engineering Works v. P.S.G. Industrial Institute (1995): Commercial-purpose exclusion explained.
  • Lilavati Kirtilal Mehta Medical Trust v. Unique Shanti Developers (2020): Dominant purpose test for 'commercial purpose'.
  • Bar of Indian Lawyers v. D.K. Gandhi (2024): Advocates' services outside the Act.
