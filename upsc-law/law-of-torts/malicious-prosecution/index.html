Malicious prosecution

Meaning

Malicious prosecution is the institution of unsuccessful criminal (or certain civil) proceedings against the plaintiff, maliciously and without reasonable and probable cause, causing him damage.

Essentials

  1. Prosecution by the defendant: he must have set the law in motion (a complaint or FIR that leads to proceedings). Mohamed Amin v. Jogendra Kumar Bannerjee (Privy Council, 1947): prosecution begins when a complaint is made to a magistrate who takes some action; it need not have reached the issue of process.
  2. Termination in the plaintiff's favour: acquittal, discharge or withdrawal. A conviction, even if later reversed on appeal, may still count once set aside.
  3. Absence of reasonable and probable cause: Hicks v. Faulkner (1881): an honest belief in the guilt of the accused, based on reasonable grounds, which would lead an ordinarily prudent person to conclude that the accused was probably guilty.
  4. Malice: an improper motive other than bringing an offender to justice (spite, revenge, coercion). Malice may be inferred from absence of reasonable cause, but not the reverse.
  5. Damage: Savile v. Roberts (1698) identified three heads: damage to reputation (fame), to the person (loss of liberty), and to property (costs of defence).

The burden of proving all essentials is on the plaintiff (Abrath v. North Eastern Railway Co., 1886).

Indian case law

  • West Bengal State Electricity Board v. Dilip Kumar Ray (2007): set out the essentials and the difference between malicious prosecution and false imprisonment.
  • Bharat Commerce & Industries v. Surendra Nath Shankar (1966): damages for malicious complaints.
  • Limitation: one year from the date of acquittal or termination of the prosecution (Limitation Act, 1963, Schedule, Art. 74).

Malicious civil proceedings

Generally not actionable, because the costs regime compensates the winner, except where the proceedings damage credit or liberty: malicious bankruptcy or winding-up petitions, malicious arrest or attachment (Quartz Hill Consolidated Gold Mining v. Eyre, 1883). In Willers v. Joyce (2016) the UK Supreme Court recognised malicious civil prosecution more broadly.

Constitutional dimension

Wrongful prosecution can also violate Art. 21. The Law Commission's 277th Report (2018), Wrongful Prosecution (Miscarriage of Justice): Legal Remedies, recommended a statutory scheme to compensate victims of wrongful prosecution.

Revision points

  • Five essentials; burden on plaintiff (Abrath).
  • Reasonable and probable cause (Hicks v. Faulkner); three heads of damage (Savile v. Roberts).
  • Limitation: one year from acquittal (Art. 74).
  • Law Commission 277th Report (2018).
Syllabus unit 12 · Asked 2 times · 2016–2023

Answer writing: Malicious prosecution

Decode the demand. Know the five essentials and the line between malice and absence of probable cause.

Open with

Malicious prosecution balances the public interest in reporting crime with protection from abuse of legal process.

Answer frame

  1. Essentials
  2. Reasonable and probable cause
  3. Malice
  4. Termination in plaintiff's favour
  5. Cases

Concept in brief

Malicious prosecution requires that the defendant prosecuted the plaintiff, without reasonable and probable cause, with malice, that the proceedings ended in the plaintiff's favour, and that damage resulted.

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Mohamed Amin v. Jogendra Kumar Bannerjee (1947, PC): Elements of malicious prosecution.
  • West Bengal State Electricity Board v. Dilip Kumar Ray (2007): Ingredients restated.
  • Willers v. Joyce (2016, UK SC): Extended to malicious civil proceedings.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Misuse of criminal process in civil disputes (courts' quashing powers under BNSS s. 528)

Critical lens

High thresholds protect complainants but leave victims of false cases with little remedy.

Close with

Remedy for malicious prosecution must coexist with freedom to report crime.
