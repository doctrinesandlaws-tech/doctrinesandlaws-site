Fault, strict and absolute liability

Fault-based liability

The general rule: a defendant is liable only for intentional or negligent wrongdoing. Negligence requires proof of duty, breach and damage.

Strict liability: Rylands v. Fletcher (1868)

  • The defendants built a reservoir; water escaped through old mine shafts and flooded the plaintiff's mine. Blackburn J.: a person who brings on his land and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes keeps it at his peril and is answerable for all damage that is the natural consequence of its escape. The House of Lords added the requirement of non-natural use of land.
  • Essentials: (1) a dangerous thing, (2) brought onto the land for the defendant's purposes, (3) non-natural use, (4) escape, (5) damage.
  • Exceptions: plaintiff's own default; act of God (Nichols v. Marsland, 1876); consent of the plaintiff; act of a stranger (Rickards v. Lothian, 1913); statutory authority.
  • Limits: escape is essential (Read v. J. Lyons & Co., 1947); damage must be foreseeable (Cambridge Water Co. v. Eastern Counties Leather, 1994).

Absolute liability: M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (Oleum Gas Leak, 1987)

  • After the oleum gas leak at Shriram Foods and Fertiliser Industries in Delhi (December 1985), a five-judge bench led by Bhagwati C.J. held: - An enterprise engaged in a hazardous or inherently dangerous activity owes an absolute and non-delegable duty to the community. - If harm results, it is absolutely liable; none of the Rylands exceptions apply. - Compensation must correlate with the magnitude and capacity of the enterprise, to have a deterrent effect.
  • Why: Rylands was a 19th-century rule unsuited to modern industrial hazards in a developing country; the Bhopal gas disaster (1984) showed the need.

Developments and statutes

  • Bhopal: the Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster (Processing of Claims) Act, 1985 made the Union the claimants' representative (upheld in Charan Lal Sahu v. Union of India, 1990); settlement of USD 470 million (Union Carbide Corporation v. Union of India, 1989). The curative petition to enhance compensation was dismissed in 2023.
  • Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991: no-fault relief to victims of accidents involving hazardous substances; mandatory insurance.
  • Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India (1996): polluter pays and absolute liability for chemical industries in Bichhri.
  • National Green Tribunal Act, 2010, s. 17(3): the Tribunal applies no-fault liability in accidents.
  • LG Polymers, Visakhapatnam (2020): the NGT applied absolute liability after the styrene gas leak.
  • Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010: channels liability to the operator with a cap; s. 17(b) gives the operator a right of recourse against suppliers, a point debated with foreign suppliers.

  • Rylands essentials and exceptions; Read v. Lyons, Cambridge Water.
  • M.C. Mehta (1987): no exceptions, deterrent damages.
  • Bhopal statutes, PLIA 1991, NGT s. 17(3), Nuclear Liability Act s. 17(b).
Syllabus unit 2 · Asked 6 times · 2016–2024

Answer writing: Liability based upon fault and strict liability; Absolute liability

Decode the demand. Questions compare strict and absolute liability. A table and the Oleum Gas rationale are essential.

Open with

From Rylands v Fletcher to M.C. Mehta, liability without fault has moved from exception to necessity in a hazardous industrial economy.

Answer frame

  1. Fault-based liability
  2. Strict liability: rule and defences
  3. Absolute liability: Oleum Gas, no defences
  4. Comparison table
  5. Statutory support (PLI Act, NGT Act s. 17)

Concept in brief

Most torts require fault (intention or negligence). Strict liability (Rylands v. Fletcher) makes a person liable for escape of a dangerous thing from land used non-naturally, subject to defences. India's absolute liability (Oleum Gas) allows no exceptions for hazardous enterprises; it underpins the Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991.

Key provisions

  • Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991
  • National Green Tribunal Act, 2010, s. 17

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Rylands v. Fletcher (1868): Strict liability rule.
  • Read v. J. Lyons (1947): Escape is essential.
  • M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987): Absolute liability for hazardous industries; no exceptions.
  • Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India (1996): Polluter pays; absolute liability applied.
  • Union Carbide Corp. v. Union of India (1989): Bhopal settlement.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Bhopal disaster and the Union Carbide settlement
  • Economics: enterprise liability and risk spreading
  • Vizag LG Polymers gas leak (2020) and NGT orders

Draw this

Fault → strict (Rylands, with defences) → absolute (Oleum Gas, no defences)

Critical lens

Absolute liability is strong in principle but weak in enforcement and compensation.

Close with

Hazardous enterprises must bear their full social cost; that is the polluter-pays idea in tort form.
