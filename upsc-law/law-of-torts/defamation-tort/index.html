Study notes

MeaningLibel and slanderEssentialsDefencesIndian developmentsRemedies

Meaning

Defamation is the publication of a false statement that injures a person's reputation, by lowering him in the estimation of right-thinking members of society or causing them to shun or avoid him.

Libel and slander

  • Libel: in a permanent form (writing, print, pictures, broadcasts, online posts). Actionable per se (no proof of damage needed).
  • Slander: transient form (spoken words, gestures). In England, actionable only on proof of special damage, with exceptions (imputation of a crime punishable with imprisonment, a contagious disease, unfitness in a trade or profession, unchastity of a woman).
  • India: courts generally treat both as actionable per se; criminal defamation (BNS s. 356) makes no distinction.

Essentials

  1. The statement is defamatory. It may be defamatory by innuendo, a secondary meaning understood by persons with special knowledge (Tolley v. J.S. Fry & Sons, 1931: an amateur golfer pictured in a chocolate advertisement; Cassidy v. Daily Mirror, 1929: a man photographed with a woman described as his fiancée).
  2. It refers to the plaintiff. Intention is irrelevant (Hulton & Co. v. Jones, 1910: a fictitious "Artemus Jones"; Newstead v. London Express Newspaper, 1940).
  3. It is published: communicated to at least one person other than the plaintiff. Communication between spouses is not publication.

Defences

  1. Justification (truth): a complete defence in civil law; substantial truth suffices.
  2. Fair comment on a matter of public interest: must be comment (opinion), not fact; based on true facts; honest, without malice (Merivale v. Carson, 1887).
  3. Privilege: - Absolute: parliamentary proceedings (Arts. 105, 194), judicial proceedings, communications between high officers of State. - Qualified: statements made in performance of a legal, social or moral duty to a person with a corresponding interest, without malice (references, complaints to authorities); fair reports of parliamentary and judicial proceedings.
  4. Apology and offer of amends can reduce damages.

Indian developments

  • R. Rajagopal v. State of Tamil Nadu (1994): public officials cannot claim damages for statements about their official conduct unless they prove the statement was made with reckless disregard for truth (adopting New York Times v. Sullivan, 1964); also recognised a right to privacy.
  • Ram Jethmalani v. Subramaniam Swamy (Delhi HC, 2006): damages awarded for defamatory statements.
  • Tata Sons v. Greenpeace International (Delhi HC, 2011): no interim injunction where the defendant pleads justification or fair comment (the Bonnard v. Perryman rule); parody of a logo in an online game was allowed.
  • Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India (2016): reputation is part of Art. 21; criminal defamation upheld.
  • Online defamation: intermediaries have safe harbour under s. 79 of the IT Act if they act on court orders (Shreya Singhal, 2015).

Remedies

Damages (general, special, aggravated, exemplary) and injunctions. Courts are cautious with pre-publication injunctions because of free speech (Art. 19(1)(a)).

Revision points

  • Libel vs slander; India treats both as actionable per se.
  • Essentials: defamatory (innuendo: Tolley, Cassidy), refers to plaintiff (Hulton v. Jones), publication.
  • Defences: justification, fair comment (Merivale v. Carson), privilege.
  • R. Rajagopal (1994); Tata Sons v. Greenpeace (2011); Subramanian Swamy (2016).
Syllabus unit 8 · Asked 9 times · 2013–2025

Answer writing: Defamation

Decode the demand. Questions test the elements and defences of defamation, and newer issues such as social media.

Open with

Defamation protects reputation, which is treated as part of the right to life, while defences protect free speech.

Answer frame

  1. Elements: statement, reference, publication
  2. Libel vs slander
  3. Defences
  4. Constitutional balance
  5. Online defamation and intermediaries

Concept in brief

Defamation is publication of a false statement that lowers a person in the estimation of right-thinking members of society. Libel is permanent, slander transient. Defences include justification (truth), fair comment, absolute privilege (Parliament, courts) and qualified privilege.

Key provisions

  • IPC s. 499 → BNS s. 356 (criminal)
  • Art. 19(2)

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Hulton v. Jones (1910): Intention irrelevant; reference to plaintiff.
  • Cassidy v. Daily Mirror (1929): Innuendo.
  • Tolley v. J.S. Fry & Sons (1931): Innuendo in advertisement.
  • Youssoupoff v. MGM Pictures (1934): Defamatory film is libel.
  • Ram Jethmalani v. Subramaniam Swamy (2006): Defamatory press statements.
  • R. Rajagopal v. State of T.N. (1994): Public officials and actual-malice-style protection.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Comparative: New York Times v Sullivan (1964) actual-malice standard
  • Intermediary rules and takedown

Critical lens

Delays in civil suits push litigants to criminal complaints.

Close with

Faster civil remedies would better balance reputation and expression.
