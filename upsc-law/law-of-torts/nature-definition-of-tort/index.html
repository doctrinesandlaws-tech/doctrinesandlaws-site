Nature and definition of tort

Definition

  • Winfield: tortious liability arises from the breach of a duty primarily fixed by law; this duty is towards persons generally and its breach is redressable by an action for unliquidated damages.
  • Salmond: a tort is a civil wrong for which the remedy is a common-law action for unliquidated damages, and which is not exclusively the breach of a contract or of a trust.
  • In India, s. 2(m) of the Limitation Act, 1963 defines a tort as a civil wrong which is not exclusively a breach of contract or of trust.

Essentials

  1. A wrongful act or omission by the defendant.
  2. Legal damage (violation of a legal right) to the plaintiff.
  3. A legal remedy, typically unliquidated damages.

Damage and legal injury

  • Injuria sine damno (legal injury without actual loss) is actionable: Ashby v. White (1703): a returning officer wrongly refused a voter; Bhim Singh v. State of J&K (1985): a legislator illegally detained to prevent him attending the Assembly got exemplary compensation.
  • Damnum sine injuria (loss without legal injury) is not actionable: Gloucester Grammar School case (1410): a rival school causing loss of fees; Mogul Steamship Co. v. McGregor, Gow & Co. (1892): lawful trade competition.

Law of tort or law of torts?

  • Winfield: a general principle of liability; all unjustifiable harm is tortious unless justified (law of tort).
  • Salmond: a set of specific, named wrongs (the "pigeon-hole" theory); no liability unless the harm fits a recognised tort (law of torts).
  • Modern courts create new torts (negligence after Donoghue v. Stevenson, privacy), which supports Winfield, but certainty still matters.

The Indian position

  • Tort law is largely uncodified; courts apply English common law where it suits Indian conditions, under "justice, equity and good conscience".
  • Statutes govern parts of the field: Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 (no-fault and fault liability), Consumer Protection Act, 2019, Fatal Accidents Act, 1855, Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991.
  • Constitutional tort: compensation under Arts. 32 and 226 for violation of Art. 21 (Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar, 1983; Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa, 1993).
  • Reasons for limited growth: delays, court fees, low damages awards, and the availability of criminal and consumer remedies.

Revision points

  • Winfield vs Salmond definitions and "tort/torts" debate.
  • Injuria sine damno (Ashby v. White, Bhim Singh) vs damnum sine injuria (Gloucester Grammar, Mogul).
  • Uncodified; constitutional tort (Rudul Sah, Nilabati Behera).

Answer writing: Nature and definition

Decode the demand. Questions ask for the definition of tort, its distinction from crime and contract, and maxims.

Open with

A tort is a civil wrong redressed by unliquidated damages, so it is defined as much by its remedy as by its wrong.

Answer frame

  1. Definitions (Salmond, Winfield)
  2. Law of tort or law of torts
  3. Injuria sine damno and damnum sine injuria
  4. Tort vs crime vs contract
  5. Growth in India

Concept in brief

A tort is a civil wrong, other than a breach of contract or trust, remedied by unliquidated damages. Liability turns on the violation of a legal right, not on loss: injuria sine damno is actionable, damnum sine injuria is not. India applies English common law as modified by justice, equity and good conscience.

Key provisions

  • Art. 300 (State liability)
  • Consumer Protection Act, 2019

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Ashby v. White (1703): Injuria sine damno actionable.
  • Gloucester Grammar School Case (1410): Damnum sine injuria not actionable.
  • Mogul Steamship v. McGregor (1892): Competition causing loss is lawful.
  • Bhim Singh v. State of J&K (1985): Constitutional tort compensation for illegal detention.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Winfield's general principle vs Salmond's pigeon-holes
  • Economics of tort: deterrence and loss distribution
  • Constitutional torts (Rudul Sah)

Critical lens

Indian tort law has grown slowly because of costs and delay.

Close with

Constitutional tort and consumer law are giving Indian tort law new life.
