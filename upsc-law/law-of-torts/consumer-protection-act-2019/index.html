Why a new Act

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 (in force July 2020) replaced the 1986 Act to address e-commerce, misleading advertisements, product liability and slow adjudication.

Key definitions (s. 2)

  • Consumer (s. 2(7)): a person who buys goods or hires services for consideration, including online, teleshopping, direct selling or multi-level marketing; excludes purchase for resale or commercial purpose (except self-employment for livelihood).
  • Defect (goods) and deficiency (services, including negligence or omission causing loss).
  • Unfair trade practice (s. 2(47)): false representations, misleading advertisements, refusal to take back defective goods, and disclosure of personal information given in confidence.
  • Unfair contract (s. 2(46)): one-sided terms (excessive deposits, unilateral termination).
  • Misleading advertisement (s. 2(28)).
  • Product liability (s. 2(34)): responsibility of a manufacturer, service provider or seller to compensate for harm caused by a defective product or deficient service.

Institutions

  • Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) (s. 10): investigates violations of consumer rights, orders recalls and refunds, and imposes penalties for misleading advertisements, up to ₹10 lakh (₹50 lakh for repeat violations) on manufacturers and endorsers (s. 21). Endorsers have a due-diligence defence. It has issued guidelines on misleading ads (2022) and dark patterns (2023).
  • Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions at district, State and national levels.
  • Pecuniary jurisdiction (Consumer Protection (Jurisdiction) Rules, 2021), based on the value of consideration paid: - District Commission: up to ₹50 lakh - State Commission: above ₹50 lakh up to ₹2 crore - National Commission: above ₹2 crore
  • Territorial jurisdiction includes where the complainant resides or works (s. 34).
  • Mediation (Chapter V) and e-filing (e-Daakhil, merged into the e-Jagriti portal from 1 January 2025).
  • Limitation: two years from the cause of action (s. 69).

Product liability (Chapter VI, ss. 82–87)

  • A product manufacturer is liable for manufacturing defects, design defects, deviation from specifications, non-conformity with express warranty, or inadequate instructions (s. 84), even without proof of negligence.
  • A service provider is liable for faulty or deficient services (s. 85).
  • A product seller is liable when he exercised substantial control, altered the product, made an independent warranty, or when the manufacturer cannot be identified or served (s. 86).
  • Exceptions (s. 87): misuse, alteration, and products used as a component under a purchaser's specifications.

E-commerce

Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020: duties of marketplace and inventory e-commerce entities, grievance officers, disclosure of seller details and country of origin, no manipulation of prices.

Leading cases

  • Lucknow Development Authority v. M.K. Gupta (1994): housing construction is a service; compensation can include harassment, and the authority may recover it from erring officials.
  • Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha (1995): medical services for payment are covered; free services are not.
  • Spring Meadows Hospital v. Harjol Ahluwalia (1998): parents of a child injured by medical negligence are consumers too.
  • Bar of Indian Lawyers v. D.K. Gandhi PS National Institute of Communicable Diseases (2024): services of advocates are not covered by the Act (contracts of personal service); the Court also questioned whether medical services should remain within it and referred V.P. Shantha to a larger bench.

Consumer protection and torts

The Act gives a quick, inexpensive forum for what would otherwise be tort claims in negligence and product liability, which is why Indian tort litigation is thin in these areas.

Revision points

  • CPA 2019: consumer includes online purchases; unfair trade practice; product liability (ss. 82–87).
  • CCPA (s. 10), penalties for misleading ads (s. 21), endorser liability.
  • Jurisdiction by consideration paid: ₹50 lakh / ₹2 crore; limitation two years.
  • LDA v. M.K. Gupta (1994), V.P. Shantha (1995), Bar of Indian Lawyers (2024).
Syllabus unit 13 · Asked 14 times · 2013–2026

Answer writing: Consumer Protection Act

Decode the demand. Frequently asked. Questions test the definition of consumer, e-commerce, product liability and the CCPA. Contrast the 1986 and 2019 Acts.

Open with

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 updated consumer law for the digital marketplace: e-commerce, product liability and a regulator.

Answer frame

  1. Consumer and its expanded definition (online, e-commerce)
  2. Rights and the CCPA
  3. Product liability (Ch. VI)
  4. Unfair trade practices and unfair contracts
  5. Dispute forums and pecuniary limits

Concept in brief

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 replaced the 1986 Act. 'Consumer' now expressly covers online and offline transactions through e-commerce, teleshopping, direct selling and multi-level marketing; a Central Consumer Protection Authority acts against unfair trade practices and misleading advertisements, and a product-liability chapter lets consumers sue manufacturers, sellers and service providers. Commissions can declare unfair contract terms void.

Key provisions

  • CPA 2019: ss. 2(7), 2(9), 2(11), 2(46), 2(47), 10, 18, 34–73, 82–87
  • Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha (1995): Paid medical services are 'service'.
  • Lucknow Development Authority v. M.K. Gupta (1994): Housing by public authorities covered; compensation for harassment.
  • Laxmi Engineering Works v. P.S.G. Industrial Institute (1995): Commercial-purpose exclusion explained.
  • Lilavati Kirtilal Mehta Medical Trust v. Unique Shanti Developers (2020): Dominant purpose test for 'commercial purpose'.
  • Bar of Indian Lawyers v. D.K. Gandhi (2024): Advocates' services outside the Act.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020
  • CCPA guidelines on dark patterns (2023)
  • Behavioural economics: information asymmetry

Critical lens

Enforcement against cross-border platforms remains difficult.

Close with

Consumer sovereignty in the digital market needs fast forums and an active regulator.
