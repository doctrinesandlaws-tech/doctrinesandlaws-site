Vicarious liability

One person is liable for a tort committed by another because of their relationship. Main relationships: master and servant (employer and employee), principal and agent, and partners.

Why employers are liable

Respondeat superior (let the master answer); qui facit per alium facit per se (he who acts through another acts himself); the employer has deeper pockets, controls the work and benefits from it.

Course of employment

  • The employer is liable for torts committed by an employee in the course of employment, including wrongful and unauthorised modes of doing authorised acts.
  • Century Insurance Co. v. Northern Ireland Road Transport Board (1942): a driver who threw a lighted match while unloading petrol acted in the course of employment.
  • Lloyd v. Grace, Smith & Co. (1912): a firm was liable for fraud by its clerk acting within apparent authority, even though the fraud was for his own benefit.
  • Lister v. Hesley Hall (2001, House of Lords): close connection test: the employer of a warden who abused boys was liable because the abuse was closely connected with his duties.
  • State Bank of India v. Shyama Devi (1978): the bank was not liable when an employee misappropriated money handed to him as a friend, outside his duties.
  • Independent contractors: the employer is generally not liable, except for non-delegable duties, ultra-hazardous work, or where the employer authorised or ratified the tort.

Liability of the State

Constitutional basis

  • Art. 300: the Government of India and State governments may sue and be sued as the Dominion of India and the Provinces could before the Constitution. This preserves the pre-Constitution position.
  • Peninsular & Oriental Steam Navigation Co. v. Secretary of State for India (1861): the State is liable for non-sovereign acts (commercial or private-like activities) but not for sovereign acts.

Indian case law

  • State of Rajasthan v. Vidyawati (1962): the State was liable for a government jeep driver's negligence; no immunity for such acts in a welfare State.
  • Kasturi Lal Ralia Ram Jain v. State of U.P. (1965): police officers misappropriated gold seized from the plaintiff; the State was not liable because arrest and seizure were sovereign functions.
  • N. Nagendra Rao & Co. v. State of A.P. (1994): sovereign immunity confined to primary inalienable functions (defence, legislation, administration of justice); the State is liable for negligence in regulatory functions.
  • Common Cause v. Union of India (1999) and Chairman, Railway Board v. Chandrima Das (2000): the State is liable for torts by its employees, including rape of a foreign national by railway employees.

Public law compensation for fundamental rights violations

  • Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar (1983): compensation for illegal detention of 14 years after acquittal.
  • Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa (1993): custodial death; sovereign immunity does not apply to public-law compensation for violation of fundamental rights; Kasturi Lal is limited to private-law claims.
  • D.K. Basu v. State of W.B. (1997): guidelines on arrest; compensation for custodial violence.
  • Recent: compensation for detention after acquittal and for wrongful prosecution has been awarded by constitutional courts under Art. 21.

Reform

The Law Commission's First Report (1956) recommended a statute on State liability modelled on the UK Crown Proceedings Act, 1947; bills were introduced in 1965 and 1967 but lapsed. The sovereign–non-sovereign distinction is now widely seen as outdated.

Revision points

  • Course of employment; Lloyd v. Grace Smith; Lister close-connection test.
  • Art. 300; P&O (1861); Vidyawati (1962) vs Kasturi Lal (1965); Nagendra Rao (1994).
  • Public-law route: Rudul Sah, Nilabati Behera, D.K. Basu.
Syllabus unit 3 · Asked 11 times · 2014–2026

Answer writing: Vicarious liability, including State Liability

Decode the demand. Questions test vicarious liability's basis and State liability. Explain the policy rationale, then sovereign immunity.

Open with

Vicarious liability rests on policy: the one who profits from an enterprise should bear its risks.

Answer frame

  1. Basis: policy over fault
  2. Employer and employee: course of employment, close-connection test
  3. Other relationships: principal and agent, partners
  4. State liability: Art. 300, Vidyawati vs Kasturilal
  5. Public-law compensation

Concept in brief

Vicarious liability makes one person liable for another's tort because of their relationship, chiefly employer and employee acting in the course of employment. It rests on policy (deep pocket, risk distribution, deterrence) rather than the employer's fault. For the State, the old sovereign-function immunity (Kasturilal) has been eroded; public law compensation for violation of Art. 21 is not subject to it.

Key provisions

  • Art. 300
  • Government of India Act, 1858, s. 65 (origin)

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Lloyd v. Grace, Smith & Co. (1912): Liability for fraud by clerk acting in apparent authority.
  • Century Insurance v. Northern Ireland Road Transport Board (1942): Negligent act in course of employment.
  • Lister v. Hesley Hall (2001): Close-connection test.
  • State of Rajasthan v. Vidyawati (1962): State liable like a private employer.
  • Kasturilal Ralia Ram Jain v. State of U.P. (1965): Sovereign immunity applied (later doubted).
  • N. Nagendra Rao v. State of A.P. (1994): Sovereign immunity limited.
  • Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar (1983): Compensation under Art. 32.
  • Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa (1993): Strict public law liability; immunity not a defence.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • First Law Commission Report (1956) on State liability in tort
  • Deep-pocket and risk-distribution theories
  • Gig platforms and liability for workers

Critical lens

The sovereign-function exception survives in doctrine but is narrowed in practice.

Close with

A statute on State liability, recommended since 1956, would bring coherence.

Previous-year questions (11)

Vicarious Liability

  1. 2026“‘Vicarious liability’ is based on public policy rather than fault.” Critically analyse the above statement in the light of decided cases. [P-II 2(a), 20 marks]
    How to approach: Critically analyse. Policy bases (deep pocket, control, enterprise risk); employer and employee tests; close-connection test (Lister); State liability (Vidyawati, Kasturilal, Nagendra Rao). Concede that fault still matters as to the servant.
  2. 2025Explain the principle of liability of the master for the torts committed by his servant. Write case laws.
  3. 2023"He who acts through another, does the act himself." Discuss the tortious liability entailed in the above statement.
  4. 2020"The common cases of vicarious liability relate to a servant driving his vehicle in the course of employment." Explain in the light of decided cases.
  5. 2018"A master is liable for all acts of his servant done during the course of employment." Explain it in general and from an Indian perspective in particular.
  6. 2017When is the principal not liable for the torts committed by his servant? Discuss.

State Liability

  1. 2024'The determination of vicarious liability of the state is linked with the negligence made by all its functionaries and no immunity can be claimed.' In light of the above observation, discuss the vicarious liability of the state with reference to its sovereign functions.
  2. 2022The 'State Liability' under the Law of Tort has undergone metamorphosis. Explain with the help of case laws.
  3. 2020The rule "Crown was not answerable for tort committed by its servant" has never been applied in India. Examine the statement in the light of the decided cases.
  4. 2015"State liability in Torts has undergone an acute transformation in recent times." Discuss in the light of the latest judicial pronouncements.
  5. 2014"Although the decision of the Supreme Court in Kasturi Lal v. State of U.P. has not been overruled as such, yet for all practical purposes its force has been considerably reduced." Elucidate.
