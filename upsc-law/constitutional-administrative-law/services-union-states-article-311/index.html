Framework (Part XIV, Arts. 308–323)

  • Art. 309: Parliament and State legislatures regulate recruitment and conditions of service; until then, rules made by the President or Governor apply.
  • Art. 310: the doctrine of pleasure: civil servants hold office during the pleasure of the President or Governor.
  • Art. 311: safeguards that qualify the pleasure doctrine.
  • Art. 312: all-India services (IAS, IPS, IFoS), created by Parliament on a Rajya Sabha resolution.

Article 311 safeguards

  1. Art. 311(1): no dismissal or removal by an authority subordinate to the appointing authority.
  2. Art. 311(2): no dismissal, removal or reduction in rank except after an inquiry where the person is told of the charges and given a reasonable opportunity of being heard. The 42nd Amendment removed the second opportunity to show cause against the proposed penalty.
  3. Exceptions (second proviso): (a) conviction on a criminal charge; (b) where it is not reasonably practicable to hold an inquiry (reasons must be recorded); (c) where the President or Governor is satisfied that an inquiry is not expedient in the interest of State security.

Leading cases

  • Parshotam Lal Dhingra v. Union of India (1958): Art. 311 applies only to punitive action. Tests: whether the person had a right to the post, and whether evil consequences (forfeiture of pay or seniority) follow.
  • Union of India v. Tulsiram Patel (1985, 5 judges): when an exception under the second proviso applies, the inquiry and natural justice are excluded altogether; the decision is open to review on limited grounds.
  • Union of India v. Mohd. Ramzan Khan (1991) and Managing Director, ECIL v. B. Karunakar (1993): the delinquent employee must be given a copy of the inquiry report before the disciplinary authority decides; failure vitiates the penalty only if prejudice is shown.
  • State of Orissa v. Dr. Binapani Dei (1967): even administrative orders with civil consequences require a hearing.
  • Union of India v. K.V. Jankiraman (1991): "sealed cover" procedure for promotion while disciplinary proceedings are pending.

Reservation in promotion

  • Art. 16(4A) (77th Amendment, 1995) and the consequential seniority clause (85th Amendment, 2001).
  • M. Nagaraj v. Union of India (2006): valid, but the State must show backwardness, inadequacy of representation and overall efficiency (Art. 335).
  • Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta (2018): no need to show backwardness of SCs/STs; creamy-layer exclusion applies to them in promotions.

Administrative tribunals

  • Arts. 323A and 323B (42nd Amendment, 1976): service tribunals and tribunals for other matters.
  • Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985: Central Administrative Tribunal and State tribunals.
  • S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India (1987): tribunals can be effective substitutes for High Courts.
  • L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997, 7 judges): tribunals are subject to High Court review under Arts. 226/227; appeals lie to a division bench of the High Court before the Supreme Court.
  • Madras Bar Association cases (2010, 2014, 2020, 2021): standards for independence of tribunal members (terms, age, selection). The Tribunal Reforms Act, 2021 re-enacted provisions the Court had struck down; challenges continued.

Public Service Commissions (Arts. 315–323)

  • UPSC and State PSCs conduct examinations and advise on recruitment, promotions and discipline (Art. 320).
  • State of U.P. v. Manbodhan Lal Srivastava (1957): consultation under Art. 320(3) is directory; failure does not invalidate the action.
  • Members can be removed only on the grounds and by the procedure in Art. 317 (inquiry by the Supreme Court for misbehaviour).

Revision points

  • Pleasure (Art. 310) is limited by Art. 311.
  • Dhingra (punitive test), Tulsiram Patel (exceptions exclude inquiry), B. Karunakar (copy of report).
  • Tribunals: Sampath Kumar → L. Chandra Kumar → Madras Bar Association.
  • Art. 320 consultation is directory (Manbodhan Lal).
Syllabus unit 9 · Asked 16 times · 2014–2026

Answer writing: Services under the Union and the States

Decode the demand. Expect the doctrine of pleasure and Art. 311 safeguards, tribunals, and the independence of UPSC and the Election Commission. Show that pleasure is constitutionally qualified.

Open with

The English doctrine of pleasure lets the Crown dismiss servants at will; in India Art. 310 adopts it only 'except as expressly provided by this Constitution'.

Answer frame

  1. Art. 310 and its limits
  2. Art. 311 safeguards and second-proviso exceptions (Tulsiram Patel)
  3. Tests of punitive termination (Dhingra)
  4. Tribunals (Art. 323A, L. Chandra Kumar)
  5. Constitutional bodies: UPSC, Election Commission

Concept in brief

Civil servants hold office during the pleasure of the President or Governor (Art. 310), but Art. 311 limits it: no dismissal by an authority subordinate to the appointing one, and no dismissal, removal or reduction in rank without an inquiry and reasonable opportunity, subject to Art. 311(2) provisos. The doctrine of pleasure in India is therefore not absolute. UPSC/SPSCs (Arts. 315–323) and the Election Commission (Art. 324) are the constitutional bodies here.

Key provisions

  • Arts. 308–323, 323A, 324
  • Central Civil Services (CCA) Rules, 1965

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Parshotam Lal Dhingra v. Union of India (1958): Tests to tell termination simpliciter from punitive removal.
  • Moti Ram Deka v. N.E. Frontier Railway (1964): Pleasure is subject to Art. 311 and other provisions.
  • Union of India v. Tulsiram Patel (1985): Art. 311(2) second proviso excludes inquiry; limited review.
  • Union of India v. Mohd. Ramzan Khan (1991): Delinquent entitled to inquiry report before punishment.
  • Managing Director, ECIL v. B. Karunakar (1993): Non-supply of report vitiates only if prejudice shown.
  • L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997): Tribunal decisions subject to HC writ jurisdiction.
  • Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India (2023): Selection committee for Election Commissioners till law made (since replaced by a 2023 Act).
  • Mohinder Singh Gill v. Chief Election Commissioner (1978): Art. 324 is a reservoir of power, subject to law and fairness.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • 2nd ARC 10th Report on personnel administration
  • Comparative: UK civil service at pleasure; US civil service protections
  • Public administration: Weberian bureaucracy and neutrality

Critical lens

Second-proviso dismissals without inquiry protect security but risk misuse; review is limited.

Close with

A secure but accountable civil service is a constitutional value in itself.

Previous-year questions (16)

(a) Recruitment and conditions of services; Constitutional safeguards; Administrative tribunals

  1. 2026“The ‘Doctrine of Pleasure’ in its absolute unrestricted application does not exist in India.” Elucidate with regard to service jurisprudence. [P-I 1(a), 10 marks]
    How to approach: Elucidate = explain with illustrations. Demand: show Art. 310 pleasure is qualified. Frame: English origin → Art. 310 'except as expressly provided' → Art. 311 safeguards → second-proviso exceptions → judicial review (Tulsiram Patel). Edge: contrast with UK Crown service.
  2. 2025"It is often said that the decision of the Supreme Court in L. Chandra Kumar Vs. Union of India (1997) has defeated the very raison d'être of establishing administrative tribunals in India." Discuss. [1b]
  3. 2024"Every person who is a member of the civil service of the Union holds office during the pleasure of the President." Is there any exception to this rule? Describe. [1c]
  4. 2023"The Constitution of India provides constitutional status and protection to civil servants." What protections have been secured for civil servants in India? Explain. [2c]
  5. 2022Are administrative tribunals competent to examine the constitutional validity of primary legislation? Discuss in the light of case law. [4b]
  6. 2018What are the constitutional safeguards available to a civil servant against dismissal, removal or reduction in rank of services? Are these rights also available to an employee of a public corporation? Discuss with reference to statutory provisions and case law. [3b]
  7. 2018(i): Give an overview of the growth of the Tribunals in India with special reference to Administrative Tribunals established under the Administrative Tribunal Act. [2a]
  8. 2017Answer the following in about 150 words each: Explain the phenomenon of tribunalisation of justice in India. [1d]
  9. 2014Define the term 'public servant'. Also, discuss the recruitment procedure of public servants in India. [2c]
  10. 2014The concept of 'Reasonable Opportunity' being a constitutional limitation on the doctrine of 'Tenure Pleasure', Parliament or State Legislature can make a law defining the content of 'Reasonable Opportunity' and prescribing procedure for affording the said opportunity to the accused government servant. Explain the concept with reference to leading cases. [2a]

(c) Election Commission: Power and functions

  1. 2023"Superintendence, direction and control of elections is vested in the office of the Election Commission and therefore, the appointment of Election Commissioner is of crucial importance in conducting free and fair elections." Critically examine the above statement with reference to recent judicial decisions. [3a]
  2. 2021"Free and fair election is the 'basic structure' of our Constitution and it is the 'heartbeat' of democracy." But widespread corruption and increasing criminalisation in the election process have made our democracy weak. Discuss the various efforts undertaken by the Election Commission to ensure free and fair election. [3a]
  3. 2018"The Election Commission of India is an autonomous constitutional authority responsible for administering the election process in India." Comment by explaining the powers and functions of the Election Commission of India. [3a]
  4. 2017Examine the powers and role of Election Commission of India in conducting free and fair election. [4b]
