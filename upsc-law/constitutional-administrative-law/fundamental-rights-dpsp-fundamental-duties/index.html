Fundamental Rights, DPSP and Fundamental Duties

Three parts, one design

  • Part III, Fundamental Rights: justiciable limits on the State.
  • Part IV, Directive Principles of State Policy (Arts. 36–51): not enforceable in court, but "fundamental in the governance of the country" and the State has a duty to apply them in making laws (Art. 37).
  • Part IV-A, Fundamental Duties (Art. 51A): duties of citizens, added by the 42nd Amendment (1976) on the Swaran Singh Committee's recommendation; the eleventh duty (education of children aged 6–14, Art. 51A(k)) came with the 86th Amendment (2002).

How the relationship evolved

  1. Strict primacy of rights. State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951): DPSPs must conform to and run subsidiary to Fundamental Rights. Parliament responded with the First Amendment (Art. 15(4)).
  2. Harmonious construction. In re Kerala Education Bill (1957): courts should give effect to both as far as possible.
  3. Protecting DPSP laws from rights challenges. The 25th Amendment (1971) inserted Art. 31C: laws giving effect to Art. 39(b) and (c) cannot be challenged under Arts. 14 and 19. Kesavananda Bharati (1973) upheld the first part but struck down the clause excluding judicial review.
  4. Balance as basic structure. The 42nd Amendment extended Art. 31C to all DPSPs. Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) struck this down: the Constitution rests on the balance between Parts III and IV; giving absolute primacy to one destroys the basic structure.
  5. DPSPs as content for rights. Courts now read DPSPs into Art. 21: right to education (Mohini Jain, 1992; Unni Krishnan, 1993, leading to Art. 21A), livelihood (Olga Tellis, 1985), humane working conditions (Bandhua Mukti Morcha, 1984), environment (Arts. 48A and 51A(g) read with Art. 21).

Article 39(b): what is a "material resource of the community"?

In Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra (2024, 9 judges) the majority held that not every privately owned resource is a "material resource of the community". Whether it is depends on the nature of the resource, its scarcity, its impact on community well-being and how it is held. The Court rejected the broad view taken by Justice Krishna Iyer in State of Karnataka v. Ranganatha Reddy (1977).

Uniform Civil Code (Art. 44)

The Court has repeatedly urged a uniform civil code (Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum, 1985; Sarla Mudgal v. Union of India, 1995), but courts cannot direct legislation. Goa has a common civil code; Uttarakhand enacted its Uniform Civil Code in 2024 (in force January 2025).

Fundamental Duties

  • Not directly enforceable, but courts use them to interpret laws and test reasonableness of restrictions (AIIMS Students' Union v. AIIMS, 2002).
  • The Justice J.S. Verma Committee (1999) listed existing laws that already give effect to many duties (e.g., the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971; environmental laws).
  • Duties sit naturally with rights: Art. 51A(g) (protect the environment) supports Art. 21 claims; Art. 51A(h) (scientific temper) is cited in debates on superstition laws.

Revision points

  • Champakam (rights first) → Kerala Education Bill (harmony) → Minerva Mills (balance is basic structure).
  • DPSPs give content to Art. 21 (education, livelihood, environment).
  • Art. 39(b): Property Owners Association (2024) narrowed "material resources".
  • Duties: 42nd Amendment (10), 86th Amendment (11th); Verma Committee 1999.
Syllabus unit 3 · Asked 13 times · 2013–2026

Answer writing: Relationship between Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles, and Fundamental Duties

Decode the demand. Questions ask how FRs, DPSPs and Fundamental Duties fit together, or test definitions such as 'State' under Art. 36. Show the shift from conflict to harmony with dated cases.

Open with

Parts III and IV are the conscience of the Constitution (Austin); the judiciary has moved them from conflict to complementarity.

Answer frame

  1. Nature: justiciable vs non-justiciable (Art. 37)
  2. Phase of conflict (Champakam, Golaknath)
  3. Amendments: 25th (Art. 31C) and 42nd
  4. Phase of harmony (Kesavananda, Minerva Mills)
  5. DPSPs and duties as interpretive aids (Unni Krishnan, Mirzapur)

Concept in brief

Fundamental Rights are justiciable; Directive Principles are not (Art. 37) but are 'fundamental in governance'. Early courts gave rights primacy (Champakam); the settled view is harmony: DPSPs inform the content of rights and the reasonableness of restrictions, and Fundamental Duties (Art. 51A) may be used to interpret laws.

Key provisions

  • Art. 36 ('State' same as Art. 12 unless context otherwise)
  • Arts. 37–51
  • Art. 31C
  • Art. 51A

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951): DPSPs must conform to Fundamental Rights.
  • Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980): Harmony and balance between Parts III and IV is a basic feature; extended Art. 31C struck down.
  • Unni Krishnan J.P. v. State of A.P. (1993): Right to education up to 14 read into Art. 21 using Art. 45.
  • State of Gujarat v. Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kassab Jamat (2005): DPSPs and duties support reasonableness of a total cow-slaughter ban.
  • AIIMS Students' Union v. AIIMS (2002): Fundamental Duties can guide interpretation and policy.
  • Ranganath Mishra v. Union of India (2003): Directions to spread awareness of Fundamental Duties.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Irish Constitution (1937) as the source of DPSPs
  • Swaran Singh Committee (1976) and Fundamental Duties
  • Justice Verma Committee (1999) on operationalising duties
  • Welfare economics: DPSPs as a social-rights floor (Art. 21A came from Art. 45)

Critical lens

Harmony reads well, but courts enforcing DPSPs through Art. 21 raise separation-of-powers and budget concerns.

Close with

Rights without directives would be formal, and directives without rights would be paternalist; their balance is itself a basic feature.

Previous-year questions (13)
  1. 2026The word ‘State’ used in the ‘Directive Principles’ has the same meaning as has been given to it by Article 12, for the purposes of enforcement of the Fundamental Rights. Examine in the light of exceptions, if any. [P-I 1(d), 10 marks]
    How to approach: Examine = probe the claim and its exceptions. Art. 36 adopts Art. 12's meaning 'unless the context otherwise requires'. Exceptions: some DPSPs bind only the legislature or a particular level (Art. 40 village panchayats, Art. 50 judiciary).

Do you agree with the view that giving primacy to one over the other is to disturb the harmony of the Constitution? Comment with reference to the present-day scenario relating to education, health, religion, etc. [4c]
