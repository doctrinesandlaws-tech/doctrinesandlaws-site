Office of the President

  • Election (Arts. 54–55): by an electoral college of elected members of both Houses of Parliament and of State Legislative Assemblies (including Delhi and Puducherry), by proportional representation through the single transferable vote. Disputes go to the Supreme Court (Art. 71).
  • Term and removal: five years (Art. 56); impeachment for "violation of the Constitution" by a two-thirds majority of the total membership of each House (Art. 61).
  • Executive power of the Union vests in the President (Art. 53) and extends to matters on which Parliament can legislate (Art. 73).

Aid and advice: a constitutional head

  • Art. 74(1): a Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister aids and advises the President. The 42nd Amendment (1976) added that the President "shall act in accordance with" such advice. The 44th Amendment (1978) allowed the President to send advice back once for reconsideration, after which it binds.
  • Art. 74(2): courts cannot inquire whether, and what, advice was tendered. Documents behind the advice may still be examined (S.R. Bommai, 1994).
  • Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab (1955): India follows the British cabinet system; real executive power lies with ministers.
  • Samsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974, 7 judges): the President and Governors exercise powers on ministerial advice except in a narrow class of situations.
  • Situations where the President has some personal choice: choosing a Prime Minister when no party has a majority, dismissing a government that has lost confidence but refuses to resign, and dissolving the Lok Sabha when a government has lost its majority.

The Council of Ministers

  • Appointed by the President on the Prime Minister's advice (Art. 75(1)); size capped at 15% of the Lok Sabha by the 91st Amendment (2003) (Art. 75(1A)).
  • Collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha (Art. 75(3)); individual responsibility to the President ("pleasure", Art. 75(2)).
  • The Prime Minister must keep the President informed (Art. 78).

Pardoning power (Art. 72)

  • Covers pardons, reprieves, respites, remissions, suspension and commutation, including in court-martial cases and death sentences.
  • Exercised on ministerial advice (Maru Ram v. Union of India, 1980).
  • Kehar Singh v. Union of India (1989): the President may examine the evidence afresh; there is no right to an oral hearing.
  • Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of A.P. (2006): limited judicial review on grounds such as non-application of mind, mala fides, irrelevant or extraneous considerations, or arbitrariness.
  • Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India (2014): unexplained delay in deciding mercy petitions is a ground to commute a death sentence.

Ordinance power (Art. 123)

  • Available only when Parliament is not in session and the President is satisfied that immediate action is necessary.
  • The 38th Amendment (1975) made this satisfaction non-justiciable; the 44th Amendment removed that bar.
  • D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987): routine re-promulgation of ordinances is a fraud on the Constitution.
  • Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017, 7 judges): laying an ordinance before the legislature is mandatory; satisfaction is open to limited review; rights created by a lapsed ordinance do not automatically survive.

Assent to bills (Art. 111)

Assent, withholding assent, or (for non-money bills) returning the bill once for reconsideration. There is no fixed time limit, which permits the so-called "pocket veto" (President Zail Singh and the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986).

Revision points

  • Art. 74 after 42nd and 44th Amendments: advice binding, one reconsideration.
  • Samsher Singh: constitutional head; discretion is the exception.
  • Pardon: Maru Ram, Kehar Singh, Epuru Sudhakar, Shatrughan Chauhan.
  • Ordinances: D.C. Wadhwa (no re-promulgation) and Krishna Kumar Singh (laying is mandatory).
Syllabus unit 4 · Asked 13 times · 2014–2026

Answer writing: Constitutional Position of the President and relation with the Council of Ministers

Decode the demand. Expect questions on how far the President is bound by advice, on the election and disputes procedure, or on pardon and ordinances. Answer with constitutional articles first, then cases.

Open with

Ambedkar described the Indian President as occupying the same position as the King under the English Constitution: the head of the State, not of the executive.

Answer frame

  1. Position: nominal head (Arts. 53, 74)
  2. Aid and advice after the 42nd and 44th Amendments
  3. Situational discretion (hung House, Art. 74(1) proviso, Art. 78)
  4. Election (Arts. 54, 55) and disputes (Art. 71)
  5. Judicial review of ordinances and pardons

Concept in brief

The President is the constitutional head; executive power is exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, which is binding after the 42nd and 44th Amendments (Art. 74(1), with one reconsideration). The President is elected indirectly by an electoral college of elected MPs and MLAs (Arts. 54–55); all election disputes are decided exclusively by the Supreme Court (Art. 71).

Key provisions

  • Arts. 52–62, 71, 72, 74, 75, 78, 123
  • Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections Act, 1952

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab (1955): Cabinet system; executive power is residuary, not limited to legislation.
  • Samsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974): President and Governor act on ministerial advice save in exceptional situations.
  • U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi (1971): A Council of Ministers must always exist, even after dissolution.
  • Kehar Singh v. Union of India (1989): Pardon power under Art. 72 is subject to limited judicial review.
  • Epuru Sudhakar v. Govt. of A.P. (2006): Clemency open to review for mala fides or irrelevant considerations.
  • Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017): Re-promulgation of ordinances is a fraud on the Constitution.
  • Charan Lal Sahu v. Giani Zail Singh (1984): Only candidates/qualified electors may challenge a presidential election under the 1952 Act.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Comparative: US executive presidency vs Westminster model
  • Practice: presidential returns of Bills (e.g. the 1986 Postal Bill pocket veto)
  • Constituent Assembly debates on an indirect election

Critical lens

Formal constraint coexists with real influence through Art. 78 information rights, timing and pocket veto.

Close with

The President's strength lies in being a constitutional conscience, not a rival centre of power.

  1. 2026The President in India is elected not directly by the people, but by the method of indirect election. Explain. Who will decide in case any dispute arises in connection with the election of the President? Discuss. [P-I 1(e), 10 marks]
    How to approach: Two-part factual answer. Electoral college of elected MPs and MLAs (Art. 54), value of votes and proportional representation by single transferable vote (Art. 55). Disputes go exclusively to the Supreme Court (Art. 71) under the 1952 Act; a vacancy in the college is no ground (Art. 71(4)).

Critically examine the above statement in the context of the status and position of the President of India under the Indian Constitution. Also, answer, if the President of India does not accept the advice of the Prime Minister, what consequences would follow? [1e]

  13. 2014Examine and elucidate the constitutional scope of the Ordinance-making power of the President and the Governors in India. [3a]
