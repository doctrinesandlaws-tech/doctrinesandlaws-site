Who must respect Fundamental Rights: "State" under Article 12

Art. 12 covers the Government and Parliament of India, State governments and legislatures, local authorities and "other authorities".

  • Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib (1981): an agency is an "instrumentality of the State" if, among other things, its share capital is held by government, it gets financial assistance covering most of its expenses, it enjoys a State-conferred monopoly, or there is deep and pervasive State control.
  • Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology (2002, 7 judges): the test is whether the body is financially, functionally and administratively dominated by government.
  • Zee Telefilms v. Union of India (2005): BCCI is not "State", though it may be amenable to writ jurisdiction under Art. 226 because it performs public functions.
  • Kaushal Kishor v. State of U.P. (2023): rights under Arts. 19 and 21 can be enforced even against non-State actors (horizontal application).

Article 13: laws inconsistent with rights

  • Doctrine of eclipse (Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of M.P., 1955): a pre-constitutional law inconsistent with a right is not dead, only eclipsed; it revives when the right is amended.
  • Severability (R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla v. Union of India, 1957): only the offending part is struck down if it can be separated.
  • No waiver of fundamental rights (Basheshar Nath v. CIT, 1959).

The core rights in brief

  • Art. 14: reasonable classification (intelligible differentia + rational nexus; State of W.B. v. Anwar Ali Sarkar, 1952) and non-arbitrariness (E.P. Royappa v. State of T.N., 1974). Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017) applied "manifest arbitrariness" to strike down instant triple talaq.
  • Arts. 15–16: special provisions and reservations. Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992): 50% ceiling, creamy layer exclusion for OBCs, no reservation in promotions (later altered by Art. 16(4A)). Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022) upheld the 10% EWS quota (103rd Amendment). State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (2024, 7 judges) allowed sub-classification within Scheduled Castes.
  • Art. 19: six freedoms, subject to reasonable restrictions in Arts. 19(2)–(6). Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) struck down s. 66A of the IT Act for vagueness; Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020) recognised the use of the internet for speech and trade and required proportionality for shutdowns.
  • Art. 21: after Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), the "procedure established by law" must be just, fair and reasonable. Art. 21 now includes livelihood (Olga Tellis, 1985), privacy (Puttaswamy, 2017), dignity in death (Common Cause, 2018) and a healthy environment.
  • Art. 21A: free and compulsory education for children aged 6–14 (86th Amendment, 2002; RTE Act, 2009).
  • Art. 22: safeguards on arrest and preventive detention.
  • Art. 32: the right to move the Supreme Court; Dr. Ambedkar called it the "heart and soul" of the Constitution.

Public interest litigation (PIL)

PIL relaxed two rules: locus standi and procedure.

  • S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981): any member of the public acting in good faith may approach the court for those who cannot (the poor, the disadvantaged).
  • Epistolary jurisdiction: letters treated as writ petitions (Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration, 1980; Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India, 1984).
  • Remedies widened: continuing mandamus, court-appointed commissions, guidelines where law is silent (Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan, 1997, sexual harassment at work).
  • Limits: PIL cannot be used for private or political ends or to review economic policy (BALCO Employees' Union v. Union of India, 2002). State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010) laid down guidelines to screen frivolous PILs.

Legal aid and Legal Services Authorities

  • Art. 39A (42nd Amendment, 1976): the State shall provide free legal aid so that no citizen is denied justice for economic or other disabilities.
  • Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, State of Bihar (1979): speedy trial and free legal aid are part of Art. 21.
  • Khatri v. State of Bihar (1981): the Magistrate must tell an accused of the right to free legal aid; Suk Das v. Union Territory of Arunachal Pradesh (1986): a trial without offering legal aid is vitiated.
  • Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (in force from 9 November 1995): - Structure: NALSA (chaired by the Chief Justice of India), State and District Legal Services Authorities, Taluk Legal Services Committees, and committees in the Supreme Court and High Courts. - Eligibility (s. 12): members of SC/ST, victims of trafficking, women, children, persons with disability, victims of mass disaster, industrial workmen, persons in custody, and those below the income limit. - Lok Adalats (ss. 19–22): an award is deemed a decree of a civil court and is final; no appeal lies (s. 21). - Permanent Lok Adalats for public utility services (Chapter VI-A, added in 2002) can decide a dispute on merits if conciliation fails.
  • BNSS s. 341 (earlier CrPC s. 304): legal aid to an accused at State expense in a trial before the Court of Session. NALSA's Legal Aid Defense Counsel System (2022) gives district-level public-defender style offices.

Revision points

  • Art. 12 test: Ajay Hasia → Pradeep Kumar Biswas; horizontal rights: Kaushal Kishor (2023).
  • Art. 21 after Maneka: fair, just and reasonable procedure.
  • PIL: S.P. Gupta (standing) → Bandhua Mukti Morcha (letters) → Balwant Singh Chaufal (screening).
  • Legal aid: Art. 39A + Hussainara Khatoon + LSA Act 1987 (Lok Adalat award = decree, no appeal).
Syllabus unit 2 · Asked 33 times · 2013–2026

Answer writing: Fundamental Rights—Public interest litigation; Legal Aid; Legal Services Authority

Decode the demand. This is the most-asked unit. Questions test a doctrine's evolution (Art. 12 tests, eclipse and severability, the golden triangle, reservation), or ask you to critique judicial creativity. Always trace evolution case by case, then evaluate.

Open with

Fundamental Rights are not static guarantees; their content has been built case by case, from Gopalan's textual reading to Maneka Gandhi's fair-procedure reading and Puttaswamy's dignity-centred one.

Answer frame

  1. Constitutional text and purpose
  2. Evolution in three phases (formal → substantive → dignity and proportionality)
  3. Leading cases with the ratio in one line each
  4. Critical appraisal: judicial creativity vs restraint
  5. Way forward

Concept in brief

Part III guarantees enforceable rights against the 'State' (Art. 12), with laws inconsistent with them void to the extent of inconsistency (Art. 13). Courts have expanded Art. 14 (arbitrariness), Art. 19 (proportionality) and Art. 21 (dignity, privacy, livelihood). PIL relaxed standing so that courts could reach the poor; legal aid (Art. 39A, Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987) is part of a fair procedure under Art. 21.

Key provisions

  • Arts. 12–35
  • Art. 32 and 226 (writs)
  • Art. 39A
  • Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, ss. 12, 19–22 (Lok Adalats)

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib (1981): Instrumentality test for 'State'; arbitrariness violates Art. 14.
  • Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology (2002): Deep and pervasive government control decides Art. 12 status.
  • Zee Telefilms v. Union of India (2005): BCCI is not 'State', though amenable to Art. 226.
  • Kaushal Kishor v. State of U.P. (2023): Arts. 19 and 21 can be enforced against non-State actors.
  • Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of M.P. (1955): Doctrine of eclipse for pre-Constitution laws.
  • E.P. Royappa v. State of T.N. (1974): Equality is antithetic to arbitrariness.
  • Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992): OBC reservation upheld; creamy layer; 50% ceiling.
  • Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022): EWS reservation (103rd Amendment) upheld 3:2.
  • State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (2024): Sub-classification of Scheduled Castes permissible; E.V. Chinnaiah overruled.
  • Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978): Procedure under Art. 21 must be fair, just and reasonable; Arts. 14, 19, 21 interlinked.
  • K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017): Privacy is a fundamental right; ADM Jabalpur overruled.
  • Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015): S. 66A IT Act struck down as vague and overbroad.
  • Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020): Internet restrictions must meet proportionality and be published.
  • T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka (2002): Minority status decided State-wise; autonomy of unaided minority institutions.
  • Aligarh Muslim University v. Naresh Agarwal (2024): Seven judges overruled Azeez Basha on the test for 'established' by a minority.
  • S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981): Liberalised standing; foundation of PIL.
  • Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979): Speedy trial and free legal aid are part of Art. 21.
  • M.H. Hoskot v. State of Maharashtra (1978): Right to free legal aid for appeal; copy of judgment.
  • Khatri (II) v. State of Bihar (1981): Magistrate must inform an accused of the right to free legal aid.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Comparative: US 'due process' and the Lochner era, which explains the Constituent Assembly's choice of 'procedure established by law'
  • Jurisprudence: Dworkin's rights as trumps; proportionality from German constitutional law
  • Data: NCRB undertrial figures for Art. 21 and legal-aid answers
  • Policy: NALSA schemes, Lok Adalat disposal figures, Tele-Law

Draw this

A.K. Gopalan (1950) → R.C. Cooper (1970) → Maneka Gandhi (1978) → Puttaswamy (2017)

Critical lens

Expansion has empowered citizens but has also produced vague rights and uneven enforcement; PIL has helped and been misused.

Close with

The test of a right is its remedy; strengthening legal aid and timely adjudication is what turns Part III from promise to practice.

Right to Life and Personal Liberty (Art. 21 & 21A)

  1. 2026“While interpreting Article 21 of the Constitution, the Supreme Court introduced the concept of ‘due process of law’ indirectly into the Constitution of India, and thereby expanded its scope considerably.” Support your answer with the help of leading case laws. [P-I 2(a), 20 marks]
    How to approach: Support = give evidence for the claim. Frame: Constituent Assembly rejected 'due process' → Gopalan → R.C. Cooper → Maneka Gandhi (fair, just, reasonable) → Mohd. Arif (2014) and Puttaswamy applying substantive review. Edge: US due-process history. Close: Art. 21 is now India's due-process clause in all but name.
  2. 2025If Article 21 confers on a person the right to live a dignified life, does it also include a right not to live? Examine the Constitutional Provisions with the help of decided case laws. [1d]
  3. 2021"Right to Education is the base for the Fundamental Rights and Human Rights." Discuss the efforts made by the Government with regard to the Right to Education of the children. [1c]
  4. 2018"Right to Privacy is protected as an intrinsic part of life and personal liberty enshrined under Article 21 of the Constitution of India." Elucidate this statement in the light of the decision of Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India. [1e]
  5. 2017Discuss the importance of 'Right to life and personal liberty' with reference to recent case laws. [1a]
  6. 2015"Right to life means something more than mere animal existence." Discuss with reference to case law. Is there any difference between the expression 'right to life' and 'personal liberty? Explain critically. [2a]

  1. 2022'Absolute equality may itself be a cause of inequality.' In light of this statement, discuss substantive equality. [1a]
  2. 2020Examine the scope of protective discrimination offered to persons belonging to Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes under Articles 15 and 16 of the Constitution of India. Explain with the help of decided cases. [3a]

A backward class may, however, be identified on the basis of occupation-cum-income without any reference to caste.
