What it is and why it grows

Delegated (subordinate) legislation is law made by the executive (rules, regulations, notifications, bye-laws) under power given by a statute. It grows because of:

  • pressure on parliamentary time
  • technical subject matter needing expertise
  • flexibility and speed (to respond to changing conditions)
  • emergencies and experiments (pilot schemes)

How far can a legislature delegate?

  • Essential legislative functions cannot be delegated: the legislature must lay down the policy and standards.
  • In re Delhi Laws Act (1951, 7 judges): the leading opinion; delegation is permissible but the legislature cannot abdicate its essential function or repeal laws through the delegate.
  • Rajnarain Singh v. Chairman, Patna Administration Committee (1954): the delegate can modify a law only so long as it does not change the essential policy.
  • Hamdard Dawakhana v. Union of India (1960): delegation without guidance (uncanalised power) is invalid.
  • Devi Das Gopal Krishnan v. State of Punjab (1967): power to fix sales tax rates without guidance was excessive delegation.
  • Gwalior Rayon Silk Mfg. v. Assistant Commissioner of Sales Tax (1974): two approaches: the majority (Khanna J.) applied the "policy and guidelines" test; Mathew J. preferred the "abdication" test (delegation is valid so long as the legislature retains control and can repeal).

Conditional legislation

The legislature makes the law complete and leaves only the time or territory of application to the executive (Queen v. Burah, Privy Council, 1878). It is not delegation in the strict sense.

Henry VIII clause

A clause allowing the executive to amend the parent Act itself or remove difficulties. Courts accept narrow "removal of difficulties" clauses but strike down those allowing changes to the policy of the Act (Jalan Trading Co. v. Mill Mazdoor Sabha, 1967).

Sub-delegation

Delegatus non potest delegare: a delegate cannot further delegate unless the statute permits it expressly or by necessary implication.

Control of delegated legislation

  1. Parliamentary control - Laying before Parliament (simple laying, laying subject to negative or affirmative resolution). - Committees on Subordinate Legislation in both Houses scrutinise rules. - Atlas Cycle Industries v. State of Haryana (1979): a laying requirement may be directory, so failure does not always invalidate rules.
  2. Judicial control: rules are invalid if they are: - ultra vires the parent Act (substantive or procedural) - contrary to the Constitution, or the parent Act itself is unconstitutional - manifestly arbitrary or unreasonable (Indian Express Newspapers v. Union of India, 1985, listed the grounds for challenging subordinate legislation) - in conflict with other statutes, or imposing taxes without clear authority
  3. Procedural control: prior publication, consultation and publication in the Gazette. Harla v. State of Rajasthan (1951): a law must be published before it can bind.

Contemporary issues

  • Large framework statutes leave key choices to rules (for example, the DPDP Act, 2023 and the Telecommunications Act, 2023), which raises concerns about excessive delegation.
  • Committees on Subordinate Legislation meet rarely; most rules go unscrutinised.

Revision points

  • Essential function rule: Delhi Laws Act → Hamdard Dawakhana → Gwalior Rayon.
  • Conditional legislation (Queen v. Burah) ≠ delegation.
  • Controls: laying (Atlas Cycle), judicial (Indian Express grounds), publication (Harla).

Answer writing: Delegated legislation and its constitutionality

Decode the demand. Questions ask for the limits of permissible delegation and controls on it. Lead with the essential-function test and support it with cases.

Open with

Delegated legislation is a practical necessity in the administrative state, but the legislature must not abdicate its essential function of laying down policy.

Answer frame

  1. Reasons for delegation
  2. Permissible limits (Delhi Laws Act, Hamdard Dawakhana)
  3. Judicial control: ultra vires, arbitrariness
  4. Legislative and procedural controls
  5. Concerns: Henry VIII clauses, sub-delegation

Concept in brief

Legislatures may delegate subordinate rule-making but cannot give away essential legislative functions: the policy and standards must be laid down in the parent Act, and the delegate fills in details. Delegated legislation can be struck down if ultra vires the parent Act or the Constitution, manifestly arbitrary, or not published; legislative committees and laying procedures add political control.

Key provisions

  • Art. 13(3)(a) ('law' includes rules, orders)
  • General Clauses Act, 1897, s. 23
  • Committees on Subordinate Legislation

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • In re Delhi Laws Act (1951): Delegation permissible; essential legislative functions cannot be delegated.
  • Hamdard Dawakhana v. Union of India (1960): Delegation without policy is excessive and void.
  • Devi Das Gopal Krishnan v. State of Punjab (1967): Uncanalised power to fix tax rates struck down.
  • Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Birla Cotton Mills (1968): Delegation to local bodies judged liberally.
  • Gwalior Rayon Silk v. Asst. Commissioner (1974): Policy-and-guideline test applied.
  • Indian Express Newspapers v. Union of India (1985): Grounds to challenge subordinate legislation.
  • Harla v. State of Rajasthan (1951): Law must be published to bind.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • UK Donoughmore Committee on Ministers' Powers (1932)
  • Committees on Subordinate Legislation of both Houses
  • Data: rules notified vs rules laid on the table (PRS)

Critical lens

Courts are generous on delegation but strict on taxation and penal powers.

Close with

Strong parliamentary scrutiny of rules is the most democratic control on delegated power.
