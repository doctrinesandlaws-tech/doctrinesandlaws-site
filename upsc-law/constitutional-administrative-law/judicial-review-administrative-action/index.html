Why courts review administrative action

Administrative bodies make decisions with large discretion. Judicial review does not ask whether the decision is right, but whether it was made lawfully: within power, by a fair procedure and reasonably.

Grounds of review

Lord Diplock's classification in Council of Civil Service Unions v. Minister for the Civil Service (GCHQ case, 1985):

  1. Illegality: the authority misunderstood or exceeded its power. Includes: - acting without jurisdiction or beyond the statute (ultra vires) - relying on irrelevant considerations or ignoring relevant ones - improper purpose and mala fides - acting under dictation (Commissioner of Police v. Gordhandas Bhanji, 1952) - fettering discretion by rigid policy
  2. Irrationality: a decision so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could have made it (Associated Provincial Picture Houses v. Wednesbury Corporation, 1948).
  3. Procedural impropriety: breach of natural justice or of statutory procedure.
  4. Proportionality (flagged by Lord Diplock as a possible fourth ground): now part of Indian law.

Proportionality in India

  • Om Kumar v. Union of India (2001): proportionality applies where Fundamental Rights are affected; Wednesbury for other administrative action.
  • Modern Dental College v. State of M.P. (2016) and K.S. Puttaswamy (2017): four-part test: legitimate aim, suitability (rational connection), necessity (least restrictive means) and balancing.
  • Anuradha Bhasin (2020) applied it to internet shutdowns.

Remedies: the writs

WritPurposeAgainst
Habeas corpusProduce a detained person; test legality of detentionState and private persons
MandamusCommand performance of a public dutyPublic authorities (not the President/Governor personally, not private persons except for public duties)

Limits on review

  • Alternative remedy: not an absolute bar; writs lie despite an alternative remedy where there is a breach of fundamental rights or natural justice, an order without jurisdiction, or a challenge to the vires of a statute (Whirlpool Corporation v. Registrar of Trade Marks, 1998).
  • Contracts and tenders: limited review to prevent arbitrariness, bias or mala fides (Tata Cellular v. Union of India, 1994).
  • Policy matters: courts defer on economic and technical policy unless it is arbitrary or unconstitutional (BALCO, 2002).
  • Private bodies performing public duties are amenable to Art. 226 (Andi Mukta Sadguru Trust v. V.R. Rudani, 1989).

Related doctrines

  • Legitimate expectation: a public authority should honour its promises or established practice unless overriding public interest requires otherwise (Navjyoti Co-op Group Housing Society v. Union of India, 1992).
  • Promissory estoppel against the government (Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v. State of U.P., 1979).
  • Doctrine of public trust for natural resources (M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath, 1997).

Revision points

  • CCSU grounds + proportionality.
  • Wednesbury vs proportionality: Om Kumar (2001), Modern Dental College (2016).
  • Whirlpool (alternative remedy exceptions); Tata Cellular (tenders); Andi Mukta (private bodies).
Syllabus unit 15 · Asked 11 times · 2016–2026

Answer writing: Judicial review of administrative action

Decode the demand. Questions test grounds of review, proportionality vs Wednesbury, and the place of judicial review in the basic structure.

Open with

Judicial review asks not whether the decision was right, but whether it was lawfully made.

Answer frame

  1. Constitutional basis (Arts. 32, 226, 227, 136)
  2. Grounds: illegality, irrationality, procedural impropriety
  3. Proportionality and legitimate expectation
  4. Limits: policy, contracts, national security
  5. Judicial review as basic structure

Concept in brief

Courts review the decision-making process, not the merits. Grounds are illegality, irrationality (Wednesbury unreasonableness) and procedural impropriety (GCHQ), with proportionality now applied where rights are affected. Legitimate expectation and the doctrine against fettering discretion also protect fairness.

Key provisions

  • Arts. 32, 136, 226, 227

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Associated Provincial Picture Houses v. Wednesbury Corp. (1948): Unreasonableness standard.
  • Council of Civil Service Unions v. Minister for the Civil Service (1985): Three grounds of review.
  • Barium Chemicals v. Company Law Board (1967): Subjective satisfaction must rest on relevant material.
  • Tata Cellular v. Union of India (1994): Limited review of contracts and tenders.
  • Om Kumar v. Union of India (2001): Proportionality and Wednesbury in India.
  • Food Corporation of India v. Kamdhenu Cattle Feed (1993): Legitimate expectation forms part of non-arbitrariness.
  • Modern Dental College v. State of M.P. (2016): Four-part proportionality test.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Comparative: UK GCHQ grounds; German proportionality; US Chevron deference (overruled in Loper Bright, 2024)
  • Administrative science: discretion and accountability

Critical lens

Deference in policy and economic matters is wise, but it must not become abdication when rights are at stake.

Close with

Without judicial review, the Constitution would cease to be what it is (Chandra Kumar).

Previous-year questions (11)

    How to approach: Critically evaluate = agree, then test limits. Demand: judicial review as basic structure. Frame: Arts. 13, 32, 226 → Kesavananda, Minerva Mills, L. Chandra Kumar → limits and self-restraint. Close with Ambedkar on Art. 32 as the 'heart and soul'.
  2. 2025"In any democratic society, judicial review of administrative action is the soul of the system. Without it, democracy and the rule of law cannot be maintained." Explain with example. [3a]

  4. 2022Elucidate 'Wednesbury's Principles of Unreasonableness'. Do these principles provide in any way, scope for 'merits review' of administrative decisions? [1e]
  5. 2020Make a distinction between judicial review and judicial power. Explain the scope of judicial review with reference to the cases arising under the Xth Schedule of the Constitution. [1b]
  6. 2018(ii): Examine and explain the following statements: (ii) Judicial Activism has both positive and negative impacts on the Judiciary. [4c]
  7. 2018What do you mean by the term 'Rule of Law'? The basic element of Dicey's doctrine of the rule of law is that, be you ever so high, the law is above you. Discuss with the help of decided cases. [4a]
  8. 2018Administrative powers/actions are not always in conflict with the 'rule of law' principle. Discuss with illustration. [1b]
  9. 2017Briefly explain the grounds on which administrative actions can be subjected to judicial review. [1e]
  10. 2016"The Policy and Guideline Theory presupposes delivery of justice by quasi-judicial authority." Elaborate it. [4c]
  11. 2016"The 'Rule of Law' is based on the principle of legality and is opposed to the exercise of arbitrary powers." Discuss. Explain whether failure to give reasons amounts to exercising power arbitrarily. [4a]
