Privileges: why and where

Legislators need freedom to speak and vote without fear, and each House needs control over its own proceedings. Arts. 105 (Parliament) and 194 (State legislatures) give:

  • Freedom of speech in the House, subject to the Constitution and the rules of procedure (Art. 105(1)).
  • Immunity from court proceedings for anything said or any vote given in the House or a committee (Art. 105(2)).
  • Other privileges "as may be defined by law", and until then those of the House of Commons (Art. 105(3)). Parliament has never codified them.

Leading cases

  • M.S.M. Sharma v. Sri Krishna Sinha (Searchlight case, 1959): privileges under Art. 194(3) prevail over Art. 19(1)(a) (a press report of expunged remarks).
  • In re Special Reference No. 1 of 1964 (Keshav Singh): the legislature cannot punish a judge for entertaining a habeas corpus petition; courts can examine the existence and extent of a privilege.
  • Raja Ram Pal v. Hon'ble Speaker, Lok Sabha (2007): expulsion of MPs in the cash-for-query case upheld, but such actions remain open to judicial review on limited grounds (gross illegality, violation of rights).
  • P.V. Narasimha Rao v. State (1998): MPs who took bribes to vote were immune; this was overruled.
  • Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024, 7 judges): there is no immunity for bribery. The offence is complete when the bribe is taken, regardless of how the member later votes; bribery is not necessary for the functioning of the House.
  • Kalpana Mehta v. Union of India (2018): courts may refer to parliamentary committee reports, but cannot question their correctness.

Courts and legislative proceedings

  • Arts. 122 and 212: courts cannot question proceedings on the ground of an "irregularity of procedure", but can examine illegality or unconstitutionality (Raja Ram Pal).
  • Money bills (Art. 110): the Speaker's certificate is final under Art. 110(3). In K.S. Puttaswamy (Aadhaar) (2018) the majority upheld passing the Aadhaar Act as a money bill; Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank (2019) referred the question to a larger bench.

Anti-defection law (Tenth Schedule)

  • Added by the 52nd Amendment (1985); the 91st Amendment (2003) deleted the "split" exception, leaving only mergers (two-thirds of the legislature party).
  • Grounds: voluntarily giving up party membership, or voting or abstaining against the party whip without permission.
  • Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992): the Speaker acts as a tribunal and the decision is subject to judicial review; para 7 (barring courts) was struck down for want of State ratification.
  • Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur (2020): Speakers should decide disqualification petitions within three months.
  • Subhash Desai (2023): the Speaker must decide which group is the political party by looking at the party constitution and the legislature party is not the political party.

Codification debate

Supporters argue codification brings certainty and protects press freedom; opponents fear rigidity and judicial interference. The National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (2002) recommended defining privileges.

Revision points

  • Art. 105(2) immunity ≠ immunity for bribery: Sita Soren (2024) overruled P.V. Narasimha Rao (1998).
  • Courts can test existence of a privilege (Keshav Singh) and review expulsion (Raja Ram Pal).
  • Tenth Schedule: Kihoto Hollohan → Keisham Meghachandra (3 months) → Subhash Desai.
Syllabus unit 8 · Asked 7 times · 2017–2026

Answer writing: Legislative powers, privileges, and immunities

Decode the demand. Questions cover privileges vs fundamental rights, bribery and immunity, and the legislative process including Money Bills and Parliament's power over UTs. State the article, then the tension with Part III.

Open with

Legislative privileges exist so that the House can function freely; they are not personal immunities of members.

Answer frame

  1. Sources (Arts. 105, 194) and content
  2. Privilege vs fundamental rights (Searchlight, Keshav Singh)
  3. Bribery and immunity: P.V. Narasimha Rao overruled by Sita Soren (2024)
  4. Legislative process: ordinary, money and financial Bills
  5. Codification debate

Concept in brief

Parliament and State legislatures enjoy privileges (Arts. 105, 194) to function freely: free speech in the House, immunity for votes and speeches, and power to punish for contempt. Courts accept privileges but review whether a claimed privilege exists and whether it is exercised constitutionally. The legislative process (Arts. 107–122) distinguishes ordinary, money (Art. 110) and financial Bills.

Key provisions

  • Arts. 105, 107–122, 194, 196–212
  • Tenth Schedule

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • M.S.M. Sharma v. Sri Krishna Sinha (1959): Privilege of keeping proceedings private prevails over Art. 19(1)(a).
  • In re Keshav Singh (Special Reference 1 of 1964): Courts can examine the legality of committal for contempt of the House.
  • Tej Kiran Jain v. N. Sanjiva Reddy (1970): Absolute immunity for anything said in Parliament.
  • Raja Ram Pal v. Speaker, Lok Sabha (2007): Expulsion upheld; privilege actions open to limited review.
  • Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024): Bribery is not protected by legislative privilege; P.V. Narasimha Rao (1998) overruled.
  • Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992): Tenth Schedule upheld; Speaker's decisions reviewable.
  • K.S. Puttaswamy (Aadhaar) v. Union of India (2018): Aadhaar Act upheld as a Money Bill (with a strong dissent).
  • Mohd. Saeed Siddiqui v. State of U.P. (2014): Speaker's certification of a Money Bill held final (later doubted).

Interdisciplinary edge

  • UK Parliamentary Privilege Act and Joint Committee (1999)
  • NCRWC (2002) recommendation to codify privileges
  • Parliamentary data: declining sitting days and committee referrals (PRS)

Critical lens

Uncodified privileges create uncertainty and occasional conflict with the press and courts.

Close with

Codified, functional privileges would protect the House without placing members above the law.

Previous-year questions (7)
  1. 2026What do you understand by legislative process? Under the Constitution, the Parliament has been empowered to make law on any matter, in any list, for any territory not included in a State. Explain. [P-I 1(c), 10 marks]
    How to approach: Two parts. Explain the Bill-to-Act process (Arts. 107–111, 117 and Money Bills under Art. 110), then Art. 246(4): Parliament legislates on any List for Union territories. Mention Art. 239AA (Delhi) as the special case.
  2. 2025What are the powers, privileges and immunities of the Houses of Parliament in India? Do they have the power to expel any of their members for breach of privileges? If so, are such expulsions subject to judicial review? Discuss. [2a]
  3. 2024"Article 194, which is an exact reproduction of Article 105, deals with the State Legislatures and their members and committees." On this background, comment that both the Articles are complementary to each other and should be read together. [3a]
  4. 2022Discuss the application of fundamental rights to parliamentary privilege cases. [2b]
  5. 2020"Parliamentary privilege is an essential incident to the high and multifarious functions which the legislature performs." Discuss. What reforms will you suggest, if any, in the existing position? [4b]
  6. 2018"The issue of Parliamentary privileges has been a bone of contention and conflict between the Parliament and the Judiciary." Analyse this statement in the backdrop of decided cases. [1d]
  7. 2017Discuss the law relating to the powers of Parliament and State Legislatures to punish a person for breach of their privileges. [3b]
