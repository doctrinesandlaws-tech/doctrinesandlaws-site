Separation of powers

The idea

Montesquieu (The Spirit of Laws, 1748) argued that liberty is lost if the power to make, execute and judge laws sits in the same hands. Strict separation exists nowhere; most constitutions use separation together with checks and balances.

The Indian model: functional separation

  • The Constitution does not adopt the doctrine in strict form, but separates functions in substance (Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab, 1955).
  • Art. 50: the State shall separate the judiciary from the executive in public services.
  • Arts. 121 and 211: legislatures cannot discuss the conduct of judges (except in removal).
  • Arts. 122 and 212: courts cannot question legislative proceedings for procedural irregularity.
  • Art. 361: immunity of the President and Governors.

Separation of powers as basic structure

  • Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975): Parliament cannot decide an election dispute by legislation; that is a judicial function.
  • Kesavananda Bharati (1973) listed separation of powers among basic features.
  • State of Tamil Nadu v. State of Kerala (Mullaperiyar, 2014): a legislature cannot directly overrule a final judgment.

Checks and balances in practice

  • Legislature on the executive: questions, no-confidence motions, committees, financial control (CAG, Public Accounts Committee).
  • Executive on the legislature: summoning, prorogation and dissolution; ordinances.
  • Judiciary on both: judicial review of laws and executive action.
  • Legislature on the judiciary: removal of judges; validating laws that remove the basis of a judgment (Indian Aluminium Co. v. State of Kerala, 1996: valid if the legislature cures the defect, not if it simply overrules the court). Madras Bar Association v. Union of India (2021): re-enacting provisions already struck down, without curing the defect, is impermissible.

Judicial activism and restraint

  • Asif Hameed v. State of J&K (1989): courts must not direct the legislature to legislate or the executive to frame policy.
  • The Court has nevertheless issued guidelines where law is silent (Vishaka, 1997; Prakash Singh v. Union of India, 2006 on police reforms).
  • Criticisms of "judicial overreach": appointment of committees to run institutions, policy directions on economic matters.

Constitutional governance

  • Constitutional morality (Manoj Narula v. Union of India, 2014): constitutional functionaries must act within the spirit, not just the letter, of the Constitution.
  • Institutional comity and collaborative federalism (Govt. of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India, 2018 and 2023): accountability chain from civil servants to ministers to the legislature to the people.
  • Independent "fourth branch" institutions (Election Commission, CAG, Information Commissions) protect constitutional governance. Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India (2023) required a selection committee (PM, Leader of Opposition, CJI) for Election Commissioners until Parliament legislated; Parliament then enacted the Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners Act, 2023, which replaced the CJI with a Union minister.

Revision points

  • India: functional separation with checks and balances (Ram Jawaya Kapur).
  • Basic structure: Indira Gandhi (1975).
  • Validating laws: cure the defect, don't overrule the judgment (Indian Aluminium; Madras Bar Association 2021).
  • Activism vs restraint: Vishaka, Prakash Singh vs Asif Hameed.
Syllabus unit 14 · Asked 7 times · 2013–2026

Answer writing: Separation of powers and constitutional governance

Decode the demand. Expect separation of powers in India and constitutional governance, including the Sixth Schedule. Show functional separation with checks and balances.

Open with

India adopts a functional, not rigid, separation of powers: each organ keeps its core and checks the others.

Answer frame

  1. Montesquieu's theory and its adaptation
  2. Constitutional provisions (Arts. 50, 121, 122, 211, 212, 361)
  3. Judicial recognition (Ram Jawaya Kapur, Indira Gandhi)
  4. Constitutional governance: Fifth and Sixth Schedule autonomy
  5. Overreach and restraint

Concept in brief

India has a functional, not rigid, separation of powers: each organ performs its core role, checked by the others. Separation of powers is part of the basic structure. 'Constitutional governance' means government that is limited and structured by the Constitution, including special arrangements such as the Fifth and Sixth Schedules for tribal areas.

Key provisions

  • Arts. 50, 121, 122, 211, 212
  • Art. 244, Fifth and Sixth Schedules

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab (1955): No rigid separation in India.
  • Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975): Legislature cannot decide an election dispute itself.
  • State of T.N. v. State of Kerala (2014): Legislature cannot nullify a judgment without removing its basis.
  • Divisional Manager, Aravali Golf Club v. Chander Hass (2008): Caution against judicial overreach.
  • Madras Bar Association v. Union of India (2021): Tribunal reforms must respect judicial independence.
  • Pu Myllai Hlychho v. State of Mizoram (2005): Governor's powers over autonomous district councils (Sixth Schedule) are limited and reviewable.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Madison (Federalist 47, 51) on checks and balances
  • Bhuria Committee and the PESA Act 1996 for scheduled areas
  • Sixth Schedule demands in Ladakh and Manipur as current debates

Critical lens

Judicial activism fills governance gaps but risks overreach; legislative overrides risk eroding judgments.

Close with

Constitutional governance means each organ respecting its limits, and the Sixth Schedule shows limits can protect diversity too.

Previous-year questions (7)
  1. 2026‘Constitutional governance’ is a system where government authority is limited, defined and structured by a supreme written or unwritten constitution. Analyze with special reference to the Sixth Schedule of the Indian Constitution. [P-I 3(b), 15 marks]
    How to approach: Analyze = break into parts. Define constitutional governance; apply to the Sixth Schedule: autonomous district councils, legislative, judicial and financial powers, Governor's role (para. 16; Pu Myllai Hlychho). Edge: Bhuria Committee, current Ladakh demand. Close: autonomy as limitation-based governance.
  2. 2025"The doctrine of Separation of Powers in its classical structural form is not followed in any country." Critically evaluate this statement with reasons. [1e]
  3. 2022In the Parliamentary system, though there is no separation between the legislature and the executive in terms of personnel, there is separation of functions between the two. Explain in the light of relevant judicial decisions. [4a]
  4. 2021What is meant by the Doctrine of 'Separation of Powers'? Is strict adherence of the doctrine possible under a parliamentary form of government? Discuss with the help of relevant case laws. [4c]
  5. 2017Do you think the current trends in judicial activism in India are inconsistent with the Principles of Separation of Powers, which is considered to be one of the basic features of the Constitution? [4a]
  6. 2014Examine the doctrine of separation of powers. Also mention the relevance of this doctrine in India. [1e]
  7. 2013“The Constitution of Indian merely subscribes to a three-fold division of gubernatorial functions and not to the doctrine of separation of powers in its absolute rigidity.” Comment. [2b]
