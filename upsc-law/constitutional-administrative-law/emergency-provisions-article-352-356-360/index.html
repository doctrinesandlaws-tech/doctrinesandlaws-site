National emergency (Art. 352) after the 44th Amendment (1978)

The 1975–77 Emergency, declared on the ground of "internal disturbance", led to strong safeguards:

  • "Internal disturbance" was replaced by "armed rebellion".
  • Art. 352(3): the President can proclaim an emergency only when the decision of the Union Cabinet (the Prime Minister and Cabinet ministers) is communicated in writing.
  • Approval by each House by a majority of total membership and two-thirds of those present and voting, within one month; renewal every six months.
  • The Lok Sabha can disapprove continuation by a simple majority; ten per cent of members can requisition a special sitting (Art. 352(7)–(8)).
  • Art. 359(1A): Arts. 20 and 21 can never be suspended.

Effects of a national emergency

  • Art. 250: Parliament may legislate on State List subjects.
  • Art. 353: Union executive power extends to giving directions to States.
  • Art. 354: the President may modify the distribution of revenues.
  • Art. 358: Art. 19 is automatically suspended, but only during an emergency declared on the ground of war or external aggression.
  • Art. 359: the President may suspend enforcement of other rights (except Arts. 20 and 21).
  • The life of the Lok Sabha may be extended by a year at a time (Art. 83(2) proviso).

President's Rule (Art. 356)

  • Based on the Governor's report "or otherwise". Art. 365: failure to comply with Union directions can be treated as breakdown.
  • Judicial control: State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) allowed only limited review. S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994, 9 judges) changed the position: - the proclamation is subject to judicial review; the material must be relevant and the satisfaction not mala fide; - the majority of a government must be tested on the floor of the House; - the Assembly may only be suspended, not dissolved, until both Houses approve the proclamation; if the proclamation is struck down, the government can be restored; - secularism is a basic feature; a State government acting against it may be dismissed.
  • Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006): dissolution of the Bihar Assembly on a Governor's report based on speculation about horse-trading was unconstitutional.
  • Nabam Rebia (2016): the Arunachal Pradesh government was restored.
  • Sarkaria Commission: use Art. 356 sparingly, as a last resort, after warning the State.

Financial emergency (Art. 360)

The Union may give financial directions to States, reduce salaries of public servants (including judges) and require money bills of States to be reserved for the President. It has never been used.

Judicial review of the President's satisfaction

  • The 38th Amendment (1975) made satisfaction under Art. 352 final; the 44th Amendment removed that clause.
  • Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980): the satisfaction is not immune if it is mala fide or based on wholly irrelevant grounds.
  • ADM Jabalpur (1976), which denied habeas corpus during the Emergency, was overruled in Puttaswamy (2017).

Critical assessment

  • Dr. Ambedkar hoped Art. 356 would remain a "dead letter"; in practice it was used repeatedly for political reasons until Bommai.
  • The Shah Commission (1977–78) documented abuses of the 1975 Emergency, which shaped the 44th Amendment.
  • Comparative warning: Article 48 of the Weimar Constitution was used to dismantle German democracy.

Revision points

  • 44th Amendment: armed rebellion; written Cabinet decision; Arts. 20–21 non-suspendable; special majority within one month.
  • Bommai: review, floor test, suspension not dissolution, restoration.
  • Art. 358 (automatic, war/aggression only) vs Art. 359 (presidential order).
Syllabus unit 10 · Asked 8 times · 2013–2026

Answer writing: Emergency provisions

Decode the demand. Questions test the 44th Amendment safeguards and judicial review of Art. 356. Contrast the 1975 Emergency with present safeguards.

Open with

The Emergency of 1975–77 reshaped Part XVIII; the 44th Amendment turned the President's 'satisfaction' into a written Cabinet decision.

Answer frame

  1. Types (Arts. 352, 356, 360)
  2. Pre- and post-44th Amendment safeguards
  3. Effects on federalism and rights (Arts. 358, 359)
  4. Judicial review (Minerva Mills, Bommai)
  5. Assessment

Concept in brief

National emergency (Art. 352) after the 44th Amendment needs 'armed rebellion' (not internal disturbance), a written decision of the Union Cabinet, approval of both Houses by special majority within one month, and Arts. 20 and 21 cannot be suspended (Art. 359). The President's 'satisfaction' is thus in substance the Cabinet's. Art. 356 (State emergency) is judicially reviewable after Bommai; Art. 360 has never been used.

Key provisions

  • Arts. 352–360
  • Art. 352(3) (Cabinet decision in writing)
  • Art. 359(1A)

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla (1976): Habeas corpus suspended during emergency; overruled in Puttaswamy (2017).
  • State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977): Limited review of Art. 356 satisfaction.
  • Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980): Satisfaction is not immune from review if mala fide or irrelevant.
  • S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994): Majority to be tested on the floor; proclamation reviewable; dissolution only after Parliament approves.
  • Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006): Governor's report must be based on objective material.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Shah Commission (1977–78) findings
  • Ambedkar's hope that Art. 356 would remain a 'dead letter'
  • Sarkaria Commission data on Art. 356 use
  • Comparative: Weimar Art. 48 as a warning

Critical lens

Safeguards are strong on paper; their worth depends on parliamentary vigilance and judicial courage.

Close with

Emergency powers should be the Constitution's fire exit, not a door for routine use.

    How to approach: Agree/disagree, then consequences. Disagree: after the 44th Amendment Art. 352(3) requires a written decision of the Union Cabinet. Consequences: Arts. 250, 353, 358, 359; extension of Lok Sabha; effect on rights except Arts. 20–21. Edge: Shah Commission.
