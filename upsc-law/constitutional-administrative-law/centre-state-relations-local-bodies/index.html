Distribution of legislative power

  • Art. 245: territorial extent; Parliament may make laws with extra-territorial operation.
  • Art. 246 and the Seventh Schedule: Union List (List I), State List (List II), Concurrent List (List III). Parliament has exclusive power over List I "notwithstanding" anything in clauses (2) and (3); this establishes Union predominance.
  • Art. 246A (101st Amendment, 2016): both Parliament and State legislatures may make laws on GST; Art. 279A creates the GST Council.
  • Residuary power (Art. 248, Entry 97 List I) rests with Parliament (Union of India v. H.S. Dhillon, 1971: wealth tax on agricultural land upheld).

Interpretive doctrines

  • Pith and substance: look at the true nature of the law; incidental encroachment on another list does not invalidate it (Prafulla Kumar Mukherjee v. Bank of Commerce, Privy Council, 1947; State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara, 1951).
  • Colourable legislation: a legislature cannot do indirectly what it cannot do directly (K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa, 1953).
  • Repugnancy (Art. 254): on a Concurrent List subject, a Central law prevails over an inconsistent State law, unless the State law was reserved for and received the President's assent (Art. 254(2)). M. Karunanidhi v. Union of India (1979) sets the tests.
  • Harmonious construction of entries and liberal interpretation of legislative entries.

When Parliament may legislate on State subjects

  • Art. 249: Rajya Sabha resolution (two-thirds present and voting) in the national interest.
  • Art. 250: during a national emergency.
  • Art. 252: when two or more States request it.
  • Art. 253: to implement international treaties.
  • Art. 356: during President's Rule.

Administrative and financial relations

  • Arts. 256–257: States must exercise executive power so as to comply with Union laws; the Union may give directions. Non-compliance can lead to Art. 365.
  • Art. 263: Inter-State Council (set up in 1990).
  • Art. 262: inter-State river water disputes (Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956).
  • Art. 280: Finance Commission recommends sharing of taxes.
  • Union of India v. Mohit Minerals (2022): GST Council recommendations have persuasive, not binding, value; cooperative federalism includes "contestation".
  • Mineral Area Development Authority v. Steel Authority of India (2024, 9 judges, 8:1): royalty is not a tax; States have power to tax mineral rights under Entry 50, List II.
  • Govt. of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2023): the elected government of Delhi controls "services" except public order, police and land.

Local bodies (73rd and 74th Amendments, 1992)

  • Part IX (Panchayats) and Part IX-A (Municipalities), with the 11th and 12th Schedules listing their functions.
  • Mandatory features: three-tier panchayats in larger States, five-year terms, elections within six months of dissolution, reservation for SCs/STs and at least one-third for women, a State Election Commission (Arts. 243K, 243ZA) and a State Finance Commission (Art. 243-I).
  • Courts treat these as constitutionally protected institutions (Kishansing Tomar v. Municipal Corporation of Ahmedabad, 2006: elections cannot be delayed).

Eminent domain and the right to property

  • The State's power to take private property for public use, subject to compensation.
  • The 44th Amendment (1978) deleted Art. 19(1)(f) and Art. 31 and inserted Art. 300A: no person shall be deprived of property save by authority of law. Property is now a constitutional and human right, not a fundamental right (Vidya Devi v. State of H.P., 2020).
  • K.T. Plantation v. State of Karnataka (2013): the law under Art. 300A must be just, fair and reasonable.
  • Kolkata Municipal Corporation v. Bimal Kumar Shah (2024): seven procedural sub-rights in acquisition, including notice, hearing, a reasoned decision, public purpose, fair compensation, efficient conduct and conclusion.
  • RFCTLARR Act, 2013 replaced the Land Acquisition Act, 1894: social impact assessment, consent requirements for private projects, rehabilitation and resettlement.

Revision points

  • Doctrines: pith and substance, colourable legislation, repugnancy (Art. 254).
  • Arts. 249, 250, 252, 253: Parliament on State subjects.
  • Mohit Minerals (2022) and Mineral Area Development Authority (2024) for fiscal federalism.
  • Art. 300A: Vidya Devi (2020), Bimal Kumar Shah (2024).
Syllabus unit 7 · Asked 22 times · 2013–2025

Answer writing: Centre, States, and local bodies

Decode the demand. Questions test distribution of legislative power and interpretation doctrines, local government, or eminent domain. Use doctrines as headings and a recent federal dispute as the hook.

Open with

The Seventh Schedule divides power, but the doctrines of interpretation decide where the line actually falls.

Answer frame

  1. Scheme: Arts. 245–254, three Lists
  2. Interpretive doctrines (pith and substance, colourable legislation, repugnancy, harmonious construction)
  3. Residuary and emergency powers (Arts. 248–250)
  4. Fiscal federalism: GST (Art. 279A), Mohit Minerals, MADA (2024)
  5. Local bodies (73rd and 74th Amendments)

Concept in brief

Legislative power is divided by the Seventh Schedule; conflicts are read through pith and substance, colourable legislation and repugnancy (Art. 254). Residuary power is with the Union (Art. 248). Parliament legislates for Union territories on any matter (Art. 246(4)). Administrative relations (Arts. 256–263), finance (Arts. 268–293, GST Council Art. 279A) and the 73rd/74th Amendments (Parts IX, IXA) complete the federal picture.

Key provisions

  • Arts. 245–263, 239AA, 243–243ZG, 268–293, 279A, 300A
  • Seventh Schedule

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Prafulla Kumar Mukherjee v. Bank of Commerce, Khulna (1947, PC): Pith and substance; incidental encroachment permissible.
  • K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa (1953): Colourable legislation: what cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly.
  • M. Karunanidhi v. Union of India (1979): Tests of repugnancy under Art. 254.
  • Union of India v. H.S. Dhillon (1972): Wide residuary power under Art. 248 and Entry 97.
  • Union of India v. Mohit Minerals (2022): GST Council recommendations are persuasive, not binding.
  • Mineral Area Development Authority v. SAIL (2024): Royalty is not a tax; States can tax mineral rights.
  • K. Krishna Murthy v. Union of India (2010): Local body reservations upheld; not a basic-structure issue.
  • State of T.N. v. L. Abu Kavur Bai (1984): DPSP-based nationalisation; Art. 39(b) 'material resources'.
  • Property Owners Assn. v. State of Maharashtra (2024): Not every private resource is a 'material resource of the community' under Art. 39(b).

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Sarkaria, Punchhi and Rajamannar recommendations
  • 2nd ARC 6th Report on local governance
  • Finance Commission devolution data
  • Comparative: US dual federalism vs Indian 'holding together' federalism (Stepan)

Critical lens

Judicial doctrines lean pro-Union, but recent rulings (Mohit Minerals, MADA) show a federal rebalancing.

Close with

Cooperative federalism works when shared institutions like the GST Council and Inter-State Council function as forums of real bargaining.
