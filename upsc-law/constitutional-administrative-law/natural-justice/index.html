Meaning and purpose

Natural justice is a set of minimum procedural fairness rules that apply to anyone who decides matters affecting the rights of others. They are not in the Constitution in words but are read into Art. 14 (non-arbitrariness) and Art. 21 (fair procedure, Maneka Gandhi, 1978).

The two core rules

  1. Nemo judex in causa sua: no one should be a judge in his own cause (rule against bias) - Pecuniary bias: any financial interest disqualifies, however small. - Personal bias: relationship, friendship or hostility. - Official or subject-matter bias: the decision-maker is involved in the policy or prosecution. - Test: whether there is a real likelihood or reasonable apprehension of bias in the mind of a reasonable person, not whether bias actually exists (Ranjit Thakur v. Union of India, 1987; State of Punjab v. V.K. Khanna, 2001). - A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India (1969): a selection board member who was himself a candidate vitiated the selection. - Doctrine of necessity: if no one else can decide, the biased authority may (Election Commission of India v. Dr. Subramaniam Swamy, 1996).
  2. Audi alteram partem: hear the other side - Notice of the case to be met, disclosure of material relied on, a reasonable opportunity to respond, and (where needed) cross-examination and legal representation. - Ridge v. Baldwin (House of Lords, 1964) revived the rule for administrative decisions. - State of Orissa v. Dr. Binapani Dei (1967): an administrative order with civil consequences requires a hearing. - Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978): a post-decisional hearing can sometimes satisfy the rule where urgency demands.

The third principle: reasoned decisions

  • Siemens Engineering v. Union of India (1976) and S.N. Mukherjee v. Union of India (1990, 5 judges): authorities exercising quasi-judicial power must record reasons, which enables review and checks arbitrariness.

From quasi-judicial to "fairness"

  • A.K. Kraipak (1969) blurred the line between administrative and quasi-judicial functions; the aim of both is a just decision.
  • Natural justice now applies to administrative action generally, in proportion to the consequences of the decision.

Exceptions

  • Statutory exclusion, express or by necessary implication, though such exclusion must itself be reasonable.
  • Legislative action (Union of India v. Cynamide India, 1987).
  • Emergency or urgency, where a post-decisional hearing may suffice.
  • Art. 311(2) second proviso: no inquiry in specified cases (Union of India v. Tulsiram Patel, 1985).
  • Useless formality: where on admitted facts only one conclusion is possible (S.L. Kapoor v. Jagmohan, 1980, limited this; Aligarh Muslim University v. Mansoor Ali Khan, 2000, applied the prejudice test).

Emerging trends

  • Prejudice test: breach of a procedural rule vitiates a decision only if prejudice is shown (State Bank of Patiala v. S.K. Sharma, 1996), except for breaches of a fundamental nature.
  • Legitimate expectation as part of fairness (Food Corporation of India v. Kamdhenu Cattle Feed Industries, 1993).
  • Sealed-cover procedure disfavoured: Madhyamam Broadcasting v. Union of India (2023) held that non-disclosure must be proportionate and suggested public-interest-immunity style safeguards instead of sealed covers.
  • Digital and AI decision-making: emerging debates on the right to reasons and human review when automated systems decide entitlements.

Revision points

  • Two pillars + reasoned decisions.
  • Bias test: reasonable likelihood (Ranjit Thakur); necessity exception (Subramaniam Swamy).
  • Kraipak and Binapani Dei: fairness applies to administrative action.
  • Prejudice test (S.K. Sharma); Madhyamam (2023) on sealed covers.
Syllabus unit 12 · Asked 9 times · 2016–2026

Answer writing: Principle of Natural Justice: Emerging trends and judicial approach

Decode the demand. Questions test the flexible content of natural justice, its exclusion, and post-decisional hearing. Use maxims, then the prejudice test.

Open with

Natural justice is not a fixed code but a minimum of fairness whose content varies with the statute, the stakes and the urgency.

Answer frame

  1. Two pillars plus reasoned decisions
  2. From quasi-judicial to all administrative action (Ridge, Kraipak)
  3. Flexibility and exclusion (Swadeshi Cotton, Tulsiram Patel)
  4. Prejudice and useless-formality tests
  5. Emerging trends: legitimate expectation, digital decisions

Concept in brief

Natural justice means the rule against bias and the right to be heard, plus a reasoned decision. Its content is flexible: it varies with the statute, the interest affected and the urgency of the situation, and it can be excluded only by clear words or necessity. Courts now ask whether the lapse caused real prejudice.

Key provisions

  • Art. 14 and Art. 21 (fair procedure)
  • Art. 311(2)

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • Ridge v. Baldwin (1964, HL): Revived the duty to hear in administrative decisions.
  • A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India (1969): Line between administrative and quasi-judicial blurred; bias rule applied.
  • Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978): Audi alteram partem implied; post-decisional hearing in urgency.
  • Swadeshi Cotton Mills v. Union of India (1981): Post-decisional hearing valid where pre-decisional is impractical.
  • S.L. Kapoor v. Jagmohan (1980): Hearing not excused as a 'useless formality' except in narrow cases.
  • Siemens Engineering v. Union of India (1976): Duty to give reasons.
  • State Bank of Patiala v. S.K. Sharma (1996): Prejudice test for procedural violations.
  • Ranjit Thakur v. Union of India (1987): Test of real likelihood of bias.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • UK Franks Committee (1957) on open, fair, impartial tribunals
  • Algorithmic decision-making and the right to reasons
  • Behavioural science on bias

Critical lens

The prejudice test promotes efficiency but can dilute rights if overused.

Close with

Fairness in process is part of Art. 14; its flexibility is a strength only if courts keep a non-negotiable core.
