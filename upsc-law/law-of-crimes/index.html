General principles of Criminal liability: mens rea and actus reus, mens rea in statutory offences

Crime needs a guilty act (actus reus) done with a guilty mind (mens rea), and the two must coincide. The IPC and BNS do not use the term mens rea; it is built into words like 'intentionally', 'knowingly', 'dishonestly' and 'voluntarily'. In statutory offences, courts presume mens rea is required unless the statute excludes it expressly or by necessary implication (strict liability).

Key provisions

  • IPC ss. 39 'voluntarily', 24 'dishonestly', 25 'fraudulently' (BNS s. 2)
  • BNS ss. 14, 17 (mistake of fact)

Leading cases

  • R v. Prince (1875): Mistake as to age no defence to abduction (strict view).
  • R v. Tolson (1889): Honest and reasonable mistake negatives mens rea.
  • Sherras v. De Rutzen (1895): Presumption of mens rea in statutory offences.
  • Srinivas Mall Bairoliya v. King-Emperor (1947, PC): Mens rea essential unless excluded.
  • Nathulal v. State of M.P. (1966): Mens rea presumed in economic offences unless excluded.
  • State of Maharashtra v. M.H. George (1965): Mens rea excluded under FERA by necessary implication.

Kinds of punishment and emerging trends as to the abolition of capital punishment

BNS s. 4 lists punishments: death, imprisonment for life, rigorous or simple imprisonment, forfeiture, fine and, newly, community service. Community service is unpaid work that benefits the community (BNSS s. 23 explanation) and is prescribed for petty offences such as first-time theft below ₹5,000 with restitution, so that local harms can be repaired locally. Death is reserved for the 'rarest of rare' cases after weighing mitigating circumstances.

Key provisions

  • IPC s. 53 → BNS s. 4
  • BNS ss. 202, 209, 226, 303(2) proviso, 355, 356(2) (community service)
  • BNSS s. 23
  • Probation of Offenders Act, 1958

Leading cases

  • Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (1980): Death penalty valid; 'rarest of rare' doctrine.
  • Machhi Singh v. State of Punjab (1983): Categories for 'rarest of rare'.
  • Mithu v. State of Punjab (1983): Mandatory death sentence (s. 303 IPC) unconstitutional.
  • Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India (2014): Inordinate delay in mercy petitions can justify commutation.
  • Swamy Shraddananda v. State of Karnataka (2008): Fixed-term life sentence beyond 14 years as an alternative to death.
  • Manoj v. State of M.P. (2022): Mitigation investigation required before death sentence.

Preparations and criminal attempt

A crime moves through intention, preparation, attempt and completion. Preparation is generally not punished (exceptions: waging war, dacoity, counterfeiting). Attempt starts when the act goes beyond preparation towards completion; impossibility is no defence under Indian law.

Key provisions

  • IPC s. 511 → BNS s. 62
  • IPC s. 307 → BNS s. 109 (attempt to murder)
  • IPC s. 122 → BNS s. 149; s. 399 → BNS s. 310(4)

Leading cases

  • R v. Eagleton (1855): Proximity rule.
  • Abhayanand Mishra v. State of Bihar (1961): Attempt begins once preparation is over and an act towards commission is done.
  • State of Maharashtra v. Mohd. Yakub (1980): Test of attempt in smuggling.
  • Asgarali Pradhania v. Emperor (1933): Impossible attempt (abortion with harmless substance).
  • Aman Kumar v. State of Haryana (2004): Distinguishing attempt to rape from indecent assault.

General exceptions

General exceptions (BNS ss. 14–44, formerly IPC ss. 76–106) excuse or justify conduct: mistake of fact, accident, necessity, infancy, insanity, involuntary intoxication, consent, duress, trifles and private defence. Mistake of fact in good faith removes mens rea, which is why it is an excuse; for strict-liability offences it generally does not help. Private defence is a right of protection, not retaliation, and must be proportionate.

Key provisions

  • IPC ss. 76–106 → BNS ss. 14–44 (76→14, 79→17, 80→18, 81→19, 84→22, 85–86→23–24, 94→32, 96–106→34–44)
  • BSA s. 108 (burden on accused for exceptions)

Leading cases

  • State of Orissa v. Bhagaban Barik (1987): Mistake of fact in good faith; s. 79.
  • Chirangi v. State of Nagpur (1952): Delusion-based mistake of fact.
  • Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakkar v. State of Gujarat (1964): Legal, not medical, insanity; burden.
  • Basdev v. State of PEPSU (1956): Voluntary intoxication: knowledge, not intention, presumed.
  • R v. Dudley and Stephens (1884): Necessity no defence to murder.
  • Darshan Singh v. State of Punjab (2010): Guidelines on private defence.
  • Deo Narain v. State of U.P. (1973): Reasonable apprehension suffices; no need to wait for injury.

Joint and constructive liability

Joint liability makes each participant liable for the act of others. Under s. 34 IPC (BNS s. 3(5)) several persons act in furtherance of a common intention, which needs a prior meeting of minds and participation. Under s. 149 IPC (BNS s. 190) every member of an unlawful assembly is guilty of an offence committed in prosecution of its common object; it creates a distinct offence, while s. 34 is a rule of evidence.

Key provisions

  • IPC ss. 34–38 → BNS s. 3(5)–(9)
  • IPC s. 149 → BNS s. 190

Leading cases

  • Barendra Kumar Ghosh v. King-Emperor (1925, PC): They also serve who only stand and wait.
  • Mahbub Shah v. Emperor (1945, PC): Common intention requires prior concert.
  • Pandurang v. State of Hyderabad (1955): Common intention distinguished from similar intention.
  • Masalti v. State of U.P. (1965): Membership of unlawful assembly; evidence of witnesses.
  • Suresh v. State of U.P. (2001): Physical presence not always essential under s. 34.

Abetment

Abetment covers instigation, conspiracy and intentional aiding (s. 107 IPC, BNS s. 45). An abettor is liable even if the offence is not committed, and the BNS punishes abetment in India of offences outside India. Abetment of suicide needs positive instigation with intent, not mere harassment.

Key provisions

  • IPC ss. 107–120 → BNS ss. 45–60
  • IPC s. 306 → BNS s. 108

Leading cases

  • Faguna Kanta Nath v. State of Assam (1959): Abettor's liability when principal acquitted.
  • Ramesh Kumar v. State of Chhattisgarh (2001): Meaning of 'instigation'.
  • M. Mohan v. State (2011): Abetment of suicide needs active act or direct act.
  • Gurcharan Singh v. State of Punjab (2017): Mens rea to abet suicide must be shown.

Criminal conspiracy

Criminal conspiracy is an agreement between two or more persons to do an illegal act, or a legal act by illegal means; for agreements other than to commit an offence, an overt act is needed. The agreement itself is the offence and can be proved by circumstantial evidence.

Key provisions

  • IPC ss. 120A–120B → BNS s. 61
  • BSA s. 8 (acts of co-conspirators)

Leading cases

  • Kehar Singh v. State (Delhi Admn.) (1988): Conspiracy proved by circumstances.
  • State v. Nalini (1999): Each conspirator need not know every detail.
  • Yash Pal Mittal v. State of Punjab (1977): Ingredients of conspiracy.
  • State (NCT of Delhi) v. Navjot Sandhu (2005): Parliament attack: limits of conspiracy evidence.

Syllabus unit 8 · Asked 41 times · 2013–2026

Offences against the State, public tranquillity, human body, property, and women

BNS reorganised specific offences. Sedition (s. 124A IPC) is replaced by s. 152 BNS, 'acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India', which drops 'disaffection towards Government' but covers secession, armed rebellion and subversive activity. Culpable homicide becomes murder unless an exception applies; Exception 4 covers a sudden fight without premeditation. Theft protects possession, not ownership. New offences include organised crime, terrorist act, mob lynching, snatching and sexual intercourse by deceitful means.

Key provisions

  • Sedition IPC 124A → BNS 152; waging war 121 → 147; promoting enmity 153A → 196
  • Unlawful assembly 141 → 189; rioting 146–147 → 191
  • Culpable homicide 299 → 100; murder 300 → 101; 302 → 103; 304 → 105; 304A → 106; dowry death 304B → 80
  • Hurt 319–325 → 114–118; kidnapping 359–363 → 137; abduction 362 → 87
  • Theft 378 → 303; snatching (new) 304; extortion 383 → 308; robbery 390 → 309; dacoity 391 → 310; CBT 405 → 316; cheating 415/420 → 318
  • Rape 375–376 → 63–64; gang rape 376D → 70; deceitful means/false promise (new) 69; 354 → 74; stalking 354D → 78; cruelty 498A → 85–86
  • Organised crime BNS 111; terrorist act 113; mob lynching 103(2)

Leading cases

  • Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar (1962): Sedition limited to incitement to violence or public disorder.
  • S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India (2022): S. 124A kept in abeyance.
  • Virsa Singh v. State of Punjab (1958): Test for s. 300 'thirdly'.
  • State of A.P. v. Rayavarapu Punnayya (1976): Culpable homicide vs murder.
  • K.M. Nanavati v. State of Maharashtra (1962): Grave and sudden provocation; cooling time.
  • Surinder Kumar v. Union Territory, Chandigarh (1989): Exception 4: sudden fight, no undue advantage.
  • Pyare Lal Bhargava v. State of Rajasthan (1963): Temporary removal is enough for theft.
  • Tukaram v. State of Maharashtra (1979): Mathura case; led to the 1983 amendment.
  • Deepak Gulati v. State of Haryana (2013): False promise vs breach of promise to marry.
  • Pramod Suryabhan Pawar v. State of Maharashtra (2019): Promise must be false from the start to vitiate consent.
  • Independent Thought v. Union of India (2017): Marital-rape exception read down for wives under 18.
  • Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar (2014): No automatic arrest in s. 498A cases.
  • Kans Raj v. State of Punjab (2000): 'Soon before death' in dowry death.

Defamation

Criminal defamation punishes imputations made or published with intent or knowledge that they will harm reputation, subject to ten exceptions including truth for public good and fair comment. BNS adds community service as a punishment option.

Key provisions

  • IPC ss. 499–500 → BNS s. 356

Leading cases

  • Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India (2016): Criminal defamation constitutionally valid.
  • R. Rajagopal v. State of T.N. (1994): Public figures and the privacy-reputation balance.

Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988

The Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, amended in 2018, punishes a public servant who obtains, accepts or attempts to obtain an undue advantage to perform a public duty improperly (s. 7), and also the bribe-giver (s. 8). A demand for money to process a routine application on time is itself an offence under s. 7. Investigation of decisions taken in official functions needs prior approval (s. 17A, except when caught red-handed), and prosecution needs sanction (s. 19).

Key provisions

  • PCA ss. 2(c), 7, 7A, 8, 9, 10, 13, 17A, 19, 20 (presumption)

Leading cases

  • Neeraj Dutta v. State (NCT of Delhi) (2022): Demand and acceptance can be proved by circumstantial evidence.

Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 and subsequent legislative developments

Art. 17 abolishes untouchability and makes its practice an offence. Parliament enacted the Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955, renamed the Protection of Civil Rights Act in 1976 with stricter penalties, then the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 (amended 2015 and 2018) and the 2013 Act banning manual scavenging. Art. 17 binds private persons as well as the State.

Key provisions

  • Constitution Arts. 15(2), 17, 23, 46
  • PCR Act, 1955 (ss. 3–7A, 15A)
  • SC/ST (PoA) Act, 1989, s. 3, 18, 18A
  • Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers Act, 2013

Leading cases

  • People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982): Art. 17 enforceable against private individuals.
  • State of Karnataka v. Appa Balu Ingale (1993): Courts must enforce the PCR Act strictly.
  • Subhash Kashinath Mahajan v. State of Maharashtra (2018): Safeguards against arrest (later recalled).
  • Union of India v. State of Maharashtra (2019): Mahajan directions recalled.
  • Prathvi Raj Chauhan v. Union of India (2020): S. 18A (no anticipatory bail) upheld.
  • Safai Karamchari Andolan v. Union of India (2014): Compensation for sewer deaths; end manual scavenging.

Plea bargaining

Plea bargaining, introduced in 2005 as Chapter XXIA CrPC (now BNSS ss. 289–300), lets an accused plead guilty for a reduced sentence for offences punishable up to seven years, excluding socio-economic offences and offences against women or children under 14. The court must examine the accused in camera to confirm the application is voluntary; BNSS adds a 30-day window from framing of charge.

Key provisions

  • CrPC ss. 265A–265L → BNSS ss. 289–300
  • Law Commission 142nd and 154th Reports; Malimath Committee (2003)

Leading cases

  • Murlidhar Meghraj Loya v. State of Maharashtra (1976): Earlier disapproval of plea bargaining.
  • Kasambhai Abdulrehmanbhai Sheikh v. State of Gujarat (1980): Conviction on bargained plea held unconstitutional (pre-2005).
  • State of Gujarat v. Natwar Harchandji Thakor (2005): Recognised plea bargaining as a legitimate reform.
