Nature of the general exceptions

The general exceptions (IPC ss. 76–106 → BNS ss. 14–44) apply to all offences in the Code and special laws (IPC s. 40, BNS s. 2). The burden of proof of an exception lies on the accused, but only on a preponderance of probabilities (Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, s. 108; IEA s. 105). They are either excusable (no mens rea: infancy, insanity, mistake) or justifiable (the act is right: private defence, judicial acts).

Key exceptions

  • Mistake: of fact, not of law (ignorantia juris non excusat). State of Orissa v. Bhagaban Barik (1987): honest belief in good faith.
  • Insanity (BNS s. 22): based on the M'Naghten Rules (1843): at the time of the act, by reason of unsoundness of mind, the accused was incapable of knowing the nature of the act or that it was wrong or contrary to law. Only legal insanity counts. Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakkar v. State of Gujarat (1964): burden on accused on a balance of probabilities; Surendra Mishra v. State of Jharkhand (2011).
  • Intoxication: involuntary intoxication is a defence like insanity (BNS s. 23); voluntary intoxication is not, but the accused is attributed knowledge as if sober (BNS s. 24). Basdev v. State of PEPSU (1956).
  • Necessity (BNS s. 19): act done without criminal intention, in good faith, to avoid other harm. R v. Dudley and Stephens (1884): necessity is no defence to murder.
  • Consent: cannot authorise intentional causing of death (BNS s. 25), but surgical acts for a person's benefit are covered (BNS s. 26).

Private defence (BNS ss. 34–44)

  • Right to defend one's own or another's body, and property against theft, robbery, mischief or criminal trespass (BNS s. 35).
  • Limits (BNS s. 37): no right where there is time to seek help from public authorities; harm must not exceed what is necessary; no right against acts of public servants acting in good faith (unless they cause reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt).
  • When death may be caused in defence of the body (BNS s. 38): reasonable apprehension of death, grievous hurt, rape, unnatural lust, kidnapping or abduction, wrongful confinement, acid attack. In defence of property (BNS s. 41): robbery, house-breaking by night, mischief by fire, theft or house-breaking causing apprehension of death or grievous hurt.
  • Commencement and duration (BNS ss. 40, 43): starts with a reasonable apprehension and lasts as long as it continues.
  • Cases: - Darshan Singh v. State of Punjab (2010): guidelines; a person facing an assault need not run away; courts should not weigh the force used "in golden scales". - Deo Narain v. State of U.P. (1973): the right begins with a reasonable apprehension, even before an actual injury. - Kishan v. State of M.P. (1974) and Yogendra Morarji v. State of Gujarat (1980): the force used must be proportionate; exceeding it leads to liability, though IPC s. 300 Exception 2 (BNS s. 101 Exception 2) reduces murder to culpable homicide when the right is exceeded in good faith.

Revision points

  • IPC 76–106 → BNS 14–44; burden on preponderance (BSA s. 108).
  • Insanity = legal insanity (M'Naghten); Dahyabhai (1964).
  • Voluntary intoxication: knowledge attributed (Basdev 1956).
  • Private defence: limits (s. 37), death permitted (ss. 38, 41), Darshan Singh (2010).
Syllabus unit 4 · Asked 16 times · 2013–2026

Answer writing: General exceptions

Decode the demand. Very frequent. Private defence, insanity, intoxication and mistake recur. Classify each as justification or excuse, then apply.

Open with

General exceptions recognise that conduct which looks criminal may be justified (private defence) or excused (insanity, mistake).

Answer frame

  1. Justification vs excuse
  2. The exception asked: ingredients with section
  3. Limits and burden of proof (BSA s. 108)
  4. Leading cases
  5. Application to facts, if a problem question

Concept in brief

General exceptions (BNS ss. 14–44, formerly IPC ss. 76–106) excuse or justify conduct: mistake of fact, accident, necessity, infancy, insanity, involuntary intoxication, consent, duress, trifles and private defence. Mistake of fact in good faith removes mens rea, which is why it is an excuse; for strict-liability offences it generally does not help. Private defence is a right of protection, not retaliation, and must be proportionate.

Key provisions

  • IPC ss. 76–106 → BNS ss. 14–44 (76→14, 79→17, 80→18, 81→19, 84→22, 85–86→23–24, 94→32, 96–106→34–44)
  • BSA s. 108 (burden on accused for exceptions)

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • State of Orissa v. Bhagaban Barik (1987): Mistake of fact in good faith; s. 79.
  • Chirangi v. State of Nagpur (1952): Delusion-based mistake of fact.
  • Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakkar v. State of Gujarat (1964): Legal, not medical, insanity; burden.
  • Basdev v. State of PEPSU (1956): Voluntary intoxication: knowledge, not intention, presumed.
  • R v. Dudley and Stephens (1884): Necessity no defence to murder.
  • Darshan Singh v. State of Punjab (2010): Guidelines on private defence.
  • Deo Narain v. State of U.P. (1973): Reasonable apprehension suffices; no need to wait for injury.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Glanville Williams and George Fletcher on justification vs excuse
  • Medical vs legal insanity (Mental Healthcare Act, 2017)
  • Comparative: the UK Criminal Justice and Immigration Act, 2008 on householders' self-defence

Critical lens

Mistake of fact excuses because it removes mens rea; in strict-liability offences it usually fails, though courts allow it where the statute's purpose permits.

Close with

General exceptions keep criminal law morally credible by punishing blameworthy conduct only.

Previous-year questions (16)

Mistake

  1. 2026How far does a ‘mistake of fact’ negate mens rea? Does it operate as a justification or an excuse, and should it be allowed as a defence in strict liability offences? Explain. [P-II 3(b), 15 marks]
    How to approach: Three parts. Mistake of fact negates mens rea (BNS ss. 14, 17); it is an excuse, not a justification; strict-liability offences: generally no, but argue for allowing a reasonable mistake (Tolson; Sherras v De Rutzen).
  2. 2017"Law recognises that 'mistake' must be in good faith." In this backdrop, explain the defence of 'mistake' contained under General Exceptions of the Penal Code.
  3. 2013X, the producer of a film showing life of ‘homosexuals’, is prosecuted under Section 292 of the Indian Penal Code, alleging that the film was obscene and indecent. The film was certified by the Censor Board of Film of India for public shows. X intends to claim protection against his charge under Section 79 of the Indian Penal Code. Can he do so? Justify your answer.

Private Defence

  1. 2025"Right to private defence is a valuable right, but it must be exercised reasonably." Explain with examples.
  2. 2023Discuss the nature and scope of the right of Private defence of property, along with limitations, if any, on the exercise of such right.
  3. 2022The Right of Private Defence is based on the cardinal principle that it is the primary duty of man to help himself, but this right is not absolute. Explain.
  4. 2019Right to private defence under the Indian Penal Code, 1860, is available only to an innocent person. It is not a right to retribution. Analyse.
  5. 2018"Right of private defence to the extent of causing the death of an assailant cannot be based on surmises and speculation. The accused must be under a bona fide fear of death or grievous hurt would otherwise be the consequence of the assault, if he does not defend. To determine the existence of apprehension is always a question of fact." Explain the above proposition in the light of existing legal provisions and judicial decisions.
  6. 2014What is meant by right of private defence? What are the general principles which form the basis of the right of private defence?

Intoxication

  1. 2024'Intoxication impairs perception and judgement, both so one fails to foresee the result of his conduct.' In this backdrop, examine the law relating to the defence of intoxication and refer to the leading cases.
  2. 2020If a person, who voluntarily consumed intoxicating liquor, commits an offence, while under the influence of such intoxication, can he plead 'voluntary intoxication' as a defence? Discuss in the light of relevant provisions of the IPC.
  3. 2013“Involuntary drunkenness is a defence.” Critically examine the law relating to intoxication as a defence.

Other Exceptions

  1. 2021Insanity: What amounts to 'Legal Insanity' that would entitle an accused for exemption from Criminal Liability?
  2. 2019Duress / Act Done Against Will: "Act done by me against my will is not my act." Examine in the light of the legal provisions of the Indian Penal Code, 1860.
  3. 2016Unintended Consequences of Lawful Act: "A man is not criminally responsible for unintended and unknown consequences of his lawful acts performed in a lawful manner, by lawful means with proper care and caution." Elucidate.
