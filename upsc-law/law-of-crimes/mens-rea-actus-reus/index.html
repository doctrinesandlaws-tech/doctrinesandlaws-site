Mens rea and actus reus

The basic rule

Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea: an act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty. A crime normally needs:

  1. a human being under a legal duty
  2. mens rea (a guilty mind)
  3. actus reus (a guilty act or omission)
  4. resulting injury or harm (in result crimes)

Actus reus

  • Includes acts and illegal omissions where there is a legal duty to act (definitions of "act" and "omission" in BNS s. 2; IPC ss. 32–33).
  • Must be voluntary; a reflex or an act during sleep is not.
  • Causation: the act must be a substantial cause of the result; a new intervening act (novus actus interveniens) can break the chain.
  • Circumstances: some offences need specific circumstances (for example, absence of consent in rape).

Mens rea

Forms, from most to least culpable:

  • Intention: aim or purpose to bring about a result. Direct intention (the purpose) and oblique intention (a virtually certain consequence).
  • Knowledge: awareness that the result is likely (culpable homicide under BNS s. 100 / IPC s. 299 uses both intention and knowledge).
  • Recklessness / rashness: conscious taking of an unjustified risk.
  • Negligence: failure to meet the standard of a reasonable person. For criminal liability it must be gross. Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab (2005) for medical negligence under IPC s. 304A (now BNS s. 106).
  • Motive is the reason behind the act; it is generally irrelevant to liability, though it matters in evidence and sentencing.

The words of the Code express mens rea: "intentionally", "knowingly", "voluntarily", "dishonestly", "fraudulently" (all defined in BNS s. 2), "rashly", "negligently".

Strict and absolute liability

  • Statutes may exclude mens rea, especially in public welfare offences (food adulteration, environment, economic regulation).
  • R v. Prince (1875): belief that a girl was over 16 was no defence; R v. Tolson (1889): honest and reasonable belief in a husband's death was a defence; Sherras v. De Rutzen (1895): presumption that mens rea is required.
  • Nathulal v. State of M.P. (1966): mens rea is an essential ingredient unless the statute excludes it expressly or by necessary implication.
  • State of Maharashtra v. M.H. George (1965): under foreign exchange law, knowledge of the new notification was not needed; strict liability applied.
  • Vicarious criminal liability exists only by statute (for example, liability of company officers under the Negotiable Instruments Act, s. 141).

Corporate criminal liability

Standard Chartered Bank v. Directorate of Enforcement (2005): a company can be prosecuted for an offence punishable with imprisonment and fine; the court may impose only the fine. Iridium India Telecom v. Motorola (2011): the mens rea of directing minds can be attributed to the company.

Under the BNS, 2023

The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (in force 1 July 2024) keeps the IPC's structure of mens rea and general explanations; definitions shift to s. 2. New offences (organised crime, s. 111; terrorist act, s. 113) also require specific intention.

Revision points

  • Four elements; act + omission + causation.
  • Intention > knowledge > recklessness > negligence; motive ≠ intention.
  • Prince, Tolson, Sherras v. De Rutzen; Nathulal (1966), M.H. George (1965).
  • Jacob Mathew (gross negligence); corporate liability (Standard Chartered, Iridium).
Syllabus unit 1 · Asked 8 times · 2013–2024

Answer writing: General principles of Criminal liability: mens rea and actus reus, mens rea in statutory offences

Decode the demand. Questions test the mens rea maxim, the concurrence of act and intent, and strict liability. Explain the maxim, show how the BNS builds mens rea into its definitions, then the statutory exceptions.

Open with

Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea: the act alone does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty.

Answer frame

  1. Elements of crime: person, mens rea, actus reus, injury
  2. Mens rea in the IPC/BNS through words such as 'intentionally', 'knowingly', 'dishonestly'
  3. Concurrence of act and intent (Fagan v Metropolitan Police Commissioner, 1969)
  4. Strict liability in statutory offences (Prince, M.H. George)
  5. Assessment

Concept in brief

Crime needs a guilty act (actus reus) done with a guilty mind (mens rea), and the two must coincide. The IPC and BNS do not use the term mens rea; it is built into words like 'intentionally', 'knowingly', 'dishonestly' and 'voluntarily'. In statutory offences, courts presume mens rea is required unless the statute excludes it expressly or by necessary implication (strict liability).

Key provisions

  • IPC ss. 39 'voluntarily', 24 'dishonestly', 25 'fraudulently' (BNS s. 2)
  • BNS ss. 14, 17 (mistake of fact)

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • R v. Prince (1875): Mistake as to age no defence to abduction (strict view).
  • R v. Tolson (1889): Honest and reasonable mistake negatives mens rea.
  • Sherras v. De Rutzen (1895): Presumption of mens rea in statutory offences.
  • Srinivas Mall Bairoliya v. King-Emperor (1947, PC): Mens rea essential unless excluded.
  • Nathulal v. State of M.P. (1966): Mens rea presumed in economic offences unless excluded.
  • State of Maharashtra v. M.H. George (1965): Mens rea excluded under FERA by necessary implication.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Jurisprudence: Hart on responsibility and excuse
  • Economic offences and public-welfare regulation as the rationale for strict liability
  • Law Commission 47th Report (1972) on socio-economic offences

Draw this

Intention/knowledge (mens rea) + conduct (actus reus) + concurrence → offence

Critical lens

Strict liability increases efficiency but risks punishing the morally innocent.

Close with

Mens rea remains the rule and strict liability the narrow, justified exception.
