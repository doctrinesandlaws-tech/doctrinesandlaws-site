Protection of Civil Rights Act and SC/ST Act

Study notes

Constitutional foundationProtection of Civil Rights Act, 1955SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989The 2018 controversyOther important rulingsAssessment

Constitutional foundation

  • Art. 17 abolishes "untouchability" and forbids its practice in any form; enforcing any disability arising from it is an offence.
  • Art. 15(2): no restriction on access to shops, wells, roads and public places; Art. 23 (forced labour); Art. 46 (protection of weaker sections).

Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955

  • Originally the Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955; renamed and strengthened in 1976.
  • Punishes enforcing religious disabilities (temple entry, worship), social disabilities (access to shops, restaurants, hotels, water sources, roads, cremation grounds), refusing to sell goods or render services, insulting a member of a Scheduled Caste on the ground of untouchability, and forcing manual scavenging or removal of carcasses.
  • Enhanced punishment for subsequent convictions; collective fines on areas; presumption that an act was committed on the ground of untouchability where the victim is an SC.
  • Limitations led to the next Act: few convictions, minor punishments, focus on untouchability rather than violence.

SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989

  • s. 3: a list of atrocities: forcing inedible substances, dumping waste, wrongful occupation of land, forced labour, insulting in public view, sexual exploitation, social boycott (added 2015), obstructing voting, and IPC (now BNS) offences with ten years or more committed on the ground of caste.
  • Special courts and exclusive special courts (s. 14); special public prosecutors.
  • Rights of victims and witnesses (Chapter IV-A, 2015 amendment): notice, legal aid, relief and rehabilitation.
  • s. 18: anticipatory bail (CrPC s. 438, now BNSS s. 482) excluded.

The 2018 controversy

  • Subhash Kashinath Mahajan v. State of Maharashtra (2018): the Court directed a preliminary inquiry before an FIR and approval before arrest of public servants, citing misuse.
  • Parliament inserted s. 18A (2018): no preliminary inquiry and no approval for arrest required; anticipatory bail barred.
  • Union of India v. State of Maharashtra (2019, review): the 2018 directions were recalled as amounting to legislation.
  • Prathvi Raj Chauhan v. Union of India (2020): s. 18A upheld; courts may still quash proceedings under s. 482 CrPC (BNSS s. 528) where no prima facie case exists, and may grant anticipatory bail in such cases.

Other important rulings

  • Hitesh Verma v. State of Uttarakhand (2020): insult must be because the victim belongs to an SC/ST and "in public view"; not every insult of an SC/ST person is an offence under the Act.
  • Khuman Singh v. State of M.P. (2019): for s. 3(2)(v), the offence must be committed on the ground that the victim is an SC/ST.
  • Ramesh Chandra Vaishya v. State of U.P. (2023): abuse inside a building not in public view does not meet the "public view" requirement.

Assessment

  • Achievements: recognition of atrocities as a distinct category, victim rights, special courts.
  • Gaps: low conviction rates, delays in investigation and trial, under-reporting, inadequate implementation of relief.
  • Way forward: time-bound investigation, more exclusive special courts, witness protection, sensitisation of police and courts.

Revision points

  • Art. 17 → PCR Act 1955 → SC/ST (PoA) Act 1989 (amended 2015, 2018).
  • Mahajan (2018) → s. 18A → review (2019) → Prathvi Raj Chauhan (2020).
  • Hitesh Verma (2020): "public view" and "because of caste".
Syllabus unit 11 · Asked 8 times · 2015–2026

Answer writing: Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 and subsequent legislative developments

Decode the demand. Questions trace the evolution of anti-untouchability law. Present it as a timeline from Art. 17 to the 2015 and 2018 amendments.

Open with

Art. 17 is one of the few constitutional provisions that abolishes a practice outright and makes it an offence.

Answer frame

  1. Art. 17 and its horizontal reach
  2. Untouchability (Offences) Act 1955 → PCR Act 1976
  3. SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989 and amendments
  4. Judicial pronouncements
  5. Gaps and way forward

Concept in brief

Art. 17 abolishes untouchability and makes its practice an offence. Parliament enacted the Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955, renamed the Protection of Civil Rights Act in 1976 with stricter penalties, then the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 (amended 2015 and 2018) and the 2013 Act banning manual scavenging. Art. 17 binds private persons as well as the State.

Key provisions

  • Constitution Arts. 15(2), 17, 23, 46
  • PCR Act, 1955 (ss. 3–7A, 15A)
  • SC/ST (PoA) Act, 1989, s. 3, 18, 18A
  • Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers Act, 2013

Leading cases (cite the ratio, not the facts)

  • People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982): Art. 17 enforceable against private individuals.
  • State of Karnataka v. Appa Balu Ingale (1993): Courts must enforce the PCR Act strictly.
  • Subhash Kashinath Mahajan v. State of Maharashtra (2018): Safeguards against arrest (later recalled).
  • Union of India v. State of Maharashtra (2019): Mahajan directions recalled.
  • Prathvi Raj Chauhan v. Union of India (2020): S. 18A (no anticipatory bail) upheld.
  • Safai Karamchari Andolan v. Union of India (2014): Compensation for sewer deaths; end manual scavenging.

Interdisciplinary edge

  • Ambedkar's role and the Constituent Assembly debates
  • NCRB data on crimes against SCs and STs
  • Sociology: caste as graded inequality
  • Manual scavenging law, 2013

Draw this

Art. 17 (1950) → UOA 1955 → PCR 1976 → SC/ST Act 1989 → 2015 amendment → s. 18A (2018)

Critical lens

Low conviction rates and social pressure on victims limit enforcement.

Close with

Untouchability ends not only with penal law but with social and economic inclusion.
